# Research brief: indoor bouldering technique handbook

## The book

A short textbook (roughly 80–140 A4 pages) that teaches indoor bouldering
*technique* and *route reading*. It is not a training or exercise book. It
will contain:

1. A catalogue of named techniques, grouped into categories, each ranked by
   the V-grade where it typically starts to matter, each with: how to do it,
   how to recognise WHEN to use it (route-reading cues on the wall), common
   mistakes and a black-and-white illustration.
2. An A–Z index of technique names (with aliases).
3. A guide for every V-grade band: what to focus on, what to look for when
   reading problems at that grade, other relevant info.
4. An appendix of sources backing the claims.

Scope: indoor bouldering only. Mention outdoor, lead or rope climbing only
where it really helps (for example a technique name that comes from rock).

## The reader

- Took up bouldering as an adult and has climbed for about a year, indoor
  bouldering only.
- Climbs all of V3 and most V4 in their gyms; has not yet sent a V5.
- Taller and heavier than average, with no sports background.
- Owns training books and found them to be mostly about exercises. Wants
  concrete, actionable technique instruction. Vague cues such as "trust your
  feet" are useless to them: say what to do with which body part, where, and
  why.

## What to deliver

Write your dossier as Markdown to the file path given in your task. Use this
structure for every technique entry:

```
### <Common name>
- **Also called:** aliases, regional names (UK/US/German if known)
- **Category:** (the category you were given)
- **Becomes important at:** V-grade (e.g. "V3") plus one line of reasoning,
  citing sources where any exist. This is the grade where a climber
  typically starts *needing* it indoors, not where it first appears.
- **What it is:** 1–2 sentences.
- **How to do it:** numbered, concrete steps with body cues (which hip,
  which knee, where the weight goes, what the arms do).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** concrete visual cues on the wall
  (hold orientation, hold position relative to body, wall angle, volume
  shapes) and *failure symptoms* that mean you needed it (e.g. "you swing
  off like a door when you let go with your left hand").
- **Common mistakes:** bullets.
- **Tall climber note:** only if there is something specific to say.
- **Illustration brief:** what one clear black-and-white diagram should
  show: the view (climber from behind facing the wall / side profile /
  close-up of hand or foot), where each hand and foot is, which holds and
  their orientation, arrows for motion or force, centre-of-gravity marker,
  before/after ghost positions if helpful. Be specific enough that someone
  could pose a stick-figure mannequin from it.
- **Related:** other technique names.
- **Watch:** 0–3 videos that demonstrate it, ONLY ones you actually found
  in search results, with channel, title and URL.
- **Sources:** source IDs from your source list, e.g. [B3, B7].
```

Non-technique material (concepts, grade guides, considerations) can use
free-form sections, but keep claims tied to source IDs.

End the dossier with a **Source list**: one line per source with its ID
(your letter prefix + number, e.g. B1, B2 ...), author or organisation,
title, publication or channel, year, type (article / video / study / book /
coaching syllabus / forum), URL, and a few words on which claims it
supports.

## Research rules

- Use WebSearch and WebFetch (or the web-fetch agent) to research. Prefer
  established coaches, coaching organisations and syllabi, peer-reviewed
  studies, reputable climbing media (Climbing.com, UKClimbing, Rock and Run,
  Lattice Training, Hooper's Beta, Power Company Climbing, TrainingBeta,
  99Boulders, Gripped, the BMC, Mountain Training, the DAV, USA Climbing,
  IFSC), and well-known technique books (*Rock Climbing Technique* by John
  Kettle, *The Self-Coached Climber* by Hague and Hunter, *Bouldering* by
  Peter Beal, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes* by Dave MacLeod,
  *Training for Climbing* by Eric Hörst, *The Climbing Bible*).
- Never invent a URL, title, author or quote. Every URL in your source list
  must come from a search result or a page you fetched. Cite a book only
  for things you could verify (publisher page, review, excerpt), and say so.
- You cannot watch videos. Cite a video only for what its page text,
  description or reliable write-ups say, or list it under **Watch** only.
- Where sources disagree (for example on the grade a technique matters, or
  on injury risk), say so and give both sides.
- Grade placement is partly judgement. Ground it where you can: coaching
  syllabi that tie skills to levels (e.g. the UK NICAS/NIBAS award schemes),
  coaches' "skills you need for V5" articles, grade-progression guides.
  Mark judgement calls as such.
- Mark anything you are unsure of with **(unverified)**.
- Write in plain British-or-American English, concrete and compact. No
  filler, no motivational fluff.

When finished, reply with a short summary (under 200 words): the file path,
how many entries, the main source types, and any gaps or doubts. Do not
paste the dossier into your reply.
