## Part 1. Concepts

### 1.1 Every hold has a direction of pull

A hold works well only in one direction, or within a narrow range of
directions. German school-climbing material built on the DAV instructor
handbook calls this the hold's *Belastungsrichtung* (load direction). In its
teaching game, students mark the best load direction of every hold on an easy
boulder with a 20–30 cm strip of tape. They then climb the problem so that the
forearm always lies along the tape. A spotter corrects the climber's body
position whenever the forearm drifts off that line [C42]. The same material
names two failure symptoms that come from loading a hold in the wrong
direction [C42]:

- **Sudden slipping off** a hold that felt fine a moment ago. The pull
  direction was wrong, and too little of the hand was locked onto the shape.
  The fix is to take the hold with as much hand as possible and change the
  pull direction until you can "lie into" the hold.
- **Pumped forearms ("thick arms")** on holds that should be easy. With the
  load direction wrong, you must squeeze harder than necessary. The fix is to
  move the body so the hold is loaded in its own direction, and to grip only as
  hard as needed. Climbing on straight arms is suggested as an indicator that
  the load direction is right.

**How to read a hold's orientation.** Define the way a hold *faces* as the
direction the gripping surface points: the surface your finger pads or palm
rest on. On a jug, the lip faces up. On an undercling, it faces down. On a
sidepull, it faces left or right. You load the hold *into* that surface, which
is opposite to the way it faces. A jug facing up is pulled down; a
right-facing sidepull is pulled to the left [C34, C36].

**Where the body must be.** The rule below is derived from the sources'
descriptions of each grip and from simple statics. It is a judgement, but it
is consistent with every source used here:

| Hold faces... | You load it... | Your centre of gravity goes... | Example | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Up (jug, edge) | Down | Below it | Hanging from a jug | [C34, C41] |
| Up and out (sloper) | Down and in | Low and below it, hips under it | "Hang or sink below the hold" | [C2, C3, C11] |
| Sideways, away from you (sidepull) | Toward your midline | To the opposite side; you lean away | Left-hand hold facing left, body to the right | [C34, C36, C40] |
| Sideways, toward you (gaston) | Away from your midline (thumb down, elbow out) | On the side the hold faces; you press outward | Right-hand hold in front of your face, facing left | [C34, C40, C41] |
| Down (undercling) | Up and out | Above it; hold at waist height | Feet high, standing up into it | [C4, C34, C52] |
| Up, but you push (palm, mantle) | Down by pressing | Over it, eventually above your hands | Pressing out a top-out | [C5, C6, C39] |

In short: on a hold you **pull**, the body goes to the side opposite the way
the hold faces. On a hold you **push** (gaston, palm, mantle), the body sits on
the side the hold faces and presses away from it.

> **Check for the editor.** The task brief's example says "a sidepull facing
> left wants the body to the left of it". With "facing" defined as above (and
> as 99Boulders uses it, "a vertical hold that faces away from the body"
> [C34]), a left-facing sidepull wants the body to the **right**, with the
> climber leaning away from the hold [C34, C36]. If the body is to the left of
> a left-facing hold, the hold is being used as a **gaston**. The book should
> define "facing" once and use it consistently.

**Opposition.** Any hold loaded sideways pulls or pushes the body sideways.
Something else, usually a foot, has to push the other way. Sidepulls need the
feet pushing against the pull [C36], underclings need the feet pressed into the
wall [C36, C4], and gastons need the core and legs working against the push
[C40].

**The direction changes as you move.** A hold's useful direction is fixed, but
your body moves past it. A sloper that holds while your hips are below it
fails as your hips rise, because the pull rotates toward parallel with the
hold's surface. That is the same as climbing on a steeper slope angle
(inference from [C29] and [C2]). An undercling gets *better* as you rise above
it [C34, C4]. When reading a problem, ask where your hips will be at the moment
you need each hold, not where they are when you first touch it.

### 1.2 Positive and negative holds

- A **positive** (incut) hold has a gripping surface that faces up or tilts
  back into the wall. Pulling down presses the fingers into it, so the hand
  locks on by shape. German teaching material calls this *Formschluss*
  (form-locking) [C42].
- A **flat** hold has a horizontal gripping surface.
- A **negative** or sloping hold tilts down and out. Part of your pull runs
  parallel to the surface, so the hold works by friction (*Kraftschluss*,
  force-locking) [C42]. 99Boulders calls a small hold of this kind a
  "slimper": it fills the space of a crimp but isn't positive [C34].

The evidence: in a lab study of eight experienced climbers (mean French 7c
redpoint), maximal vertical finger force fell as hold depth decreased and as
slope angle increased, and the two effects interacted. On 3–4 cm holds, slope
made no significant difference from 0° to 30° and only hurt at 40°. On 1–2 cm
holds, force dropped significantly beyond 20°. Climbers chose a half crimp on
the 1–2 cm holds and an open hand on the 3–4 cm holds [C29]. Practical
reading: **a small hold that slopes is much worse than either a small flat
hold or a big sloping one.**

The same physical hold can be positive or negative depending on how it is
bolted on. Rotating a dual-texture hold or volume changes the move [C49, C50].

### 1.3 Hold type, orientation and grip are three separate choices

Treat them as three questions:

1. **Hold type:** what shape is it? Jug, edge, sloper, pinch, pocket, volume.
2. **Orientation:** which way does it face? Down-pull, sidepull, gaston,
   undercling.
3. **Hand posture:** open hand or drag, half crimp, full crimp, pinch, wrap,
   palm.

A pinch can be a sidepull. An edge can be an undercling. Motion-capture data
shows that even the hand postures blur together: in 11 advanced climbers,
"crimp" averaged about 87° of middle-joint (PIP) flexion, half crimp about 70°
and open hand about 39°, but individual ranges overlapped a great deal [C28].

### 1.4 Contact strength and grip endurance (brief)

**(unverified: standard coaching usage; no dedicated source fetched.)**

- **Contact strength** is the ability to generate grip force quickly at the
  instant you catch a hold, as on a slap to a sloper or a dynamic catch on a
  crimp. Symptom of weakness: "I touch the hold but can't latch it." Hooper's
  Beta uses rate-of-force-development drills such as "rapid rows" late in
  pulley rehabilitation, which reflects the same concept [C14].
- **Grip endurance** is the ability to keep gripping over many moves.
  Symptom: you fall on the last moves with pumped forearms.

Technique reduces both demands. Loading holds in their proper direction cuts
the grip force you need [C42]. Gripping as lightly as possible saves energy
[C35, C38]. A German teaching drill trains "soft gripping": load a hold, then
reduce force until you are barely holding on [C42].

## Part 2. Technique entries

### Jug
- **Also called:** bucket, handle (US/UK). German: *Henkel* **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** VB–V0 as a hold; **V2–V3** as a *technique*,
  meaning hanging on jugs with straight arms and using them as rests. Alpinetrek
  advises using jugs as rest spots as difficulty increases and warns against
  pulling on them with brute force [C37]. The grade is a judgement.
- **What it is:** a large, deep, positive hold you can wrap your whole hand
  around. "Jug" is subjective: one climber's jug is another's struggle [C34].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Look for the deepest, most incut part and put your fingers in up to the
     second joint. On a big incut jug, lock the thumb over the edge or around
     the front of the hold, which reduces load on the fingers [C1].
  2. Grip only as hard as needed. Aim for "the lightest possible grip to make
     each move" [C35, C42].
  3. Hang on a straight arm so the skeleton carries the load instead of the
     biceps [C41].
  4. Put your hips on the side opposite the way the jug faces (below it if it
     faces up). If it faces sideways, treat it as a sidepull or gaston (see
     Concepts 1.1).
  5. To rest, hang the free arm below your waist and shake it, then swap hands
     **(unverified: common practice)**.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Big, deep, chalked holds, often at the start, the finish and between
    crux sections. On steep problems, spot them as rest points before you
    start.
  - A jug that faces sideways or down is no longer a down-pull hold. Read its
    face direction before you grab it.
  - *Failure symptoms:* forearms pump on "easy" jugs, meaning you are
    over-gripping or climbing on bent arms [C42]. You swing out sideways
    ("barn-door") when you let go with the other hand, meaning the jug faces
    sideways and your body is in line with it rather than on its far side.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling up with bent arms and hauling (jug-hauling) instead of pushing
    with the legs [C37, C41].
  - Over-gripping big holds and arriving pumped at the crux [C35, C42].
  - Leaving the thumb unused on incut jugs [C1].
  - Ignoring the jug's direction and getting pulled off sideways.
- **Tall climber note:** long arms let you hang lower beneath a jug on
  straight arms and skip intermediate holds. On steep walls this also drops
  your hips far below the hold, so you may need higher feet to keep weight on
  them (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** view from behind, climber on a slightly overhanging
  wall. Right hand on a jug at head height, arm fully straight. Left arm
  hanging down beside the hip, shaking out (motion lines on the hand). Both
  feet on footholds at knee height, hips directly below the jug. Centre-of-
  gravity marker on the vertical line under the jug. Inset close-up: fingers
  curled into the incut to the second joint, thumb wrapped over the front of
  the hold.
- **Related:** Sidepull, Undercling, Wrap grip, Re-gripping and adjusting.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C1, C34, C35, C37, C41, C42]

### Open hand and drag
- **Also called:** open grip, drag, three-finger drag, open crimp (some
  authors), chisel (index straight, other fingers bent [C1]), bird-beak
  (fingers at about 160°, a half-crimp/open hybrid [C1]), slope grip (research
  term [C14, C30]). German: *offene Hand*, *hängende Finger* [C42, C43].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V2–V3**. Lattice testing of 3,484 climbers found
  that at V1–V4, open-hand testers recorded higher loads than half-crimp
  testers (+22.5 kg vs +14.5 kg), so open hand is the natural strong grip of
  climbers at this level. The two grips converged at V12+ [C21]. In the lab,
  climbers picked open hand on holds 3–4 cm deep [C29]. The grade is a
  judgement.
- **What it is:** the finger pads hang over the edge with little bend at the
  middle joint. The grip relies on skin friction and tendon tension rather than
  on bending the fingers sharply [C1, C10].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Lay the finger pads over the edge so the end joint (DIP) hooks over the
     lip. Keep the middle joint (PIP) only slightly bent; motion capture put
     open hand at about 39° PIP on average [C28].
  2. **Four-finger open:** index and little finger straight, middle and ring
     slightly bent [C10]. **Three-finger drag:** index, middle and ring on the
     hold, little finger off [C1, C10].
  3. Keep the wrist slightly extended. The wrist extensors improve the finger
     flexors' mechanical advantage [C9].
  4. Get your body below the hold so the pull runs down through the finger pads
     and into the wall. Hang on a straight arm.
  5. Maximise skin contact: on a rounded edge, more hand on the hold means more
     friction [C35].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Rounded edges and rails where bending the fingers hooks nothing.
  - Deeper holds (3 cm or more) [C29].
  - Long, easier sections where you need to save energy [C1].
  - The first grip to return to after a finger injury [C14, C15]. Fischer et
    al. suggest the three-finger drag for rehab because it should load the
    pulleys less **(their inference, not measured)** [C28].
  - *Failure symptoms:* fingers peel off as your hips rise or as you lean out,
    meaning you have moved above the line of pull. The open hand slides on a
    1–2 cm edge: switch to a half crimp [C29]. It "opens" on a dynamic catch:
    the open grip gives less traction on hard moves [C1].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Forcing the open hand onto small edges where a half crimp would hold [C29].
  - Starting in a crimp and letting the hand be forced open under maximal load.
    That is a suspected pulley-injury mechanism, different from choosing an
    open hand from the start [C14, C15, C20].
  - Leaving the little finger idle when it could contribute. In one preprint,
    climbers at 8a and above put relatively more force through the little
    finger in a four-finger half crimp [C30].
- **Tall climber note:** for a heavier climber, the open hand is the
  lower-pulley-load default wherever it holds (see the finger-injury evidence
  section) [C14, C26].
- **Illustration brief:** close-up side profile of a right hand on a 25 mm
  rounded edge. Three-finger drag: index, middle and ring fingers over the lip,
  little finger lifted off. Middle joint bent about 30–40°, end joint flexed
  over the lip, wrist straight to slightly extended. Arrow from the finger pads
  pointing down and slightly into the wall. Inset: the four-finger open
  version, index and little finger straight. Small ghost figure behind,
  showing the body hanging below the hold.
- **Related:** Half crimp, Sloper, Pocket, Re-gripping and adjusting.
- **Watch:** Lattice Training coaches Jen and Billy, "Half crimp, drag, sloper
  and more: Lattice coaches break down every grip type" (Instagram video
  embedded on Lacrux), https://www.instagram.com/p/DX18JFekZWr/ (via
  https://www.lacrux.com/en/videos/how-to-cheat-sloper-lattice-training/).
- **Sources:** [C1, C9, C10, C14, C15, C20, C21, C22, C28, C29, C30, C35, C42, C43]

### Half crimp
- **Also called:** half-crimp, open crimp (some authors). German: *halb
  aufgestellt* [C43]. Neil Gresham's "chisel" is a half crimp with the index
  straight [C1].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4**. Holds get "smaller and crimpier" at V5
  [C48], and climbers in the lab chose the half crimp on 1–2 cm edges [C29].
  Lattice uses it as the base training grip because it transfers to other grips
  [C21]. The grade is a judgement.
- **What it is:** index, middle and ring fingers bent to about 90° at the
  middle joint, end joint not bent backwards, little finger straight or on the
  hold, thumb free [C1, C9, C10].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Place the fingertips on the edge with the middle joint at roughly 80–90°.
     Schweizer recommends about 85° PIP flexion with the end joint slightly
     flexed (5–15°), and avoiding the full crimp where possible [C25].
  2. Keep the end joint neutral or slightly flexed. If it bends backwards,
     you are in a full crimp [C9].
  3. Put the little finger on if it reaches. It is shorter, so it usually stays
     straight [C1].
  4. Rest the thumb beside the index finger, or press it on any feature on the
     side of the hold [C1].
  5. Pull down and slightly in, elbow below the hand, hips under or beside the
     hold according to its direction.
  6. If you feel the hand being forced open under heavy load, re-grip or let
     go. Don't keep fighting it: hands opening under maximal load is believed
     to be a primary pulley-injury mechanism [C15, C20].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Edges 1–2 cm deep, especially flat or incut ones [C29].
  - Small edges on volumes.
  - The default on any edge too small for an open hand.
  - *Failure symptoms:* the fingertips roll off the front of the edge, meaning
    the body is too high above the hold or the edge slopes. The hand peels open
    on each attempt: try a thumb catch or full crimp for that move, or change
    body position. Pain at the base of the finger (A2 area) means stop.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Collapsing into a full crimp by default (beginners overuse the full crimp
    because it feels mechanically comfortable [C9]).
  - Leaving the thumb idle when a thumb catch exists [C1].
  - Making many maximal attempts in a row on the same crimp move [C15].
  - **Disagreement:** Gresham calls the half crimp one of the safest grips
    because "it tends to open out before placing harmful load" on the tendons
    [C1]. Hooper's Beta argues the opposite: hands opening under heavy load is
    linked to pulley injuries [C15, C20]. Gripped also questions whether the
    half crimp is as safe as many believe [C10].
- **Tall climber note:** pulley forces scale with fingertip force, which
  scales with the share of body weight on the hand [C26]. A heavier climber
  should limit maximal crimp attempts per session (judgement based on [C15,
  C26]).
- **Illustration brief:** close-up side profile, right hand on a 15 mm flat
  edge. Index, middle and ring fingers with the middle joint at 90° (label the
  angle), end joint neutral (label "not bent back"), little finger straight on
  the edge, thumb resting against the side of the index finger. Force arrow
  pointing down. Next to it, a small comparison silhouette of a full crimp
  with the end joint visibly hyperextended and the thumb over the index nail.
- **Related:** Open hand and drag, Full crimp, Thumb wrap and thumb lock.
- **Watch:** Lattice Training, "Half crimp, drag, sloper and more" (Instagram),
  https://www.instagram.com/p/DX18JFekZWr/.
- **Sources:** [C1, C9, C10, C15, C20, C21, C25, C26, C29, C30, C43, C48]

### Full crimp
- **Also called:** closed crimp [C9], crimping. German: *voll aufgestellt*
  [C43]. Two forms: little finger extended, or little finger also crimped [C10].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V5+** as a *necessity* on small edges. Gresham
  calls it "the old-timers' go-to grip, now less common" [C1]. At V3–V4 the
  main skill is *not* using it by default, since beginners overuse it [C9].
  Ned Feehally (Beastmaking) argues it must be trained because hard crimps
  depend on it [C8]. The grade is a judgement.
- **What it is:** the middle joint bent beyond 90°, the end joint bent
  backwards (hyperextended), usually with the thumb locked over the index
  fingernail [C1, C9, C25].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put the fingertips on the edge and lift the knuckles until the middle
     joint is bent past 90° and the end joint bends back.
  2. Lock the thumb over the index finger's nail [C1, C9]. In one variant the
     thumb meets the index at its most extended point [C10].
  3. Bring the palm close to the wall. This lets you get further behind the
     hold and suits in-cut edges [C10].
  4. Use it for the crux move only, then switch back to a half crimp or open
     hand on the next hold.
  5. Keep the feet solid. Foot slips create sudden shock loads on the
     fingers [C14, C15, C26].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Very small edges, especially in-cut ones [C10].
  - Dynamic catches on small edges.
  - Cases where the half crimp keeps peeling open.
  - Feehally argues it is mechanically stronger on small holds [C8].
  - *Failure symptoms showing you need it:* the half crimp opens every time on
    a micro-edge, even with good body position.
  - *Failure symptoms showing you overuse it:* aching end joints, pain at the
    base of a finger after crimpy sessions.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Full crimping on holds big enough for a half crimp or open hand [C9].
  - Full crimping in pockets (judgement).
  - Many high-intensity crimps in a row [C15].
  - Using it at all in adolescence. Schweizer warns full crimp loading can
    cause growth-plate fractures in young climbers [C25]. This is not
    relevant to the reader but relevant to the book.
- **Tall climber note:** Gresham notes that finger length affects how stable
  the full crimp feels [C1]. On body weight, see the evidence section below.
- **Illustration brief:** close-up side profile of a right hand on a 6 mm
  in-cut edge. Fingers in full crimp, middle joint about 100°, end joint
  hyperextended (label), thumb pressed over the index nail (label "thumb
  lock"), palm close to the wall. Next to it, a cut-away of one finger
  showing the flexor tendon bowstringing away from the bone at the A2 pulley,
  with a bold arrow at A2 (label "highest pulley load").
- **Related:** Half crimp, Thumb wrap and thumb lock, Matching on small holds.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, Ep. 167 "Ultimate A2 Pulley Injury Recovery Guide
  (PT's Full Protocol)", companion page:
  https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/ultimate-a2-pulley-injury-recovery-guide
  (YouTube episode; video link not located).
- **Sources:** [C1, C8, C9, C10, C14, C15, C25, C26, C43]

#### Finger-injury evidence for crimp grips (free-form)

- **Pulley injuries are the commonest climbing-specific injury, and the crimp
  is the mechanism.** A 2021 review (Miro, vanSonnenberg, Sabb, Schöffl)
  calls flexor pulley injuries the most common overuse injury in climbers and
  says "the 'crimp grip' ... is the mechanism for these injuries". A2, A3 and
  A4 are most vulnerable, especially under eccentric load [C27]. A2 is
  involved 1.5–2 times more often than A4 [C25]. A 2015 case series of 911
  climbing injuries found 52% were finger injuries, pulleys the most frequent
  [C58, snippet only].
- **Load numbers (biomechanical, not injury-rate data):**
  - Vigouroux et al. (2006): with about 96 N at the fingertip, the A2 pulley
    took about 255 N in the crimp versus about 8 N in the open "slope" grip,
    roughly a 97% reduction [C14, citing C23].
  - A figure of "36 times lower A2 force in the slope grip" circulates, with
    the attribution unclear (Vigouroux 2006 or Schweizer 2001). Treat it as
    the same order of magnitude as the 255 vs 8 N figure.
  - Schweizer (2012): pulley loads in the crimp are 3–4 times the fingertip
    force and rise sharply once PIP flexion exceeds about 80° [C25].
  - Schöffl I. et al. (2009), as cited by Bosco et al.: about 287 N on A2 and
    226 N on A4 for a 70 kg climber in the crimp. A sudden one-finger shock
    load (for example a missed foot) can push A2 to about 450 N, against a
    cited failure force of about 400 N [C26, citing C53 and others].
  - In a biomechanical model, middle and ring fingers carried 60% of total
    fingertip force, with their A2 pulleys at 64% (ring) and 90% (middle) of
    breaking point under normal conditions [C26, citing Vigouroux 2008].
- **The thumb lock does not add risk to the other fingers (one study).**
  Quaine et al. (2011): putting the thumb on the index increases the resultant
  force on the hold and reorganises force sharing between fingers, while "the
  pathology risks of the middle, ring and little fingers were not modified"
  [C24]. A figure of "about 17% more force" appears on a physio exercise site
  citing this paper **(unverified; not in the abstract)** [C59].
- **What the evidence does not show.** I found no prospective study that
  counts injuries per hour of full crimping versus half crimping. The case
  against the full crimp rests on load modelling and clinical case series
  (search-based observation; could be wrong).
- **Disagreement among coaches:**
  - *Against default full crimping:* the physio-sourced Climbing.com piece says
    the full crimp places "extreme stress on the finger's tendons and pulleys"
    and should be used only when needed [C9]. Schweizer says to avoid it where
    possible [C25]. *The Climbing Bible* (via Bergwelten) calls it the most
    aggressive grip, with a higher risk to pulleys, cartilage, bones and
    tendons [C43].
  - *For training it carefully:* Feehally says training it on a fingerboard is
    "relatively safe", "although it is not 100 per cent risk free", and that
    serious climbers need it [C8]. Hooper's Beta says crimping isn't
    inherently dangerous and pulleys can be strengthened [C14], and that
    reducing pulley load in training may leave tissues less resilient [C20].
- **Practical consensus for the book:** half crimp or open hand by default;
  full crimp only for the move that needs it. Avoid the hand being forced
  open under maximal load. Warm up properly: bowstringing increases by up to
  30% over the first 100–120 moves, so Schweizer recommends 8–12 boulder
  problems as a warm-up [C25]. Warm-up increases physiological bowstringing
  and is thought to protect the pulleys [C27].

### Thumb wrap and thumb lock (on edges)
- **Also called:** thumb lock (thumb over the index nail) [C1, C24]; thumb
  wrap (thumb wrapped round the front or side of the hold) **(unverified as a
  distinct named term)**; inverse crimp (thumb pad on a small feature, fingers
  crimped over the thumbnail) [C7].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V4**. The thumb is easy to forget, and Gresham
  notes climbers often miss the thumb catch that goes with an edge [C1]. The
  grade is a judgement.
- **What it is:** adding the thumb to a finger grip. Either it locks the
  fingers (over the index) or it grips a separate feature of the same hold
  (round the front, under or beside the edge).
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Thumb lock:** in a crimp, press the thumb pad over the index fingernail
     (or index and middle). This adds force in the same direction as the
     fingers [C24].
  2. **Thumb wrap on an incut jug or rail:** wrap the thumb over the top or
     round the front of the hold so it pulls against the fingers. On large
     in-cut holds this reduces finger load [C1].
  3. **Thumb catch on an edge:** feel the face below or beside the edge for a
     bump, dimple or the hold's own side, and press the thumb into it [C7].
  4. **Inverse crimp:** on a single small knob, place the thumb pad on it and
     crimp the fingers over the thumbnail [C7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Look at the *underside and sides* of every edge for a thumb feature.
    The thumb can turn a too-small or sloping edge into a usable hold [C7].
  - Wide rails and jugs with a front lip suit a thumb wrap.
  - *Failure symptom:* the fingers slide forward off a hold even though your
    body is under it. Add the thumb to stop the hand rotating out.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Not looking for a thumb catch at all [C1].
  - Locking the thumb on every hold, so a full crimp becomes the default
    (see Full crimp).
- **Illustration brief:** two close-ups, side profile.
  (a) Thumb lock: full crimp on a small edge with the thumb pad pressed on the

  direction (down).
  (b) Thumb wrap on a rail: fingers over the top lip, thumb curled round the

- **Related:** Full crimp, Pinch, Thumb catch, thumb press and thumb tricks.

### Pinch (narrow, wide, volume pinch)
- **Also called:** pinch grip. German: *Zange*, *Zangengriff* [C42, C43].
  Narrow pinch: "crab's claw" [C35].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** wide pinches **V2–V3**; narrow pinches and volume
  pinches **V4–V5** (judgement). *The Climbing Bible* (via Bergwelten) notes
  the pinch helps keep body tension upright but costs a lot of strength [C43].
- **What it is:** gripping a hold by squeezing between thumb and fingers on
  two opposing faces [C34, C43]. Pinches are usually vertical, because a
  horizontal one would be used as a crimp or sloper [C34].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Find the two opposing faces. Thumb on one, fingers on the other, so the
     squeeze line is straight across the hold.
  2. **Narrow pinch:** squeeze between the thumb pad and the side of the index
     finger [C35]. Fingers usually in a half crimp; narrow pinches are crimpier
     [C1, C38].
  3. **Wide pinch:** fingers open and flat, thumb pad flat on the opposite
     face. Wide pinches are more open-handed [C38]. Hand size affects how hard
     a wide pinch is to hold [C37].
  4. **Volume pinch:** thumb on one face of a volume, fingers on the other,
     squeezing the edge between them. Where an edge meets the lip, a "Vulcan"
     with two fingers either side can work [C11].
  5. On vertical pinches, some climbers twist the thumb so it faces up and use
     its pad and width [C38].
  6. Place the thumb where chalk marks show other climbers put theirs [C38].
  7. Body: depending on the pinch's orientation, layback off it, twist, or
     compress between two pinches. A roughly 90° wrist angle with the body
     sagging into a flag can work [C38].
  8. Grip as lightly as you can. Over-gripping makes you stiff [C38].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Vertical ribs, blocks, the edges of volumes and wide flat holds with two
    usable sides.
  - A sidepull with a thumb catch can be turned into a pinch for more inward
    pull [C36].
  - Sometimes a pinch is better held like an edge, depending on body position
    [C37].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the hold "squirts" out sideways, meaning the squeeze
    line is not straight across the hold. The thumb slips, meaning it is on a
    smooth face: find texture or a dimple. You barn-door when using a pinch,
    meaning you are squeezing but your body is on the wrong side for its
    direction (treat it as a sidepull).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Using fingers only and leaving the thumb off.
  - Squeezing harder instead of moving the body to load the pinch along its
    direction [C42].
  - Choosing a narrow pinch line when a wider one exists. A study of 22
    advanced climbers found traction force utilisation 21–62% higher on a
    broad pinch than a narrow one; force dropped 6–19% (broad) and 31–46%
    (narrow) compared with a jug [C31].
- **Tall climber note:** a tall climber probably has large hands, which helps on
  wide pinches [C37] but not on narrow ones (inference).
- **Illustration brief:** three close-ups, view facing the wall.
  (a) Narrow pinch: a 3 cm wide vertical rib, thumb pad on the left face,
  side of the index on the right face, two horizontal arrows pointing inward.
  (b) Wide pinch: a 12 cm wide block, fingers flat on the right face, thumb flat
  on the left face, inward arrows.
  (c) Volume pinch: a triangular volume seen from slightly to the side, thumb on
  the near face, fingers wrapped over the edge onto the far face, inward
  squeeze arrows, a down arrow for the pull.
- **Related:** Sidepull, Wrap grip, Dual-texture holds and macros, Thumb catch.
- **Watch:** Lattice Training, "Half crimp, drag, sloper and more" (Instagram),
  https://www.instagram.com/p/DX18JFekZWr/.
- **Sources:** [C1, C11, C31, C34, C35, C36, C37, C38, C42, C43]

### Sloper
- **Also called:** sloping hold. German: *Aufleger* [C42, C43]. A small sloper
  the size of a crimp is a "slimper" [C34].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4**. In a Mountain Project thread, a climber
  stuck between V3 and V4 named slopers and thin, oddly placed holds as the
  hardest part; replies pointed to body position and footwork rather than
  grip [C47]. Slope angle cuts force most on small holds [C29]. The grade is a
  judgement.
- **What it is:** a rounded or down-sloping hold with no positive edge, held by
  friction and body position rather than finger curl [C34, C37].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Read it from the ground.** Find the best part of the hold, thinking
     about where your body will be [C2]. Chalk marks show the usual spot
     [C11].
  2. **Hand:** on big rounded slopers, spread the fingers and press with palm
     and fingertips. On flatter ledges, keep the fingers together and press
     down from the base of the fingers [C3]. Drag the finger pads and, if
     possible, the palm over the hold [C2]. Crimp any small depression [C2].
     Wrap the hand round the hold rather than gripping straight on, and try
     angling the wrist slightly left or right [C3].
  3. **Thumb:** pinch with the thumb where you can. Try it below and to the
     side of the hold; this matters most on steep problems [C2].
  4. **Wrist:** "Focus on keeping your wrist stiff" [C2].
  5. **Elbow:** vary the elbow's position and feel the difference. A German
     teaching drill has students move the elbow while holding a sloper [C42].
  6. **Hips:** "hang or sink below the hold", weight low, hips in toward the
     wall [C3]. Keep the hips central between the handholds so they don't
     swing on each reach [C2].
  7. **Feet:** don't put both feet high, "as this will force your hips outward
     and cause you to lose traction". Use one high foot and keep the other low
     [C2]. Heel and toe hooks help keep you in [C2, C3].
  8. **Arms:** straight, heavy body [C11].
  9. **Movement:** slow and controlled; don't overpower or underpower the reach
     [C2, C11]. Commit; hesitation "opens the door to failure" (Meagan Martin,
     in [C3]).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Rounded holds and ledges with no lip; flat-topped volumes.
  - The more the hold slopes, the more your hips must be under it at the
    moment you load it [C2, C3, C29].
  - If the sloper is angled sideways, lean away from it, in the opposite
    direction [C3].
  - *Failure symptoms:*
    - The hand slowly slides off as your hips rise or as you bring both feet
      up, because hips moving out reduces traction [C2, C3].
    - The hips swing out on a reach and "unwind" you off the hold [C2].
    - The hand skates on contact, meaning you overpowered the move [C2].
    - Your core sags, so you are holding on with fingers alone [C3].
    - It's warm and humid, and slopers become hard or impossible [C3].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Reaching straight for the hold without first finding its best part [C2].
  - Twisting the body into the hold; Gresham advises keeping the hips parallel
    [C2].
  - Both feet high [C2].
  - Squeezing with the forearm instead of using shoulders, back and core [C3].
  - Slapping the same sloper over and over, which strains the wrist [C2].
  - **Disagreement:** Climbing.com advises hips "sucked into the wall" [C3].
    Hooper's Beta argues that pinning the body to the wall can block rotation,
    so you swing out when a hand lets go. Climbers usually find the side-to-side
    balance point but stay too close in the in-and-out direction [C19].
    Reconcile in the book: hips *low* and under the hold, but not necessarily
    *pressed* to the wall.
- **Tall climber note:** long arms let you hang the hips lower beneath a sloper
  without changing footholds, which helps. Long legs mean a high foot pushes
  the knee and hips further out from the wall, so a tall climber should choose
  lower feet on sloper sequences (inference from [C2]). Body weight scales all
  hand forces equally. What decides whether a sloper holds is the *direction*
  of pull, so the fix is position, not more squeeze (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** side profile on a 20° overhang. Right hand on a large
  dome sloper at head height, fingers spread and palm touching, wrist straight
  and stiff. Arm straight. Hips low and directly below the hold, close to but
  not touching the wall. One foot high on a foothold at hip height, the other
  low at knee height. Centre-of-gravity marker under the sloper. Arrow from the
  hand showing pull down and slightly into the wall. Ghost figure (dashed) with
  both feet high and hips pushed out, the arm angled outward, and a red slip
  arrow on the hand.
- **Related:** Open hand and drag, Palming and pressing, Dual-texture holds and
  macros, Wrap grip.
- **Watch:** Lattice Training, "Half crimp, drag, sloper and more" (Instagram),
  https://www.instagram.com/p/DX18JFekZWr/. Hooper's Beta, Ep. 164 "When
  'Tight to the Wall' Actually Does the Opposite", companion page:
  https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/body-tension-coaching-with-dan.
- **Sources:** [C2, C3, C11, C19, C29, C34, C37, C42, C43, C47]

### Pocket (mono, two- and three-finger)
- **Also called:** hole, mono (one finger) [C34]. Two-finger combinations:
  front two (index–middle), middle two (middle–ring), back two (ring–little)
  [C13, C17]. German: *Loch*, *Lochgriff*, *Dreifingerloch* [C42, C43].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V5** indoors (judgement). Pockets are
  commoner on outdoor limestone than in gyms **(unverified)**. Lumbrical tears
  are described as a rapidly increasing climbing injury [C33].
- **What it is:** a hole that takes one to four fingers [C34].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Count the fingers that fit, and feel how deep the pocket is (to the first
     joint or the second).
  2. **Three fingers:** bunch three fingers in the hole [C42]; index–middle–ring
     is the usual choice **(unverified)**.
  3. **Two fingers:** middle–ring or index–middle [C42]. VDiff says middle–ring
     balances the load better [C35]. Alpinetrek says choose by comfort, since
     muscle use is similar [C37].
  4. **Mono:** use the middle finger [C35].
  5. **The fingers outside the pocket:** the strong but risky way curls them
     into the palm, recruiting all four tendons. The safer way keeps their
     first segments *parallel* with the pocket fingers, bent only enough to
     clear the wall. Use the safer way by default [C12, C13, C16, C17].
  6. Open hand or crimp inside the pocket depending on depth [C35]. Don't
     crimp hard over a sharp pocket rim; the tendons grind over it [C35].
  7. Weight the pocket smoothly. Watch your feet: when tired, a foot slip can
     shock-load the lumbricals [C16].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Holes in holds and volumes.
  - "Two-hole" holds.
  - *Failure symptoms:* sharp pain in the palm while pulling a two-finger
    pocket is the lumbrical warning sign; stop. Only part of a finger fits:
    use fewer, better-seated fingers.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Middle–ring pocket with the little finger curled hard into the palm. This
    shears the fourth lumbrical, which is pulled in opposite directions by the
    straight ring finger and the curled little finger [C12, C16].
  - Pulling hard pockets your lumbricals are not trained for [C17].
  - Crimping over sharp rims [C35].
- **Evidence:**
  - Schweizer (2012): a one- or two-finger pocket with the adjacent fingers
    fully flexed raises single-finger flexion force by up to 50%. Pocket
    loading strains lumbricals III and IV [C25].
  - A cadaver study reproduced the mechanism. Loading the little finger's deep
    flexor alone tore the fourth lumbrical at about 11 kg (SD 4.9) [C32].
    Injury occurs in asymmetric "one, two or three-finger" pocket positions
    when the load "increases out of control, either accidentally or due to
    fatigue" [C32].
  - In 60 patients (57 climbers) with lumbrical tears, all healed and returned
    to sport [C33].
  - **Disagreement:** VDiff prefers middle–ring for load balance [C35].
    Hooper's Beta and Gripped flag middle–ring with curled outside fingers as
    the main lumbrical-risk position [C12, C16]. Both can be true: middle–ring
    is a strong combination provided the outside fingers stay parallel.
- **Tall climber note:** slim fingers fit pockets better [C37]. Large fingers
  may fit fewer fingers, forcing more two-finger and mono positions with
  higher lumbrical shear (inference).
- **Illustration brief:** close-up from below and to the side of a right hand
  in a two-finger pocket (middle and ring in the hole to the second joint).
  Two versions side by side.
  (a) "Safer": index and little finger with their first segments parallel to
  the pocket fingers, slightly bent, tick mark.
  (b) "Riskier": index and little finger curled tight into the palm, with a
  red shear arrow drawn across the palm between ring and little finger
  (label "4th lumbrical").
  Small inset: mono with the middle finger.
- **Related:** Open hand and drag, Half crimp, Sweet-spot finding.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, Ep. 155 "This Routine Can Save So Many Climbers
  from Injury" (pocket and lumbrical training), companion page:
  https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/pocket-training-lumbricals.
- **Sources:** [C12, C13, C16, C17, C25, C32, C33, C34, C35, C37, C42, C43]

### Sidepull
- **Also called:** layaway (UK) [C41], sidepull, layback (when both hands pull
  and the feet push, as on a flake) [C34, C41]. German: *Seitgriff*
  **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V1–V2**. Directional holds appear early. Movement
  gyms put "directional holds" (sidepulls, underclings, gastons) first in its
  five core techniques [C40]. The grade is a judgement.
- **What it is:** a vertical edge, or any hold, whose gripping surface faces
  sideways *away* from you. You pull it toward your midline and lean away from
  it [C34, C36].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Read which way it faces. A left-hand sidepull out to your left faces left.
  2. Grip the edge with the fingers pointing into it (thumb up) and pull toward
     your body's centre line.
  3. **Lean away:** move your hips and shoulders to the opposite side, so the
     arm points from your shoulder to the hold. 99Boulders describes the body
     as a 7-shape [C34, C36, C40].
  4. **Feet push the other way:** put the feet where they can push against the
     pull, usually under or slightly toward the hold's side [C36].
  5. Turn side-on when reaching: sidepulls often give more reach than
     horizontal holds [C36]. A backstep or drop knee on the hold side is a
     common pairing (see the footwork chapter) [C36].
  6. If there's a thumb catch, turn the sidepull into a pinch for more inward
     pull [C36].
  7. Move the free hand while staying leaned off.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Vertical edges and flakes.
  - Holds whose lip faces away from the line of the problem.
  - Holds out to the side at shoulder height or lower.
  - *Failure symptoms:* you swing open like a door when you let go with the
    other hand. Your body is in line with the hold or on its facing side, or
    no foot is pushing the other way. Or your fingers peel outward because you
    are pulling it as if it were a down-pull hold.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling down on a vertical edge instead of sideways [C36].
  - Standing square to the wall with the hips flat, instead of leaning off and
    turning side-on.
  - Feet on the wrong side, so nothing opposes the pull.
- **Tall climber note:** long arms make leaning off easier and give big reach
  from sidepulls (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** view from behind on a vertical wall. Left hand on a
  vertical edge at shoulder height out to the left, its gripping surface facing
  left (draw the hold so its incut clearly faces left). Left arm nearly straight.
  Hips and torso leaning right, body forming a 7-shape. Right hand reaching up
  and right to a target hold. Left foot on a foothold below the sidepull,
  pushing left (arrow). Arrow on the left hand pointing right (pull toward the
  midline). Centre-of-gravity marker right of the sidepull. Ghost figure: body
  directly under the hold with a curved "barn-door" arrow swinging it open.
- **Related:** Gaston, Pinch, Undercling. Footwork chapter: Backstep, Drop knee,
  Flagging.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C34, C36, C40, C41, C42]

### Gaston
- **Also called:** gaston (after French climber Gaston Rébuffat
  **(unverified)**), reverse sidepull [C57].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4** (judgement). 99Boulders notes it needs a
  lot of shoulder strength [C34], which beginners avoid until problems force
  it.
- **What it is:** a hold whose gripping surface faces toward your midline,
  usually in front of the face or chest. You grip it thumb-down, elbow out, and
  press it away from your body [C34, C41, C57].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put your fingers on the face of the hold that faces your midline, with the
     thumb pointing down and the elbow pointing out sideways [C41, C57].
  2. Pull or press the hold outward, away from your centre line, like prying
     doors apart. The shoulder does most of the work [C34, C40].
  3. Keep the elbow high and out, roughly level with the hand rather than
     hanging below it **(unverified)**.
  4. Keep the hold close to the shoulder. Gastons get weaker the further the
     hand is from the body **(unverified)**.
  5. **Opposition:** the hold pushes your hand toward your midline, so your
     legs and core must push your hips toward the gaston side. A foot pressing
     outward on the opposite side, or a flag, does this (derived; [C40] says to
     engage core and legs).
  6. Two gastons together, or a gaston against a sidepull, pressing apart,
     make a stable "chicken-wing" squeeze **(unverified)**.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Vertical edges or seams in front of your face or chest whose usable side
    faces you.
  - The same hold can be a sidepull for one hand and a gaston for the other
    [C34].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the elbow drops and the hand peels off. The shoulder
    burns quickly because the hold is too far from your body. You can't let go
    with the other hand without the body falling toward the gaston's facing
    side, because no foot is opposing it.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Turning the hand thumb-up and trying to pull it like a sidepull.
  - Letting the elbow sag below the hand.
  - Reaching for a gaston at full arm's length.
- **Illustration brief:** view from behind on a vertical wall. Right hand on a
  vertical edge directly in front of the climber's right shoulder, its gripping
  surface facing left (toward the body). Thumb pointing down, elbow out to the
  right at hand height. Arrow on the hand pointing right (outward). Left hand
  reaching up-left. Left foot on a foothold out left, small arrow showing it
  pressing left; right foot on a low foothold. Centre-of-gravity marker left of
  the gaston. Inset close-up of the hand: thumb-down, fingertips on the
  left-facing surface.
- **Related:** Sidepull, Undercling, Pinch.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C34, C40, C41, C57]

### Undercling
- **Also called:** undercut (UK) [C41, C52], undercling. German: *Untergriff*
  **(unverified)**. Overhead thumb undercling: "thumbercling" [C7].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V2–V3** (judgement). Underclings appear early,
  but using them well depends on getting the feet high, which beginners
  resist [C4].
- **What it is:** a hold whose gripping surface faces down. You grip it palm up
  and pull up and out, with your body above or level with it [C4, C34].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Feet first.** Build the feet up before you reach to the hold:
     "Undercuts always feel poor when they are high above your head, so the
     key is to build your feet up first before reaching to the hold and then
     to reel it in close to your torso as soon as possible" [C52].
  2. **Hand:** palm up, open hand rather than crimping, as much hand behind the
     hold as possible, thumb pointing outward [C4].
  3. **Hold at the waist:** get your midsection level with the hold [C4]. The
     higher your body relative to the hold, the easier it becomes [C4, C34].
  4. **Arms straight:** keep weight on the skeleton, not the biceps [C4, C36].
  5. **Pull out, push with the legs:** lean back off the hold and stand up
     into it, pressing the feet into the wall [C4, C36].
  6. **Hips:** feet high so the hips sit at about 90°. Turn the hip toward the
     wall as you stand up on one foot to reach [C4].
  7. An undercling with a straight arm lets you reach far with the free hand
     [C34].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Holds with their lip facing down: flakes, the underside of volumes,
    downward-facing edges.
  - Usually at chest to waist height, with a target well above.
  - *Failure symptoms:* biceps burning means bent arms [C4]. Feeling off
    balance, as if falling backward, means the hold is too high relative to
    your body [C4, C52]. Feet skating means they aren't fully weighted [C4].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling with the arms instead of driving with the legs [C4].
  - Crimping instead of using an open hand [C4].
  - Feet too low [C4].
  - Standing down on smears instead of pressing into the wall [C4].
- **Injury note:** underclings with the wrist bent toward the little-finger
  side load the hook of the hamate bone via the ring- and little-finger
  tendons, and can cause hamate fractures [C25].
- **Tall climber note:** your waist sits higher above your feet than a shorter
  climber's, so you reach "hold at waist height" with your feet relatively
  lower. That's an advantage. Very high feet will cramp long legs (inference).
- **Illustration brief:** side profile on a vertical wall. Right hand palm-up
  under a downward-facing flake at waist height, arm straight, thumb pointing
  out. Feet high on footholds at knee height of the standing figure, hips at
  about 90°, torso leaning slightly back. Left hand reaching high above. Arrow
  on the right hand pointing up and out; arrows at the feet pointing into the
  wall. Ghost figure: the same hold above the head with feet low, bent arm and
  a backward-tipping arrow.
- **Related:** Gaston, Sidepull, Thumb catch, thumb press and thumb tricks
  (thumberclings).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C4, C7, C25, C34, C36, C41, C52]

### Palming and pressing
- **Also called:** palming, palm press, support (Alpinetrek) [C37], push.
  Related to stemming (bridging) [C41].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V2** on slabs and volumes; **V4** in corners and
  on steep volume problems (judgement).
- **What it is:** pressing an open palm against the wall or a volume face, so
  friction and push replace grip. It is the hand version of smearing [C41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Find a surface you can push *into*: a volume face, the wall beside you, a
     corner's other wall, a dimple.
  2. Open the hand fully and put it flat. Chalk the whole hand first [C11].
  3. Put the pressure through the heel of the hand **(snippet only)** [C56].
  4. Point the fingers so the wrist can stay in line with the push. For
     pushing down, fingers point sideways or toward the other hand, as in a
     mantle [C6]. For pushing into a corner, fingers point up or down
     **(unverified)**.
  5. Elbow behind the hand in the line of push; straighten the arm as you
     move.
  6. **Push away from where you're going:** to move up and left, press down
     and right [C11].
  7. Use the push to take weight off a foot so you can move it [C37, C56].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Big volumes with flat faces [C11].
  - Blank wall beside you.
  - Corners, where you palm both walls and push yourself up **(snippet only)**.
  - Slabs.
  - Problems with few handholds near a good foothold [C37].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the palm skids, meaning you are pushing along the
    surface instead of into it, so press more perpendicular to it. Your foot
    won't come up, meaning no hand is unweighting it: add a palm press.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pressing with the fingertips instead of the whole palm.
  - Pushing in the direction of travel rather than against it [C11].
  - Forgetting to chalk the palm [C5, C11].
- **Illustration brief:** view from behind, climber on a vertical wall with a
  large triangular volume at waist height to the right. Right palm flat on
  the volume's top-left face, fingers pointing right, elbow straight behind the
  hand, arrow from the palm pointing down and right. Left hand reaching up and
  left. Right foot lifting (dashed motion line) to a higher foothold; left
  foot smearing low. Centre-of-gravity marker over the right foot area.
- **Related:** Mantle onto volumes, Thumb catch, thumb press and thumb tricks,
  Dual-texture holds and macros. Footwork chapter: Stemming, Smearing.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C5, C6, C11, C37, C41, C56]

### Mantle (classic top-out)
- **Also called:** mantel, mantelshelf (UK) [C41], press-out [C6], top-out.
  Variants: rockover, heel-hook mantel or heel rockover, "pool exit", hand-in
  and hand-out press [C5, C6, C39].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V0–V2** on gym walls with top-outs; harder
  mantles at **V4–V6**. Touchstone's clinic for climbers bouldering V5–V6
  lists "rock overs and mantles" alongside drop knees, hooks and dynos [C46].
  Flanagan notes topouts are hard to train in gyms [C6]. The grade is a
  judgement.
- **What it is:** converting a pull on a ledge or lip into a push down on it,
  then standing up on it, like climbing out of a swimming pool [C39].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Before you start:** check the top, and chalk your palms, because mantles
     need palm contact [C5, C6].
  2. **Feet high:** "Your feet lead your hips" [C5]. Place a foot on a hold high
     enough that your waist will be level with your hands when you stand [C6].
     On steep or rounded tops, heel-hook the lip instead [C5, C6].
  3. **Pull and stand:** pull your shoulders toward the lip and stand up on the
     high foot [C6].
  4. **Pick the first hand:** the hand on the higher or more positive side, or
     your preferred hand [C6]. Chris Schulte uses the hand farthest from the
     high leg [C5].
  5. **Flip it:** as your shoulders clear the lip, turn that hand flat. The
     fingers point toward the other hand and the elbow points up [C6].
     Schulte: fingers point toward you, shoulder turned in, elbow strongly out
     [C5]. *Hand-in* (thumb toward the ground) gives more initial push through
     shoulder and triceps. *Hand-out* (thumb up the slope) works once your
     weight is over the lip [C39].
  6. **Press:** straighten that arm, flip the second hand so the fingers face
     each other, and press until both arms are straight [C6].
  7. **Foot on top:** bring a foot onto the lip, lean forward to weight it, move
     the near hand up, then bring the other foot up [C6]. With the high knee
     bent up and out over the toe, point the knee down to pull the hips over
     the foot [C5]. Use the trailing leg as a flag for balance [C5].
  8. **One motion:** do steps 3–7 smoothly, without pausing at the tipping
     point [C5, C6].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A top-out lip with no holds beyond it.
  - A flat ledge mid-wall with nothing above within reach.
  - A blank, rounded top: heel rockover [C6].
  - *Failure symptoms:* stuck at chest height with bent elbows and nowhere to
    go, meaning no high foot [C5, C39]. Falling backward as you move the second
    hand, meaning you moved a hand before you were locked off and balanced
    [C39]. Foot blowing as you rock over, meaning you put a knee or thigh on top
    [C5].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - The knee dab: knee or thigh on the top reduces friction and changes the
    foot's angle [C5, C39].
  - Hesitating at the start [C5].
  - Not inspecting the top [C6].
  - Learning on hard terrain; practise 2–3 grades below your limit [C6].
  - Always mantling on your favourite side [C6].
- **Tall climber note:** reaching the lip and placing a high foot are easier.
  Rocking a long torso over a high foot is harder, because the knee comes up
  near the chest. A slightly lower foot that lets you press out first can
  suit a tall body. A heavier climber pushes more weight through the triceps,
  so get as much weight onto the high foot as early as possible [C5]
  (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** side profile, four-frame sequence on a vertical wall
  with a flat top.
  (1) Both hands on the lip, right foot high on a foothold just below the lip
  (heel or toe), left foot low.
  (2) Shoulders above the lip; left hand flipped flat on the top, fingers
  pointing right toward the other hand, elbow pointing up; arrow down from the
  palm.
  (3) Both arms pressing straight, hips at lip level, right foot about to step
  onto the lip.
  (4) Right foot on top, body leaning forward over it, left knee off the top
  (crossed-out ghost knee on the lip to show "no knee dab").
  Centre-of-gravity marker in each frame moving from below the lip to over the
  right foot.
- **Related:** Mantle onto volumes (mid-problem), Palming and pressing.
  Footwork chapter: Rockover, Heel hook, High step.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C5, C6, C39, C40, C41, C46]

### Mantle onto volumes (mid-problem)
- **Also called:** volume mantle, press-out, rockover onto a volume. "Pool
  exit" for flat ledges [C5].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V6** (judgement, based on Touchstone listing
  mantles and rockovers as V5–V6 skills [C46]).
- **What it is:** the same pull-to-push transition as a top-out, but onto a
  volume or big flat hold in the middle of a problem. You often have to keep
  moving off it rather than stand up and walk away.
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Spot it from the ground:** a big flat or gently sloping volume top at
     chest height, with nothing usable above it within reach.
  2. **High foot first:** a foothold or smear high on the volume's side, or a
     heel on the volume's lip [C5, C39].
  3. **Lock off** on the volume's edge or a hold near it until you're
     balanced. Only then flip a hand [C39].
  4. **Press on the flat face:** heel of the palm on the volume's top face.
     Hand-in for power, hand-out once the weight is over it [C39]. Press away
     from the direction of travel [C11].
  5. **Rock over:** move the hips over the high foot and use the other hand
     for balance on the volume or wall [C6].
  6. **Exit:** stand up on the volume. On steep walls, keep pressing with the
     palm while the next hand goes up, because there is no flat top to rest on.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Large chalked volume tops with no holds above.
  - Footholds placed on the volume itself.
  - Dual-texture volumes whose textured zone is on top: the setter is telling
    you to press there [C49].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the palm slides on the volume because you are pressing
    with fingers instead of the heel of the hand, or pushing parallel to the
    face. You topple backward on releasing the lower hand, because your hips
    are not over the hand and foot.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Trying to pull on the volume's edge when it slopes and should be pressed.
  - Hesitating at the tipping point [C5, C6].
  - Kneeing the volume [C5].
- **Illustration brief:** side profile on a vertical wall. A large wedge volume
  at chest height with a flat top. Climber's left foot high on the volume's
  side face, right foot low. Left palm flat on the volume top, heel of the hand
  pressing (arrow down), elbow up and straightening. Right hand reaching up to
  the next hold. Centre-of-gravity marker moving (arrow) from below the
  volume's lip to above the left foot.
- **Related:** Mantle (classic top-out), Palming and pressing, Dual-texture
  holds and macros.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C5, C6, C11, C39, C46, C49]

### Thumb catch, thumb press and thumb tricks (volumes, blobs)
- **Also called:** thumb catch, thumb press, thumb mantle, thumb hook,
  "thumbercling" (overhead thumb undercling), inverse crimp [C7]; Vulcan
  (two fingers either side of an edge meeting a lip) [C11].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V5** (judgement). On steep problems the thumb
  matters most on slopers [C2], and volume-heavy gym problems at these grades
  need thumb use.
- **What it is:** using the thumb as a separate contact point. It can catch an
  edge or dimple, press against a face, or pinch an edge, adding force or
  turning a bad hold into a usable one [C7].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Thumb catch:** probe the hold for small bumps or dips and press the
     thumb into one while the fingers hold the main part. You may use a weaker
     part of the hold to get the thumb on [C7].
  2. **Thumb press on a volume or blob:** press the thumb pad flat on a face
     beside or below the fingers, as an extra pushing point that takes load
     off the fingers [C7].
  3. **Thumb on a sloper:** try the thumb below and to the side of the hold to
     pinch it [C2].
  4. **Volume edge:** thumb on one face, fingers on the other [C11]. Where an
     edge meets the lip, use two fingers either side (Vulcan) [C11].
  5. **Thumbercling:** on an overhead hold that faces down, press up with the
     thumb pad, keeping wrist and fingers straight [C7].
  6. **Inverse crimp:** see Thumb wrap and thumb lock [C7].
  7. Gresham (UKC, Part 3, snippet): change grip with the first hand and pinch
     the hold, a technique "particularly useful on volumes or when using
     indented sloping holds" [C51].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Rounded holds and volumes with a ridge, dimple or second face near your
    hand.
  - Pumpy sequences, where thumb friction can rest the fingers [C7].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the hand rotates off a sloper or volume edge (the
    fingers slide forward). Add a thumb below to stop the rotation.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Never looking for thumb features [C1].
  - Bending the wrist on thumberclings [C7].
- **Illustration brief:** close-up, view facing the wall, of a right hand on a
  large rounded volume edge. Fingers draped over the top face; thumb pressed
  into a small dimple on the front face below the fingers. Arrows: fingers
  pulling down-in, thumb pushing up-in. Inset: thumbercling, hand overhead,
  thumb pad pressing up on the underside of a downward-facing edge, wrist
  straight.
- **Related:** Pinch, Sloper, Thumb wrap and thumb lock, Dual-texture holds and
  macros.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C1, C2, C7, C11, C51]

### Wrap grip (aretes, big round holds)
- **Also called:** wrap, guppy (a hold you wrap sideways, gripping mainly
  across the palm [C34]), meat-hook (hand hooked over an A-frame volume peak,
  palm on one side and fingers on the other [C11]).
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3** (judgement): aretes and large round holds
  become common on gym problems at about this level.
- **What it is:** wrapping the whole hand, palm included, round a convex
  feature such as an arete, volume ridge, ball or horn, so friction acts over
  a large area and the thumb and palm oppose the fingers.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put the palm on the near face of the feature and wrap the fingers round
     onto the far face [C11, C34].
  2. Use the heel of the hand on horns and edges to spare the finger muscles
     [C37].
  3. On round holds, wrap the hand round the hold rather than gripping
     straight on, and angle the wrist slightly left or right to find the
     secure position [C3].
  4. **Arete:** palm and thumb on one face, fingers on the other. Squeeze, and
     keep the elbow low and behind the hand so the pull goes along the arete's
     edge **(unverified)**.
  5. Pair it with a foot or the other hand pushing the other way (compression)
     [C11].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Aretes on gym features.
  - Peaked (A-frame) volumes [C11].
  - Big spherical holds.
  - Guppy-shaped holds with a vertical slot for the palm [C34].
  - *Failure symptoms:* the hand slides round the feature, meaning the pull is
    across the feature instead of along it, or nothing is compressing against
    the wrap.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Using only the fingertips on a feature that needs palm contact.
  - Pulling straight out from the wall on an arete instead of squeezing.
- **Tall climber note:** large hands wrap round bigger features (inference
  from [C37] on hand size).
- **Illustration brief:** view from behind on a gym arete (two wall planes
  meeting at an outside corner). Right hand: palm and thumb on the left
  plane, fingers wrapped round onto the right plane. Squeeze arrows from
  palm and fingers toward the edge. Right elbow low. Left foot smearing on the
  right plane, pushing back toward the arete (compression arrow). Inset:
  meat-hook over an A-frame volume peak.
- **Related:** Pinch, Sloper, Dual-texture holds and macros.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C3, C11, C34, C37]

### Dual-texture holds, macros and volumes as handholds
- **Also called:** dual-tex, dual texture (two finishes on one hold or volume)
  [C49, C50]; macro (very large hold, between a hold and a volume)
  **(unverified definition)**; volume.
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V2+** for reading, **V4+** for using smooth
  zones and faces well (judgement). Volumes and dual-texture are common on
  modern gym and competition walls [C49].
- **What it is:** big features that you hold by shape, friction and pressing
  rather than by a lip. On dual-texture pieces, a grippy zone marks the
  intended contact area and a smooth zone marks where you should not rely on
  contact [C49]. The smooth part can turn "a comfortable jug into a more
  interesting feature that requires more precise movement" [C50].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Read the texture:** grippy zones are where the setter wants hands or
     feet. Smooth zones tell you to keep the body off them or pass them [C49].
  2. **Get under it:** hang low, straight arms, heavy body [C11].
  3. **Use every surface:** flat faces for palms and feet, edges for crimps or
     open hands, A-frame peaks for a meat-hook, the edge for a pinch [C11].
  4. **Look for bulges and scoops** on round volumes; chalk marks show the
     best spot [C11].
  5. **Push and pull:** press away from the direction of travel [C11].
  6. **Compress:** on steep walls, push with both feet or hands on opposite
     faces [C11].
  7. **Move slowly and deliberately** [C11].
  8. Alpinetrek: holding a volume from underneath is harder, so grip its edge
     with the fingertips [C37].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Any large geometric feature. The setter's orientation of the volume or
    dual-tex hold fixes the move; rotating the same piece changes it [C49,
    C50].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you slide off a volume face because you are pulling on
    a face you should press, or your hips are above the face [C11]. Skin
    abrasion, meaning rushed placements [C11].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Ignoring the texture zones and trying to use the smooth surface.
  - Moving fast [C11].
  - Standing too close to the wall when standing on volumes. Balance is
    farthest from the wall [C11].
- **Illustration brief:** view facing the wall, slightly from the side. A large
  dual-texture hexagonal volume, with the textured zones shaded (stippled) on
  the top-left face and the right edge, and the rest left smooth (white).
  Climber's left hand pinching the textured right edge; right palm pressing the
  textured top-left face. Feet: one smearing on the lower smooth face (red X,
  slipping), corrected ghost foot on a textured footstrip. Legend: "stipple =
  grip here, white = don't".
- **Related:** Sloper, Pinch, Palming and pressing, Mantle onto volumes, Wrap
  grip, Sweet-spot finding.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C11, C37, C49, C50]

### Re-gripping and adjusting on a hold
- **Also called:** grip switching [C1, C51], readjusting, shuffling **(the last
  unverified as a named term)**.
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4** (judgement). Problems start to
  include holds you must take one way and use another, such as catching a
  sloper then rolling to a pinch.
- **What it is:** changing how a hand holds a hold after first contact, or
  changing grip type during a move, without letting go.
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Plan it:** decide from the ground how you will *catch* the hold and how
     you will *use* it. The best catching spot is not always the best using
     spot [C2].
  2. **Aim precisely:** aim at a small visible target on the hold such as a
     screw hole or logo ("aim small, miss small"). Aim with your head, putting
     your nose where your balanced body position needs it, so a fumbled hand
     has time to correct [C18].
  3. **Small adjustments under low load:** shift the fingers one at a time
     ("walk" them) while body weight is mostly on the feet or the other hand
     **(unverified)**.
  4. **Change grip type as the body moves:** for example, open hand while
     below a hold, shifting toward a half crimp or pinch as you move above it
     **(unverified)**. Gresham describes changing grip with the first hand and
     pinching the hold, useful on volumes and indented slopers [C51].
  5. **Switch grips to share load:** alternating grip types saves energy [C1].
     Gresham recommends practising grip-switching when warming up or on easy
     terrain, and notes it helps during finger-injury rehab [C51].
  6. **Don't let the hand be forced open under maximal load.** Re-grip with
     control or let go [C15, C20].
  7. **Relocate the whole hand** on bigger holds [C3].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Holds you catch at speed.
  - Long rails or volumes you must move along.
  - Holds whose best spot depends on your body position.
  - *Failure symptoms:* you hit the hold in the wrong spot and spend energy
    fixing it, so aim smaller [C18]. Your fingers creep open during a hard
    move, so re-grip deliberately or stop [C15].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Fiddling with the grip while hanging on a bent arm.
  - Neglecting the lower hand. German teaching material notes that climbers pay
    less attention to the holding hand than the reaching hand, which causes
    unstable positions [C42].
- **Illustration brief:** close-up sequence, view facing the wall, three frames
  of a right hand on a large rounded edge.
  (1) Catch: fingers land open on the near part (dashed target circle on a
  screw-hole mark).
  (2) Walk: index and middle lifted and moved 2 cm right (curved arrows).
  (3) Final: fingers in a half crimp on the incut sweet spot, thumb pressed
  into a dimple. Tiny ghost figure under each frame showing the hips moving
  from left of the hold to under it.
- **Related:** Matching on small holds, Sweet-spot finding, Thumb catch.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, Ep. 168 "A Radical Shift in How You Take Holds —
  Coaching w/ Dan #6", companion page:
  https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/a-radical-shift-in-how-you-grab-holds.
- **Sources:** [C1, C2, C3, C15, C18, C20, C42, C51]

### Matching on small holds
- **Also called:** match, matching hands, hand match; finger swap
  **(unverified)**; stacking (hand on hand) **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4** (judgement). Small single holds you must
  move both hands through appear around here.
- **What it is:** getting both hands onto the same hold, or swapping one hand
  for the other, when the hold has little room.
- **How to do it:** **(this whole entry is unverified: standard coaching
  practice, no source fetched)**
  1. **Leave room:** when you first take the hold, put the first hand on the
     side away from where the second hand will arrive, and use fewer fingers
     if necessary.
  2. **Bring the body under or beside the hold first,** so both hands can load
     it in the same direction. A match on a sidepull needs the body leaning off
     for both hands.
  3. **Finger swap:** lift one finger of the first hand at a time and put a
     finger of the second hand into its place, moving from one end of the hold
     to the other.
  4. **Stacking:** if there is no room, put the second hand's fingers on top of
     the first hand's fingers, then slide the first hand out.
  5. **Pinch swap:** on a pinch, the second hand's thumb takes over the first
     thumb's spot while its fingers squeeze from the other side.
  6. **Keep the feet weighted** throughout. A foot slip during a match shock-
     loads two half-gripped hands (see the shock-load figures in the
     finger-injury evidence [C26]).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A single hold with no other usable hold nearby for the next hand.
  - A traverse.
  - A sequence where the next move goes from the "wrong" hand.
  - *Failure symptoms:* there isn't room for the second hand, so the first hand
    was placed in the middle. You barn-door as the first hand leaves, so the
    body wasn't moved under the hold first.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Grabbing the best part with the first hand and leaving no space.
  - Matching while on a bent arm.
- **Illustration brief:** close-up, view facing the wall, of a 15 cm edge in
  three frames.
  (1) Left hand half-crimping the left half only, right hand approaching.
  (2) Left little and ring fingers lifted; right index and middle fingers
  placed in their spots (arrows).
  (3) Right hand fully on, left hand leaving (upward arrow).
- **Related:** Re-gripping and adjusting, Half crimp, Sweet-spot finding.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [C26] (shock-load note only; technique steps unverified)

### Sweet-spot finding
- **Also called:** finding the best part of the hold; "aim small" [C18].
- **Category:** Hands and holds (grips)
- **Becomes important at:** **V2–V3** (judgement). Once holds are no longer
  uniform jugs, where you grab them matters.
- **What it is:** finding the part of a hold, and the hand position on it,
  that gives the most contact and the best direction of pull for where your
  body will be.
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Inspect from the ground:** walk close to the hold and look at its shape
     from the side as well as the front. Pick the best part, considering your
     body position at the moment you'll use it [C2].
  2. **Read the chalk:** chalk marks show where other climbers place fingers and
     thumbs [C11, C38].
  3. **Read the texture:** on dual-texture holds, the grippy zone is the
     intended contact [C49].
  4. **Look for incuts, ridges, bulges, scoops and thumb dimples** [C7, C11].
  5. **Feel for it:** take as much of the hold as possible and adjust the pull
     direction until you can "lie into" it [C42]. Try fingers together, apart,
     crimped, pinched [C3].
  6. **Aim at a small target** on the hold (screw hole, logo) [C18].
  7. **Accept a worse part if it gives a better body position:** a more
     sloping part of the hold can be the better choice if it is easier to reach
     or supports a more stable position (search-result snippet; source not
     identified).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Every non-jug hold.
  - Large holds and volumes with one good spot.
  - *Failure symptoms:* you slip off a hold that worked for someone else, so
    you used the wrong spot or the wrong pull direction [C42]. You re-adjust on
    every hold, so you're not aiming before you move [C18].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Reaching straight for the hold without first finding its best part [C2].
  - Choosing the spot by how it feels in the hand while ignoring where your
    hips will be.
- **Illustration brief:** close-up, view facing the wall, of a large irregular
  hold. Chalk smudges shaded on the upper-right incut. A dashed circle marks
  the sweet spot. Three hand outlines: one on the smooth left part (X), one
  centred (?), one on the chalked incut (tick). A small inset side profile
  shows that the incut faces up-left, so the hips must be down-right of the
  hold (arrow).
- **Related:** Re-gripping and adjusting, Dual-texture holds and macros,
  Sloper.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, Ep. 168 "A Radical Shift in How You Take Holds",
  https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/a-radical-shift-in-how-you-grab-holds.
- **Sources:** [C2, C3, C7, C11, C18, C38, C42, C49]

## Part 3. Grade-placement evidence (free-form)

All V-grade placements in this chapter are **judgement calls**, grounded in
the following:

- **No syllabus ties grips to V-grades.**
  - NICAS Bouldering (the UK scheme; a Scottish education resource calls the
    pair NICAS and NIBAS, the National Indoor Climbing and Bouldering Award
    Schemes [C55, snippet only]) has five levels. Level 2 "Foundation
    Boulderer" introduces reading a route. Level 3 "Competent Boulderer"
    covers "hold types and advanced movement techniques", and is said to match
    most gym membership standards [C44]. Level 2 venues must offer problems on
    "a variety of angles" including an overhang [C45].
  - The published NICAS pages give no level-by-level grip list and no grade
    equivalents.
  - The German school material treats hold types (pocket, sloper, pinch, edge)
    and load direction as beginner content [C42].
  - Coaches' "skills for V5" lists name movement skills (drop knees, hooks,
    rockovers, mantles, dynos) rather than grips [C46, C48].
- **Grade-progression guides:**
  - Bouldering Boss: V4 is where "good technique starts to be essential". At
    V5, holds are "smaller and crimpier" and body tension matters. It takes
    roughly 6–12 months to a first indoor V4 and up to two years to V5 [C48].
  - Mountain Project thread: V3 can be muscled through. V4 needs technique,
    especially footwork. Slopers and oddly placed holds were the stuck
    climber's main problem [C47].
- **Grip-strength data:** open hand is the stronger grip for V1–V4 climbers
  [C21]. Climbers choose a half crimp on 1–2 cm edges [C29].
- **Counterpoint:** on an outdoors Q&A forum, one discussion argued you can't
  generally tie techniques to grades; a hard problem might be all slopers, all
  pinches or all crimps [C54, snippet only].
