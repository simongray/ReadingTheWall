# Body positioning and balance: research dossier

Category: **Body positioning and balance**. Source prefix: **B**.

Conventions used throughout: LH/RH = left/right hand, LF/RF = left/right foot. "Holding hand" = the hand that stays on during a move; "reaching hand" = the hand that moves. CoG = centre of gravity (centre of mass). Examples are written for one side; mirror them for the other. "Judgement" marks my own synthesis where no source states the point directly. German names are from my own knowledge, not from sources, and are all marked **(unverified)**.

**Hold orientation convention.** A hold "faces" the way its gripping surface points. You pull the opposite way, and your body ends up on the side you pull toward.
- A **sidepull** faces away from your body. Example: a hold on your left, gripping edge facing left; you pull it rightward with your body to its right, leaning away from it.
- A **gaston** faces toward your midline. Example: a hold on your right, edge facing left; you pull it rightward, away from your midline, elbow out and thumb down.
- An **undercling** faces down; you pull it up.

In every example below, "RH on a sidepull" means a hold to your right whose edge faces right, so you hang to its left.

## 1. Concepts: the mechanics under every body position

### 1.1 Centre of gravity

- Your whole weight acts as if it were concentrated at one point, the CoG. Standing upright it sits roughly behind the navel, inside the pelvis; Hörst uses "near the navel" as the climber's marker [B16]. It moves whenever a limb moves: flag a leg out to the left and the CoG shifts left; raise both arms and it rises (standard mechanics).
- Balance on the wall is about where this point sits relative to your contacts. The FUNdamentals of Climbing 1 workshop (the first step of the UK coaching pathway) teaches "Centre of Gravity", "Weight Transfer" and "Economy of Climbing Movement" as the core movement skills [B2]. FUNdamentals 2 adds "base of support and its relationship to the centre of gravity" [B1]. FUNdamentals 3 covers how CoG and base of support change "as climbing becomes more challenging" [B3].
- Practical consequence for a heavy, tall climber (judgement): the turning force that a CoG offset creates equals weight × offset distance. A heavier body produces a bigger turning force for the same offset, and a long body makes big offsets easy to create. The positions that put weight on the feet and keep the CoG near the line of support therefore pay off more for a heavy, tall climber than for a light climber.

### 1.2 Base of support and the "triangle"

- On flat ground you are stable when a plumb line from your CoG falls inside the area enclosed by your contact points (the base of support). *The Self-Coached Climber* (Hague and Hunter, verified via a reader's detailed notes) distinguishes three balance states [B10]:
  - **stable:** CoG well inside the base;
  - **offset:** CoG near or at the edge;
  - **dynamic:** CoG outside the base, as in a dyno.

  It lists ways to improve balance: broaden the base (wider feet, backstepping, flagging), lower the CoG, change its path (arching or rocking over a foothold before moving the hands), use momentum, and create body tension [B10].
- How to translate this to a wall (judgement, built on B1, B6, B16, B17):
  - **Seen from behind:** the plumb line from your CoG should fall between your outermost contacts, and ideally close to the line joining the hand and foot that will hold you during the next move (see 1.5).
  - **Seen from the side:** on slabs and vertical walls the CoG should sit over your feet, or just behind them on a slab, so the legs carry the weight. Hörst says to keep the body straight and over the feet on vertical walls and to move the hips *out* from the wall over the foothold on slabs [B17]. On overhangs the CoG cannot be over the feet, so the arms must take more of the weight [B16].
- **The triangle.** With three limbs on, the three contacts form a triangle. In an instrumented study, when a climber hanging on four holds lifted the right foot, the vertical load was shared by all three remaining holds (a "tripod"). The horizontal (in/out) forces were carried by the left hand and left foot [B6]. The BMC FUNdamentals 2 syllabus teaches finding "an efficient rest or clipping position using the triangle principle and straight arms" [B1].
- Practical rule (judgement): before you move a limb, check that the three remaining contacts form a triangle that has your CoG inside it as seen from behind. If only two contacts will remain (for example when you cut a foot), you have a line, not a triangle, and you will rotate about that line unless the CoG is on it (1.5).
- **Caveat.** The author of the notes on *The Self-Coached Climber* argues that base-of-support pictures cannot explain flagging, and that body tension does not change the base at all. He prefers analysing the force each contact can supply [B10]. That view is useful because each contact can only push or pull in some directions: a foothold can only be pushed, a downward-facing edge can only be pulled down, and friction is limited. Balance means the available contact forces can cancel gravity and any turning forces (judgement).

### 1.3 Why straight arms save energy (skeleton vs muscle)

- **Lever mechanics** (my explanation; standard statics). When the elbow is straight, the pull from the hold runs along the line of the forearm and upper arm, very close to the elbow joint. The elbow flexors (biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis) then produce almost no torque, because bones, ligaments and the shoulder carry the load. With the elbow bent to 90°, the line of pull passes far from the elbow, and the flexors must produce a large torque continuously just to keep the angle.
- **Coaching sources:**
  - With straight arms "the skeleton carries the load instead of the upper-arm muscles" on vertical-to-overhanging rests [B12].
  - Underclings: keep arms straight so the weight rests on the skeleton rather than the biceps [B30].
  - Straight arms transfer force through the skeleton and save forearm strength; "lower your hips" to straighten them [B22].
  - Rowland adds that grip is weaker with bent elbows. He gives this as a coaching claim without a cited study [B36].
  - A physiotherapist links straight arms to less biceps and forearm reliance and lower elbow-injury risk [B28].
- **Not passive hanging:**
  - Hooper (physiotherapist) calls "hang on your skeleton" incomplete advice. Climbing cycles through relaxing, setting up, contracting and catching, and catches need shoulder-blade engagement to control momentum and protect the shoulder [B50].
  - Gresham's default is arms "virtually straight" with a few degrees of bend, and he warns that sagging onto fully straight arms can cause repetitive shoulder strain (search-result summary; full text paywalled) [B23].
- **Lab nuance.** A 2025 study compared a bent-arm/straight-legs stance (UP) with a straight-arm/bent-legs stance (DOWN) on a +5° slab, a vertical wall and a −5° overhang [B8]:
  - The steeper the wall, the more load moved from the feet to the hand, even over that 10° range.
  - The straight-arm, low-hip position put 23–32% more of body weight on the hand and excited the finger flexors more at every angle.
  - On the overhang, the bent-arm position excited the latissimus dorsi and biceps more.
  - The authors note that climbers prefer bent arms on non-overhanging walls and straight arms on overhangs.

  Translation (judgement): straight arms save the biceps and lats. But sinking low to straighten the arms on a slab or vertical wall moves weight off the feet onto the fingers, so there you should stand tall over the feet instead. A UKC forum thread reaches the same view from experience [B68].

### 1.4 Hips close to the wall on steep ground

- **Statics** (judgement). On an overhang your feet are on the wall and your CoG hangs out from it. The horizontal distance between the CoG and the footholds acts as a lever arm: the farther out the CoG, the more weight the hands must hold and the more the feet tend to swing off. Bringing the pelvis, or one hip, to the wall shortens that lever arm.
- **Sources:**
  - A physiotherapist says bringing the pelvis or one hip into the wall on steep rock moves the CoG closer to the toes, so gravity loads the shoulders, elbows and fingers less [B28].
  - On steep walls many climbers let the CoG slump away from the wall, which makes the climbing pumpy; the twist lock and backstep pull the body toward the wall and put more weight on the feet [B16].
  - Gresham says parallel ("square") hips push you away from the wall on steep terrain and force you to use arm strength [B22].
  - Measured data: on overhangs climbers relied much more on their arms [B7], and load moves from the feet to the hand as the wall steepens [B8].
- **Why turning side-on gets closer than square** (judgement). Square-on, the pelvis can only come forward as far as the knees and feet allow, which takes hip external rotation (the frog, 4.2). Turned side-on, the side of the pelvis can touch the wall and the leg can lie along it.
- **Where sources disagree.** "Hips in" is a steep-wall rule, not a universal one:
  - On slabs, move the hips out over the feet [B17].
  - Elite climbers held their CoG about 5 cm *farther* from the wall than controls and shifted their weight side to side about 1.3 times more [B9].
  - Hooper argues that being too tight to the wall blocks rotation and unloads the feet, so the fix is to "cast out" to a balance point [B48].

### 1.5 Rotation about the axis between two contacts (the barn door)

- **Mechanics.** With two contacts, the line between them acts like a hinge. If the CoG is not on that line, gravity creates a turning force (torque) equal to weight × the perpendicular horizontal distance from the hinge, and the body swings around it. The 2025 sensorised-wall study states this directly: the offset between the CoG and "the axis connecting the right hand and foot" would rotate the climber around that axis, and extra load on the other foot is needed to counteract it [B8].
- **Same-side vs diagonal pairs.** A same-side pair (LH + LF) makes a near-vertical hinge on one side of the body, with the CoG hanging beside it, so you swing open. A diagonal pair (LH + RF) makes a hinge that runs through the middle of the body, so the torque is small. This is Hörst's "Left-Right Rule": pair a left hand with a right foot, because same-side pairings cause "barndooring or sideways rotation off the wall" [B11].
- **Four ways to stop the rotation** (judgement, synthesised from B11, B13, B35, B45, B59, B62):
  1. **Put the CoG on the hinge:** shift the hips over the standing foot and under the holding hand.
  2. **Add a counterweight on the far side of the hinge:** flag a leg [B13].
  3. **Add a contact that pushes back:** a flag foot or off-foot pressing into the wall, or the other hand on a sidepull [B45].
  4. **Move fast:** a quick pop or dyno gives the rotation no time to build [B62].

  A fifth option is to hold the swing with arm and core strength on good holds, but it costs energy [B62].
- **Spinout is a different failure.** In a backstep you can follow cross-body tension and still rotate out of the twist, because the foothold is in the wrong place [B39]. See Backstep.

### 1.6 Opposition forces

- **Friction needs a squeeze** (standard mechanics). The friction a hold or smear can supply is limited to roughly the coefficient of friction × the force pressing the surfaces together. Holds that are not downward-pulling edges (sidepulls, gastons, underclings, slopers, volumes, smears) only work when something else pushes or pulls the other way to create that pressing force.
- **Coaching syllabi and sources:**
  - BMC FUNdamentals 2 has a whole unit on "Opposing forces techniques", taught through bridging and laybacking [B1].
  - Hörst lists the standard pairings: a sidepull with the outside edge of the opposite foot, a gaston with the inside edge of the opposite foot, an undercling with the opposite foot, opposing sidepulls pulled inward, and opposing gastons pulled outward [B14].
  - "Stemming is all about oppositional force" [B29].
  - On slopers, "once your core sags, you will lose opposition" [B31].
- **Three families:**
  - **Hand vs hand:** opposing sidepulls, opposing gastons, compression.
  - **Hand vs foot:** undercling with high feet, layback, sidepull with a backstep.
  - **Foot vs foot:** stem, drop knee, bicycle.

### 1.7 Weight shift (zig-zag up the wall)

- Gresham's first "counterintuitive" fix for off-balance climbers is to shift the hips sideways over the weight-bearing leg, so they zig-zag up the wall rather than staying centred ("Shift, baby!") [B22].
- Elite climbers show larger, systematic side-to-side CoG oscillations than novices, with weight alternating between limbs [B9].
- Hörst puts the CoG over the foothold before standing on it, and pre-weights the hold [B17]. He prefers a "step, step, reach, reach" rhythm to "step, reach" [B69].

## 2. Grade-placement evidence (what exists and what does not)

- **UK award schemes (NICAS / NIBAS).** NIBAS is now "NICAS Bouldering", with five levels: New, Foundation, Competent, Skilled and Performance Boulderer. Level 3 covers "advanced movement techniques" [B4]. Depot says Level 3 takes a minimum of 18 weekly sessions and Level 4 a minimum of 30 [B5]. None of the public NICAS pages I found name individual techniques per level or give grade equivalents. That is a gap; the detail lives in the logbooks [B4, B5].
- **UK coaching pathway (BMC / Mountain Training FUNdamentals):**
  - Workshop 1 covers CoG, weight transfer and economy of movement [B2].
  - Workshop 2 covers base of support, the triangle principle, straight arms, bridging, laybacking, flagging and drop knee. It includes the outcome "identify and explain how a climber will choose to flag or drop knee" [B1].
  - Workshop 3 is for coaching climbers with "advanced" technique, and its course page asks coaches to boulder V3/f6A+ [B3].

  The scheme therefore treats flagging and the drop knee as *fundamentals*, below the advanced tier (my inference).
- **US gym curricula:**
  - Movement's "Intermediate Technique Series" targets "that V5 plateau" with heel and toe hooks, flagging, body tension, compression, deadpoints and sequencing [B63].
  - Touchstone's high-intermediate clinic requires "Experience bouldering at V5-V6" and teaches drop knees, hooks, rockovers and dynamic movement [B64].
- **Coach statements tied to angle or grade:**
  - Twist-locks belong on overhangs of roughly 10° or more, and a reverse flag "is rarely necessary below 5.9 or V4" [B44].
  - Start learning twist lock and backstep on boulders about 20° overhanging [B15].
  - The drop knee "works poorly" on 5–25° walls and comes into its own at 30–75° [B20].
  - Low- to intermediate-level climbers struggle on overhangs because they keep their hips parallel to the wall [B22].
  - A V3 cave already demands full-body tension [B52].
- **Disagreement.** The UK coaching pathway places the drop knee among the fundamentals [B1], while Touchstone teaches it in a V5–V6 clinic [B64]. My placement (V4) splits the difference: the first steep V3s can be done square, but V4 and V5 on steep walls usually need the drop knee.

## 3. Technique entries

### Weight shift (hips over the active foot)
- **Also called:** shifting, weighting the foot, "zig-zag" (Gresham's image), weight transfer (BMC) [B2, B22]; German: Gewichtsverlagerung **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V0. Weight transfer is the first movement skill on the UK coaching pathway [B2], and Gresham names a centred, un-shifted body as the first habit of off-balance climbers [B22].
- **What it is:** Moving your hips sideways, and on slabs outward, until your CoG sits over the foot you are about to stand on, before you stand up or move a hand.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Place the foot precisely. Then press a little weight into it before committing, because downward pressure makes the rubber stick [B17].
  2. Slide your hips sideways until your belly button is over that foot; on a slab, also push the hips out from the wall so they are above the foot [B17, B22].
  3. Stand up on that leg alone, letting the other foot become light [B17].
  4. Reach only after you have stood up ("feet first, then reach") [B22].
  5. On the next move, shift the hips the other way, so they zig-zag up the wall [B22].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** Always on slabs and vertical walls, and whenever a foothold is out to one side of your current centre. Failure symptoms:
  - Your foot "bangs" or skates as you stand on it [B22].
  - You stand up on both feet at once and your hands take the weight.
  - You reach and your heels lift off the holds [B22].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Keeping the hips centred between the feet while stepping [B22].
  - Reaching with low feet [B22].
  - Climbing "ladder style" with one foot pushing at a time while the arms pull [B17].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs make the sideways shift distance larger. Move the hips first and the foot second (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical wall, as a three-frame sequence.
  - *Frame 1:* feet on two footholds at knee height 70 cm apart, hips centred, CoG marker between the feet, hands on two edges at head height.
  - *Frame 2:* hips slid right until the CoG marker sits over the right foot; the left foot is shown light (dotted outline). Horizontal arrow at the hips.
  - *Frame 3:* standing up straight on the right leg, LH reaching to an edge above.
  - *Overlay:* a dashed zig-zag line traces the path of the hips over several frames.
- **Related:** Square / frog position; Barn door; Straight arms.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B2, B9, B17, B22, B69]

### Straight arms (hanging on the skeleton)
- **Also called:** straight-arming, hanging on your skeleton, resting on straight arms; German: "lange Arme" **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V2.
  - Judgement: it starts to matter on the first overhanging problems and decides V3–V4 overhangs.
  - The UK coaching pathway teaches straight arms in its second, foundation-level workshop [B1].
  - Gresham notes that beginners naturally bend their arms [B22].
- **What it is:** Holding the wall with elbows straight, or bent only a few degrees, so the arm bones and shoulder carry the load and the elbow flexors rest. You move by pushing with the legs and turning the hips rather than pulling with the arms.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Between moves, let the elbows straighten to a slight bend [B23]. Keep the shoulders "set": pull the shoulder blades slightly down and away from the ears rather than hanging shrugged [B28, B50].
  2. On steep ground, lower your hips until the arms are straight [B22]. Keep the hips close to the wall and arch the back slightly so the feet stay loaded [B36].
  3. Start every upward move with the legs. Press with the feet while the arms stay straight, and bend them only once the legs have started the move [B27].
  4. To gain height without bending the arms, rotate: the hip on the reaching side turns to the wall, then that shoulder turns in [B43, B54, B23].
  5. Rest and shake one arm at a time from a straight-arm stance [B12].
  6. Bend the arms only for the last part of a move, a precise catch, or a lock-off. Catch with the shoulders engaged, not passive [B36, B50].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Overhanging walls whenever you hold a jug, rail, sidepull or undercling you can lean off [B56, B12].
  - Whenever you pause to read, chalk or shake [B12].
  - Underclings near the waist [B30].
  - Slopers on vertical-to-slightly-overhanging walls, together with low hips [B31].

  Failure symptoms:
  - Biceps burn on easy overhangs.
  - Your forearms pump before the crux.
  - Your chin ends up next to the hold you are on.
  - Your elbows point out sideways [B28].
  - You are breathless on a V2 overhang.

  *Do not* force it on slabs or vertical walls with good feet: standing tall with a slightly bent arm keeps more weight on the feet there [B8, B68].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Hanging passively with the shoulders shrugged, which strains the shoulder [B23, B28, B50].
  - Sinking low on a vertical wall, which moves weight from the feet to the fingers [B8].
  - Bending the arm to reach instead of twisting [B23, B43].
  - Pulling first and pushing second; reverse the order [B27, B11].
  - Assuming straight arms cure over-gripping; they do not [B36].
- **Tall climber note:** For a tall climber, sinking to straight arms on a vertical wall can drop the hips far below a good pushing angle. On vertical walls, stand up and accept a slightly bent arm (judgement, based on B8). On overhangs, long arms put the CoG far from the holds unless you turn a hip in [B28].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* side profile on a 30° overhang, two figures side by side.
  - *(a) Bent-arm version:* elbows at 90°, hips sagging 40 cm from the wall, CoG dot far from the wall, a small arc arrow at the biceps marked "working".
  - *(b) Straight-arm version:* both hands on one jug slightly above head height, arms straight, a dashed force line from hand through elbow to shoulder, hips 15 cm from the wall, heels low on two footholds at knee height, CoG dot close to the wall. An arrow from the feet pushing up and in.
- **Related:** Sitting / sagging into a position; Side-on / turning the hip in; Twist lock; Lock-off.

### Square / frog position
- **Also called:** front-on, square-on, facing in, frogging, frog legs, frog-legging, the "climbing square" [B57, B55, B61]; German: Frosch **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V1. It is the default stance from the first climb; a deliberate frog with the knees turned out matters from V1–V2 slab high steps.
  - Rowland: front-on is "generally preferred", and on vertical walls you should try every front-step option before twisting [B44].
  - Jorgeson calls the frog "crucial for highstepping" on slab [B32].
- **What it is:** Chest and both hips parallel to the wall, both feet on their inside edges (big-toe side), and the knees turned out to the sides so the pelvis can come close to the wall between them.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put both feet on footholds of similar height, about shoulder width or wider, on the inside edge of the shoe [B55].
  2. Turn each foot so it lies roughly along the wall with the heel turned in toward it; this keeps the hips square and the CoG close to the wall over the heels [B38].
  3. Rotate the knees outward, as in a plié, so the thighs open away from the wall [B55, B61].
  4. Bring the hips toward the wall between the knees on vertical and steeper walls. On a slab, keep them just above the feet instead [B17].
  5. To step up, lift one foot to a hold near hip height, knee out, move the hips over it, and stand ("rock over") [B32, B65].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Slab and vertical terrain.
  - Footholds side by side at similar heights.
  - Small edges where you need the inside edge for contact [B55].
  - Handholds straight above you that you pull straight down: horizontal edges, incut crimps and jugs.
  - A high foothold in front of your hip.
  - On overhangs: Gresham's frogging video is described as the fix for when you struggle to keep weight on your feet or your hips drift away from the wall [B61].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You feel your hips dragging you off the wall [B61].
  - You stand on your front points with heels high and calves burning.
  - Your knees bump into the wall and push your hips out.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pointing the knees forward into the wall, which pushes the hips out (judgement).
  - Staying square on steep ground. Parallel hips push you off the wall there; turn side-on instead [B22, B60].
  - Frogging when you need to move fast or far: double insteps "limit mobility significantly" [B55].
  - Hips too close to the wall on a slab [B17].
- **Tall climber note:** Long femurs need a lot of hip external rotation to get the pelvis close in a full frog; a mobility background such as yoga helps. If the hips will not come in, widen the feet before forcing the knees (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *Main view:* from behind, vertical wall. Two footholds level at knee height, 60 cm apart, both feet on insteps with toes pointing outward. Knees turned out; pelvis close to the wall; both hands on horizontal edges above head height; CoG marker between the feet, near the wall.
  - *Ghost:* knees pointing at the wall and hips 25 cm out.
  - *Inset:* a side profile comparing hip distance in the two versions.
- **Related:** Side-on / turning the hip in; Weight shift; Stemming / bridging.
- **Watch:** Neil Gresham Climbing Masterclass - Crux Films, "11 Neil Greshams Masterclass - Frogging", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sY4OicuWbiA
- **Sources:** [B17, B22, B32, B38, B44, B55, B57, B60, B61, B65]

### Side-on / turning the hip in
- **Also called:** hip in, hip to the wall, twisting in, turning in, stepping through (when you cross a foot to get there), outside-edging [B22, B60]; German: eindrehen **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V2, on the first overhanging problems.
  - Gresham: low- to intermediate-level climbers find overhangs strenuous because they keep their hips parallel to the wall [B22].
  - Rowland: twist-locks pay off on overhangs of roughly 10° or more [B44].
- **What it is:** Turning the pelvis so the hip on the reaching-hand side touches or nearly touches the wall, while the chest turns toward the holding hand. The reaching-side foot is usually on its outside edge.
- **How to do it** (RH holds, LH reaches):
  1. Hold with RH, ideally on a jug or a sidepull you can lean off [B38]. The sidepull sits to your right with its edge facing right, so you lean left, away from it.
  2. Place LF on its outside edge (little-toe side of the toe box) on a foothold below the right hand, or step LF through across the right foot [B22, B37].
  3. Put RF on its inside edge on a hold to the right, or flag it out straight [B15, B37].
  4. Rotate the pelvis so the left hip comes to the wall. Hörst: the hip opposite the pulling hand faces the wall and the chest turns toward the side-pulling hand [B14].
  5. Keep the right arm straight and lean off it [B43].
  6. Stand up on the left leg and reach with LH. The twist raises the left shoulder and adds reach [B19, B43].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Overhanging walls.
  - The holding hold is a jug, or a sidepull facing away from your body. For RH that means a hold on your right with its edge facing right, so you hang to its left and reach with LH.
  - A gaston as the holding hold (edge facing your midline) is a poor choice; stay squarer instead [B14, B38].
  - The target is up and toward the reaching-hand side.
  - A foothold sits below or slightly beyond the holding hand.
  - Pinches and vertical features: turning side-on reduces the thumb strength needed [B24].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You are a hand's width short on a reach you could make on a vertical wall.
  - You lock off and your hips sag.
  - Your feet cut as you reach.
  - Your arms are doing the work on easy steep ground [B22].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Turning the hip without turning the foot onto its outside edge, which jams the knee (judgement).
  - Turning the wrong hip: it is the reaching side that goes in [B15].
  - Using it on slabs with small footholds, where the twist can pop your foot off [B44].
  - Overuse: Rowland suggests twist-locks for roughly 25% of moves and reads heavy use as a possible weakness [B44].
  - Stopping between the lower-body and upper-body twist [B43].
- **Tall climber note:** Turning side-on narrows the body, which helps a broad-shouldered tall climber with cross moves [B49].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 25° overhang.
  - *Holds and limbs:* RH on a vertical sidepull at shoulder height to the right, edge facing right. LF on its outside edge on a foothold directly below the right hand at knee height. RF on an inside edge on a foothold 50 cm farther right, leg nearly straight.
  - *Body:* left hip touching the wall, chest facing right, LH reaching up-left at full stretch to a hold. CoG marker close to the wall above LF.
  - *Ghost:* the square-on version, hips 30 cm off the wall and LH stopping 10 cm short.
- **Related:** Backstep; Drop knee; Twist lock; Reaching.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B14, B15, B19, B22, B24, B37, B38, B43, B44, B49, B60]

### Backstep
- **Also called:** back-step, outside edge, outside-edging, stepping through (the move that gets you there).
  - Naming conflict: Jorgeson writes "backstep (drop-knee)" as nearly one move [B32], while Rowland and Hörst treat them as separate [B37, B40, B15].
  - German: part of "eindrehen" **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3. Hörst says to start learning twist lock and backstep on boulders about 20° past vertical [B15], and Rowland puts twist-locks on overhangs of roughly 10° or more [B44]. Gym V3s on 20–30° walls are where this starts to be needed (judgement).
- **What it is:** Standing on the outside edge of the reaching-side foot on a hold below the holding hand, with the other leg flagged or on a second foothold, so the reaching-side hip turns to the wall and the weight settles over that foot's heel.
- **How to do it** (RH holds, LH reaches):
  1. Hold with RH on a good jug or a sidepull: a hold to your right, edge facing right, which you pull leftward while hanging to its left [B38]. Flat edges lose grip as you turn. Gastons (edge facing your midline) are the worst holds for this, because the twist can lift your hand off them [B38].
  2. Find a foothold for LF that is on, or to the right of, "the line": the vertical line through the hold your RH is pulling on [B38]. A foothold to the left of the line (behind it) causes a spinout [B39].
  3. The foothold should be at roughly knee height or higher [B38].
  4. Place LF's outside edge on it. Point the toe down a little and lift the ankle [B32]. Keep the heel angled away from the wall: not flat against it, and the foot not fully parallel to the wall [B38].
  5. Flag the right leg out straight to the right, thighs parallel, or put RF on a complementary hold [B37, B15].
  6. Use your abdominals to rotate the lower body until the left hip is close to the wall. Let the hips drift until they sit "almost directly above your left heel" [B37].
  7. Press the feet toward each other while RH pulls down and in toward your torso [B15].
  8. Stand up through the left leg: as the toe pivots, the knee drops and the hips rise (Jorgeson's "wave of extension"). Then reach with LH [B32].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Overhanging walls; on vertical and slabby ground it gives little benefit (forum view) [B67].
  - The holding hand has a sidepull or jug.
  - A foothold at knee height or higher sits under or beyond the holding hand.
  - The reaching hand is going up.

  Failure symptoms:
  - **Spinout:** you are twisted in with cross-body tension, but your body keeps rotating back out of the twist and the arms have to hold it. The foothold is behind the line; switch to a drop knee [B39].
  - The move feels like a hard lock-off.
  - The hips swing out when the reaching hand lets go.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Foot behind the line [B38, B39].
  - Foot too low to get the hips over the heel [B38].
  - Heel flat against the wall, or foot fully parallel to it [B38].
  - Backstepping off a flat edge or a gaston [B38].
  - Letting the flagged leg bend, so the hips drift [B44].
  - Using it on slabs with small feet [B44].
- **Tall climber note:** "Knee height or above" is relative to your own knee. Footholds that look awkwardly high to shorter climbers can be ideal backsteps for a tall climber (judgement from B38).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 25° overhang. RH on a sidepull at shoulder height, its gripping edge facing right (away from the body). A dashed vertical line drawn down from the RH hold, labelled "the line".
  - *Feet:* LF on its outside edge on a foothold on the line at knee height. RF flagged straight out right, foot on blank wall.
  - *Body:* left hip at the wall; CoG marker over the left heel.
  - *Ghost:* LF placed 30 cm left of the line, with a curved arrow showing the body rotating out ("spinout").
  - *Close-up inset:* side view of the shoe, outside edge on the hold, toe pointing slightly down, heel angled away from the wall.
- **Related:** Side-on / turning the hip in; Drop knee; Twist lock; Back flag.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B15, B32, B37, B38, B39, B44, B67]

### Drop knee
- **Also called:** Egyptian (UK), knee drop, sometimes loosely "backstep" [B56, B32]; German: Ägypter **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V4 (judgement).
  - Gresham: works poorly on 5–25° walls and comes into its own at 30–75° [B20].
  - Touchstone teaches drop knees in a clinic for V5–V6 boulderers [B64].
  - The UK coaching pathway treats it as a fundamental [B1].

  Steep V3s can often be climbed square; on steep V4–V5 the drop knee is frequently the intended position.
- **What it is:** A twisted stance with both feet pushing outward against two footholds, and the reaching-side knee turned inward and down so that thigh lies against the wall. It locks the hips in, pulls the CoG toward the wall and raises the reaching shoulder [B19, B41].
- **How to do it** (RH holds, LH reaches):
  1. Find two footholds at roughly equal height, one on each side [B20]. Or, per Rowland, the drop-knee (left) foot on a hold between waist and shoulder height beside your start holds, and the other foot wide on a hold below the waist [B41].
  2. If you can, place RF first and then LF from a stable position [B20].
  3. Put LF on the front point of the toe, on the near corner of the hold so there is room to pivot [B20]. Then rotate it from a front-step onto the outside edge as the knee drops [B41]. (Gresham says front point, not outside edge [B20]; Rowland says outside edge [B40]. The "start on the toe, pivot onto the edge" version reconciles the two.)
  4. Turn the left knee in toward the wall and then point it down, so the thigh lies against the wall and the left hip faces the wall [B41, B20].
  5. Straighten the right leg, pushing out on its hold with the inside edge. This drives your CoG back over the left foot and takes load off the arms [B40]. The feet push apart like a stem [B20].
  6. Pull in with RH, ideally on a sidepull (to your right, edge facing right), turn the chest toward it, and reach with LH. Lead the reach by rotating the chest [B20, B43].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Steep walls, about 30–75° overhanging [B20].
  - Two footholds wide apart at similar heights [B20].
  - The best foothold for the reaching-side foot is *behind* the line, so a backstep would spin out [B40, B39].
  - The holding hand is on a sidepull facing away from your body, an undercling, or a sloping, diagonal or vertical hold [B19].
  - There is an inward-facing foothold on the side opposite the gripping hand to press against [B19]. With RH gripping, this is a foothold on your left whose surface faces right, toward your midline; LF pushes it leftward, the way you would push a gaston.
  - Small holds, or moments when you are pumped [B20].

  It is not possible on an overhanging arête, or when a volume or roof lip is in the way [B20].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You attack front-on with hips square and backside out, and tire fast [B20].
  - A backstep keeps spinning you out [B39].
  - The foot skates off as you pivot [B20].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Dropping the wrong knee. Drop the knee on the same side as the reaching hand, even when the arms cross [B20].
  - Too little foot space for the pivot [B20].
  - A bent opposition leg, so the hips drift [B44].
  - Forcing a deep drop when tired [B33].
- **Injury note:**
  - A 2020 study (Lutter et al., *Am J Sports Med*, as reported by a physiotherapist on Climbing.com) attributed about 20% of climbing knee injuries to drop knees [B33].
  - The inward-rotated hip and deeply bent knee load the medial collateral ligament (MCL) [B33].
  - Treat pain near the inner knee as a stop signal, and take longer breaks if you are older, previously injured or tired [B33]. That advice applies to older and heavier climbers (judgement).
  - Gresham: avoid drop knees with an existing knee injury [B20].
- **Tall climber note:**
  - On very wide drop knees, let the knee pass inside the elbow so the limbs do not tangle [B20].
  - Long femurs make deep drops hard in tight spaces. A partial drop often gives most of the hip lock with less knee load (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 40° overhang.
  - *Holds and limbs:* RH on a sidepull at shoulder height to the right, edge facing right. LF on a foothold at waist height to the left, its surface facing right (inward), knee pointing down at the floor, thigh flat against the wall. RF on a foothold low and wide to the right, leg straight.
  - *Arrows:* outward from both feet; LH reaching up-left to the target.
  - *Body:* CoG marker close to the wall between the feet.
  - *Ghost:* the same holds climbed square-on, hips 35 cm out.
  - *Inset:* front view of the left knee showing the inward rotation, with an MCL warning icon.
- **Related:** Backstep; Twist lock; Stemming / bridging; Opposition.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B1, B10, B19, B20, B33, B39, B40, B41, B43, B44, B56, B64]

### Side flag (outside flag)
- **Also called:** side flag, outside flag (US usage, e.g. B59), counterbalance flag.
  - Naming conflict: in UK coaching (Gresham [B21]; *The Self-Coached Climber* [B10]), "outside flag" appears to mean the crossing-behind flag described under Back flag. That is my reading of their descriptions, **(unverified)**.
  - German: Flaggen, "Seitflagge" **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V2 (judgement). Flagging is taught as a steep-rock fundamental on the UK coaching pathway [B1], and Movement still lists it in its V5-plateau course [B63], so refinement goes on for years.
- **What it is:** The free leg is extended straight out to its own side (it does not cross the standing leg), with the inside edge or ball of the foot pressing on the wall. It works as a counterweight and an extra pushing contact.
- **How to do it** (LH holds above RF; RH reaches up-right):
  1. Identify the hinge: the line from your holding hand (LH) to your standing foot (RF). Reaching right will try to swing you open to the right [B26, B59].
  2. Your free foot (LF) is already on the far side of the hinge, so extend that leg straight out to the left [B26].
  3. Press the inside edge of LF into the wall, on blank wall if nothing else is there. Think of the whole wall as a foothold [B26, B57].
  4. Keep the flagging leg straight and pushing, not dangling [B26].
  5. Shift your hips toward the standing foot so your weight sits under the holding hand [B13].
  6. Reach with RH.
  7. Tune the flag: a flag foot that is too low or too close to the body gives little leverage. On overhangs, push it up and into the wall [B58].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The standing foot is roughly below the holding hand ("hand and opposite foot stacked roughly vertically") [B59].
  - The reach is toward the standing-foot side.
  - The free foot has no foothold where it would help.
  - Long or dynamic moves on steeper walls [B59].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You swing out to the right as RH lets go.
  - Your free leg hangs loose.
  - Your free foot sits on a good-looking hold that it is not actually loading. Hooper's term for this is "trapped by good feet" [B45].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - A loose, dangling flag leg [B26].
  - A flag foot that is too low or too close [B58].
  - Flagging on slabs, where it "generally doesn't work" [B26].
  - Stabbing at the hold before settling into balance [B26].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs give a longer counterweight lever, so a tall climber can flag farther out for more effect [B58].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical to 10° wall.
  - *Holds and limbs:* LH on an edge at head height, 20 cm left of centre. RF on a foothold directly below LH at knee height. LF leg extended straight to the left, inside edge pressing on blank wall at shin height. RH reaching up and right.
  - *Hinge:* dashed line from LH to RF; CoG marker sitting on the hinge.
  - *Ghost:* the same position without the flag, body rotated open to the right, with a curved arrow.
- **Related:** Back flag; Inside flag; Barn door; Opposition.
- **Watch:**
  - Movement for Climbers, "How to Flag - A Climbing Technique for Achieving Balance", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jLsii8s6acM
  - The Boardroom Climbing Ltd, "Flagging", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=juPtjVgcQbg
- **Sources:** [B1, B10, B13, B21, B26, B45, B57, B58, B59, B63]

### Back flag (rear flag)
- **Also called:** rear flag, back flag, back-flag.
  - Apparently the "outside flag" of UK coaching, where the passive leg swings underneath and behind the standing leg [B21, B10]. That reading is **(unverified)**.
  - Rowland's "reverse flag" is a hip-turned version [B42].
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3 (judgement). Same-side moves on steep V3s need it. Rowland says his hip-turned reverse flag "is rarely necessary below 5.9 or V4" [B44].
- **What it is:** With hand and foot on the same side, the free leg crosses *behind* the standing leg and presses on the wall on the other side. That puts the leg's weight across the hinge.
- **How to do it** (LH and LF on, moving right with RH, per B26):
  1. Stand on LF (inside edge or front point) under LH.
  2. Cross RF behind the left leg so the right foot ends up to the left of and below LF, the flag leg crossing the standing leg nearly at a right angle [B26].
  3. Keep the flag leg straight and engaged, pressing the foot into the wall [B26, B57].
  4. Move the hips toward the standing foot [B26].
  5. Keep the reaching-side hip (right) tucked against the wall [B59].
  6. Reach with RH.
  7. If the standing foothold is high (knee height or above): pull up with the hips roughly parallel to the wall and swing the passive leg underneath. Aim to find balance at the *top* of the pull, not before it, or you will lose it as you reach [B21].
- **Reverse flag variant (Rowland)** [B42]:
  - Front-step the same-side foot.
  - Cross the opposite leg behind it like a curtsy.
  - Turn the hips into the wall and tilt them toward vertical, so the opposite hip comes over the standing foot.
  - It costs more upper-body effort than a backstep or drop knee. Use it when all footholds are on the same side as the pulling hand and are too high or too small for a backstep, on roofs, or when a foot swap would be slow or low-percentage.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - All good holds are on one side of your body and you need to move toward the other side [B26, B59].
  - Hand and standing foot are roughly stacked on the same side, and there is no foothold for the other foot.
  - Common on overhangs [B26].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You barn-door open as soon as the reaching hand lets go.
  - You find yourself doing a foot swap just to avoid the door [B59].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Not bringing the flag leg far enough through [B21].
  - Finding balance before pulling up [B21].
  - Snatching for the hold before you are balanced [B21].
  - Letting the leg hang loose [B26].
  - Flag foot too low or too close to the body [B58].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs reach farther across the hinge, so the flag works with less hip shift (judgement, from B58's leverage point).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 20° overhang.
  - *Holds and limbs:* LH on a jug at head height left of centre. LF on a foothold directly below at knee height. RF crossed behind the left calf, foot pressed flat on the wall 40 cm to the left and lower. RH reaching up-right.
  - *Hinge:* dashed hinge LH–LF; CoG marker moved onto the hinge.
  - *Ghost:* the unflagged position, body rotated open with a curved arrow.
  - *Inset:* Rowland's reverse flag with hips turned in.
- **Related:** Side flag; Inside flag; Barn door; Backstep.
- **Watch:** Movement for Climbers, "How to Flag - A Climbing Technique for Achieving Balance", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jLsii8s6acM
- **Sources:** [B10, B21, B26, B42, B44, B57, B58, B59]

### Inside flag
- **Also called:** inside flag, inside-flag; German: none known.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V4 (judgement).
  - Gresham presents it as the steep-terrain answer to a single low foothold [B21].
  - Gripped calls it "rarely efficient but occasionally ideal" [B57].
  - Climbing House calls it less intuitive and often replaced by a side flag [B59].
- **What it is:** The free leg crosses *in front of* the standing leg, between it and the wall, and presses on the wall on the far side. Hips, chest and shoulders turn in a corkscrew, ending with all limbs straight.
- **How to do it** (LH on a low hold, LF below it; RH reaches far right):
  1. Stand front-on with LF on a low foothold (below knee height) on the same side as the holding hand, on the inside edge [B21].
  2. As you stand up, pass the right leg through in front of the left leg in one coordinated movement [B21].
  3. Let hips, chest and shoulders corkscrew, pulling one hip toward the wall [B21, B59].
  4. Finish with all limbs straight and reach with a nearly straight RH [B21].
  5. Keep hold of the lower handhold until the reach is done [B21].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A single low foothold and a long reach up and away from it [B21].
  - To avoid a foot swap [B59].

  Failure symptoms:
  - A back flag leaves you a hand short, because your hips stay low and out.
  - You keep swapping feet before every reach.

  The notes on *The Self-Coached Climber* say inside flags are harder on high feet [B10], so prefer a back flag when the standing foot is high.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Keeping the standing leg too bent, so you never stand up [B21].
  - Not flagging through far enough [B21].
  - Letting go of the lower handhold too early [B21].
- **Tall climber note:** On near-vertical walls there may not be room for a tall climber's thigh to pass between the standing leg and the wall. It works better on steeper walls (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical to 15° wall.
  - *Holds and limbs:* LH on an edge at waist height. LF on a low foothold directly below it. RF crossed in front of the left shin, foot pressing the wall 40 cm left. Body fully upright, all limbs straight, RH at full stretch up and right.
  - *Ghost:* the crouched start position before standing.
  - *Inset:* top-down view of the hips showing the corkscrew rotation arrow.
- **Related:** Side flag; Back flag; Reaching.
- **Watch:** Movement for Climbers, "(Climbing Analysis) Inside Flag - The Move You Never Do But Maybe Should", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LOkhYCi-IrE
- **Sources:** [B10, B21, B57, B59]

### Twist lock
- **Also called:** twist-lock; Rowland uses "twist-lock" as the umbrella term for backstep, drop knee and reverse flag [B44]; German: eindrehen **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V4 (judgement). This is the full combination of a lower-body twist and an upper-body turn for long static reaches on steep walls. Hörst's progression starts on boulders about 20° overhanging and moves steeper [B15].
- **What it is:**
  - Hörst: turn the reaching-side hip to the wall with a backstep and a complementary foot. Press the feet together while the holding arm pulls down and in to the torso, so the body locks and the free hand reaches with little effort [B15, B16].
  - Rowland adds the upper-body twist: the shoulder on the side of the twisted-in foot turns to the wall [B43].
- **How to do it** (RH holds, LH reaches):
  1. Set the lower body: an LF backstep if the foothold is on or in front of the line, or a drop knee if it is behind the line [B38, B40].
  2. Put RF on a complementary foothold to the right [B15].
  3. Start with the right arm straight and lean out on it while the hips twist in [B43].
  4. As your left hip bone nears the wall, start turning the left shoulder in. Bend the right arm as the shoulder turns, so the right elbow ends near your ribs and your chin is nearly tucked to the outside shoulder [B43]. Pull the right arm down and in toward the torso [B15].
  5. Squeeze the feet together [B15].
  6. Reach with LH, across your body if needed [B43].
  7. Make it one fluid movement; do not pause between the hip twist and the shoulder twist [B43].
  8. On flat holds or gastons, pull along the hold's long axis, not across it [B43].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Steep wall.
  - The target is high, in line with or beyond the hold you are pulling.
  - The holding hand is on a sidepull facing away from your body (for RH: on your right, edge facing right) or an undercling.
  - Front-on feet do not allow the reach.

  Failure symptoms:
  - You can touch the target but cannot hold the position to latch it.
  - You swing out as you let go.
  - You can only do the move as a full front-on lock-off.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Stopping between the lower and upper twist [B43].
  - Pulling across a flat hold [B43].
  - Not finding the exact locked position, so the lock feels insecure [B15, B16].
  - Letting the CoG drift off the wall [B16].
  - Defaulting to twist-locks everywhere [B44].
- **Tall climber note:** A long torso can rotate through a bigger arc, so the shoulder turn yields more reach (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 30° overhang, three frames.
  - *Frame 1:* RH on a sidepull (edge facing right), arm straight; LF on a backstep under the right hand; RF on a foothold right; hips half-turned.
  - *Frame 2:* left hip touching the wall.
  - *Frame 3:* left shoulder turned to the wall, right elbow bent against the ribs, LH at full extension on a target up and left.
  - *Arrows:* a rotation arrow around the vertical body axis, inward squeeze arrows at both feet, and a "pull down and in" arrow at RH.
- **Related:** Backstep; Drop knee; Side-on / turning the hip in; Lock-off; Reaching.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B15, B16, B38, B40, B43, B44]

### Barn door
- **Also called:** barn-dooring, swinging open, "the door"; German: "die Tür aufmachen" **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance (a failure mode, not a technique)
- **Becomes important at:** V1 for recognising and preventing it. Hörst teaches the left-right rule as a basic fundamental [B11], and Rowland teaches cross-body tension on slab first [B35]. Stopping it on steep V3+ needs flags (see those entries).
- **What it is:** Uncontrolled rotation of the body away from the wall, around the hinge line between two same-side contacts, when the CoG is off that line. Hörst calls it a "sideways rotation" that is hard to correct once it starts [B13, B11, B8].
- **How to prevent and fix it:**
  1. Before the move, name the hand that will stay and the foot that will carry you. If they are on the same side, expect a door.
  2. **Pair opposite limbs.** If you step up with RF, keep LH on and reach with RH [B35, B11]. Accept that during the move you hang from one hand and one foot, and let the lower foot go [B35].
  3. **Swap feet or step through** so the opposite foot is the one standing, often on its outside edge [B62].
  4. **Flag.** Put the free leg across the hinge with a back flag or inside flag, or straight out to its own side if it is already on the far side [B13, B26, B59]. Flag in the direction your body would swing toward if it opened [B59].
  5. **Add a pushing contact.** Press the off foot into the wall roughly at 90° to the line from your control hand to your main foot [B45], or put the other hand on a sidepull.
  6. **Move fast:** a quick pop or dyno before the rotation builds [B62].
  7. **Hold it with strength** on good holds only. It works, but costs energy [B62].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Same-side hand and foot are the only good holds.
  - Holds and feet run in a diagonal line up one side [B35].
  - Laybacks on arêtes [B54].
  - Sidepulls where the other hand must move.

  Failure symptoms:
  - When you let go with one hand, your body swings open toward that side like a door, and the free foot flies off.
  - On an arête you peel off sideways [B54].
  - If you are twisted in with cross-body tension and *still* rotate out, that is a spinout, not a barn door; see Backstep [B39].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Reaching before the flag is set.
  - A loose flag leg [B26].
  - Trying to stop a door that has already started, instead of setting up so it never starts [B13, B54].
- **Drill:** deliberately barn-door on a slab to learn the feeling, then practise cross-body tension there, where you will not get pumped [B35].
- **Tall climber note:** The turning force equals weight × offset, so a tall, heavy body opens faster for the same misplacement. Set the flag earlier (judgement, from 1.5).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical wall.
  - *Main figure:* LH on an edge at head height left; LF on a foothold directly below; RH just released from a hold to the right. The body rotating open around a dashed hinge line LH–LF, with a curved arrow showing the right side swinging away from the wall. CoG marker to the right of the hinge, with the horizontal gap labelled "lever arm".
  - *Insets, three small fixes:* (a) standing on RF instead; (b) RF back-flagged behind the left leg; (c) RF inside-flagged in front.
- **Related:** Side flag; Back flag; Inside flag; Weight shift; Layback.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, "The "Off Foot" is Climbing Technique #1 － Coaching w/ Dan #1", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TAjHSueeOkw
- **Sources:** [B8, B11, B13, B26, B35, B39, B45, B54, B59, B62]

### Lock-off
- **Also called:** locking off, static lock; German: blockieren **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V4 (judgement). It matters for static reaches off small holds when turning in is not possible. Baker says lock-off strength matters more on steeper, harder climbs [B62].
- **What it is:** Holding one arm bent with the shoulder pulled down while the other hand lets go and reaches statically, usually catching the next hold without swing.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Set your feet first, as high as is useful, and let the legs push you up to the lock position. Do not pull from a straight arm [B27].
  2. Bring the holding hand toward the shoulder; keeping it closer to the shoulder helps [B62].
  3. Keep that elbow down and in line with the hand and hold, not flared [B28].
  4. Pull the shoulder blade down and keep the body close to the wall. Catching a hold in a lock-off keeps you near the wall and reduces swing [B57].
  5. Reach smoothly and latch.
  6. Before committing, check whether a hip turn or drop knee could replace the lock. Gresham uses them precisely to avoid locking off [B23, B20].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The target is small or must be hit precisely: a crimp, a pocket, or a sloper you cannot catch with momentum.
  - Only front-on feet are available (two footholds directly below, no sidepull to twist off).
  - Moving between two downward-pulling edges (judgement).

  Failure symptoms:
  - You arrive at a poor hold with momentum and swing off; you needed a lock.
  - The lock is too strenuous to hold long enough to reach; you needed a twist lock instead.
  - Rowland notes a twist-lock lets you lock off "very low", extending static reach [B41].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Locking off without leg drive [B27].
  - Flared elbow [B28].
  - Locking where a twist would do [B23].
  - Getting stuck in tight, bound positions. Hooper notes locked-off positions "feel secure but limit your reach", and suggests generating from straight arms instead on overhangs [B47].
- **Tall climber note:** A tall climber can often reach from a half-lock where shorter climbers need a full one. Extra distance usually comes from rotating the shoulders rather than locking harder [B46].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* side profile, vertical wall.
  - *Holds and limbs:* RH on a small edge at chest height, elbow fully bent, hand beside the shoulder, elbow tucked to the ribs. Both feet high on footholds at knee height. LH reaching overhead at full stretch to a small crimp.
  - *Body:* CoG close to the wall.
  - *Arrows:* the legs pushing up, and the shoulder blade drawn down.
  - *Ghost:* a bent-arm lock with low feet and hips sagging out.
- **Related:** Twist lock; Straight arms; Reaching.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B20, B23, B27, B28, B41, B46, B47, B57, B62]

### Opposition / counter-pressure
- **Also called:** counter-pressure, opposing forces (BMC), push–pull; German: Gegendruck **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3 (judgement).
  - Hand–foot opposition (underclings, laybacks) appears from V2.
  - Hand–hand opposition (opposing sidepulls, gastons, compression) appears from V3–V4.
  - Hörst describes sidepull–outside-edge pairings as standard on "most moderate-to-advanced climbs" [B14].
  - The UK coaching pathway teaches "opposing forces techniques" at foundation level [B1].
- **What it is:** Making two contacts push or pull against each other so that holds which are useless on their own become usable. The sideways forces cancel and the resulting squeeze creates friction.
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Read each hold's direction.** You always pull opposite to the way the gripping surface faces [B70]:
     - A sidepull faces away from your body; you pull it toward your midline and lean away from it.
     - An undercling faces down; you pull it up with firm feet.
     - A gaston faces your midline; you pull it outward, elbow out.
  2. **Find the partner contact** whose force points the opposite way.
     - *Hand vs hand:* two sidepulls pulled inward toward your chest (left hold facing left, right hold facing right). Or two gastons at or above head height pulled outward (left hold facing right, right hold facing left) [B14].
     - *Hand vs foot:* an undercling pulled up and out while the feet press into the wall, with feet high, hips at about 90° and the arm straight [B30]. Or a sidepull with the outside edge of the opposite foot, hips turned [B14]. Or a gaston with the inside edge of the opposite foot [B14].
     - *Foot vs foot:* a stem [B29]; a drop knee, where the feet create sideways torque [B20]; a bicycle, with one toe on top of a hold and the other toe hooking under it [B56]; or a flag foot pressing the wall against the standing foot [B57].
  3. **Load both at the same time**, then move the limb that is not part of the pair.
  4. **Keep your core from sagging;** a sag releases the squeeze [B31].
  5. **With two sidepulls:** upgrade the foot opposite the better hold first, then release the other hand [B14].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Holds facing sideways or downward.
  - Pairs facing away from each other: squeeze them inward (opposing sidepulls, compression).
  - Pairs facing toward each other: push them apart (opposing gastons, stems).
  - No positive edges.
  - Volumes, corners and arêtes.
  - An undercling at the waist.

  Failure symptoms:
  - A sidepull slides out of your hand because no foot pushes the other way.
  - You cannot hold an undercling with low feet.
  - Your feet skate on smears because nothing presses them in [B30].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling straight down on a sidepull or undercling.
  - Low feet on underclings [B30].
  - One side of the pair relaxing.
  - Core sag [B31].
  - Opposing gastons on overhangs stress the shoulders [B14].
- **Tall climber note:** A long span lets you oppose holds that are far apart. The squeeze then has to travel through a long, bent body, so tension matters more (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* three small panels, all from behind.
  - *(a) Hand vs hand:* two vertical sidepulls 60 cm apart at chest height, both edges facing outward, with inward arrows from both hands.
  - *(b) Hand vs foot:* an undercling at waist height, feet high on smears. Arrow up and out at the hand, arrows into the wall at the feet.
  - *(c) Foot vs foot:* a stem in a 90° corner, with arrows from each foot pointing perpendicular into each wall.
- **Related:** Stemming / bridging; Layback; Compression; Drop knee; Body tension.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B1, B14, B20, B29, B30, B31, B56, B57, B70]

### Stemming / bridging
- **Also called:** bridging (UK), stemming (US), chimneying (when wide); German: spreizen **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V1 (judgement). Indoor corners, dihedrals and volume pairs appear on easy problems. The UK coaching pathway teaches bridging as a basic opposing-force technique [B1].
- **What it is:** Pushing outward with the feet, and often the hands, against two surfaces that face each other, so friction holds you without positive holds.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Place the feet on the two opposing surfaces (the walls of a corner, two volumes, or a volume and the wall), higher and wider than feels natural [B29].
  2. Push into each surface, roughly perpendicular to it, and trust the friction [B29].
  3. Keep the heels low for friction and to spare the calves [B29].
  4. With the hands, use holds or palm the walls with fingers pointing down or back, away from the corner. Mostly the hands balance [B29].
  5. Move one limb at a time with three anchored. Shift weight onto one foot to unweight the other, lift it, place it, repeat [B29].
  6. Variations: an X-shaped stance; backstepping one foot to press the hip into the wall [B29].
  7. To rest, stand fully on any ledge, or lean a shoulder into the wall [B29].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Any corner or dihedral.
  - Two volumes either side of the line.
  - An arête with a volume next to it.
  - A blank section between two features [B29, B65].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You are pulling hard on small holds in a corner with your feet on tiny edges.
  - Your calves cramp.
  - You keep slipping because your feet push down rather than out (judgement).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Relying on arm and finger strength [B29].
  - Pushing too hard instead of relaxing and trusting friction [B29].
  - Heels high [B29].
  - Staying in one stance when another would work [B29].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs bridge wide corners easily. In narrow corners the knees get in the way, so turn one foot into a backstep to fit (judgement, using B29's backstep variation).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, looking into an open 90° corner.
  - *Holds and limbs:* LF smearing on the left wall at knee height; RF on the right wall 20 cm higher. LH palming the left wall at chest height, fingers pointing down. RH reaching up.
  - *Arrows:* perpendicular from each foot and from the left palm into its wall.
  - *Body:* CoG centred between the feet.
- **Related:** Opposition; Layback; Drop knee; Square / frog position.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B1, B29, B65]

### Layback / lieback
- **Also called:** lieback (US), layback (UK), laybacking; German: piazen / Piaz-Technik **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V2 (judgement). Arêtes and lines of sidepulls appear at V2–V3, and the UK coaching pathway teaches laybacking with bridging [B1].
- **What it is:** Pulling sideways on a vertical edge (an arête, flake, crack edge or line of sidepulls) while leaning away from it, with the feet pushing the other way against the wall. 99Boulders likens the body to the figure "7" [B54].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Grip the edge with both hands, or one hand on it and one on the next sidepull, arms straight [B65, B54]. The edge works as a sidepull: it faces away from you, and you pull it toward you while hanging on the other side.
  2. Lean away so you hang off the edge [B54].
  3. Put your feet on the wall and push them sideways, against the direction your hands pull [B65].
  4. Hold core tension [B65].
  5. Find the balance point where you neither fall back nor swing; losing it causes a slow barn door [B54].
  6. Move hands and feet in short steps, keeping the push–pull balanced (judgement).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - An arête with nothing usable on the face.
  - A vertical edge with no footholds near it [B65].
  - A vertical line of sidepulls.
  - Pinch-shaped vertical features: Gresham notes turning side-on into a layback reduces the thumb strength needed [B24].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You swing off the arête like a door [B54].
  - Your feet skate; they are probably too low, so they push down rather than in (judgement).
  - Your arms explode; your feet may be too high, which loads the hands more (judgement).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Staying front-on to a sidepull or pinch with the hips parallel to the wall [B24].
  - Losing the balance point [B54].
  - Bending the arms (judgement; see Straight arms).
- **Tall climber note:** A long body makes the "7" long, and feet placed high close the angle quickly. Keep the feet a little lower than a shorter climber would (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind and slightly left. A vertical arête runs up the right side of the frame.
  - *Holds and limbs:* both hands wrapped on the arête edge at head and chest height, the gripping surface facing right (away from the climber), arms straight, pulling left toward the climber. Body leaning left, hips out. Both feet smearing on the face at knee to hip height, pushing right.
  - *Arrows:* the hands pulling left, the feet pushing right.
  - *Body:* the "7" shape outlined; CoG left of the arête.
  - *Ghost:* both limbs on one side opening like a door around the arête.
- **Related:** Opposition; Barn door; Stemming / bridging; Compression.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B1, B24, B54, B65]

### Sitting / sagging into a position (low hips, pre-loading)
- **Also called:** sitting back, sinking, loading the feet, pre-loading, setting up, "casting out" (Hooper) [B48].
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3 (judgement). It matters for moves on overhangs that need leg power, for slopers and for deadpoints [B47, B31].
- **What it is:** Lowering the hips, and letting them move slightly away from the wall, onto bent legs with straight arms. The feet are then loaded and pressing into the wall before the move, and you drive from that position.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Set your feet with the heels low [B47].
  2. Straighten your arms. Let the hips sit down and slightly out until the feet press firmly into the wall and you feel balanced. Hooper's balance point is "as far away from your bottom hand (or the moving hand) as possible" [B47, B48].
  3. Relax the arm that will move next [B48].
  4. Drive: extend the legs, move the hips in and up, and pull only for the last part. For dynamic moves, a small "auto-in" pull from the bottom hand starts the movement [B48, B47].
  5. On slopers, sit with the hips low and close to the wall and keep the core tight [B31].
  6. In general, lowering the hips relieves strain on the arms [B22].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Overhang with a big move ahead.
  - Slippery or poor footholds that blow when you pull in tight [B47].
  - Slopers [B31].
  - Before any deadpoint.

  Failure symptoms:
  - No power on overhangs [B47].
  - Your feet blow off or cut when you pull.
  - You start moving away from the wall before you reach the target [B48].
  - You stall in a lock-off.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Sitting low on slabs and vertical walls, which moves load from the feet to the fingers [B8].
  - Staying "sucked in" so the feet unload [B48].
  - Pausing too long before cruxes, so momentum is lost [B69].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs let you sit very deep, which can put your hips too far below and away from the next hold. Sit only as far as the feet need to press (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* side profile, 25° overhang, two frames.
  - *(1) "Tight":* hips pulled in, knees against the wall, feet barely weighted, with a small arrow showing a foot sliding off.
  - *(2) "Sat":* arms straight, hips down and back, heels low, with arrows at the toes pressing into the wall.
  - *Overlay:* a dashed trajectory arrow of the hips moving up and in toward the next hold.
- **Related:** Straight arms; Body tension; Lock-off.
- **Watch:**
  - Hooper's Beta, "Why You Have NO POWER On Overhangs － Coaching w/ Dan #3", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TZxhg6NMwaI
  - Hooper's Beta, "Why Do Climbers that "Stay Tight" Have Poor Body Tension? － Coaching w/ Dan #4", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EGdusH97XcY

### Hand crossing: cross-through, crossover, cross-under
- **Also called:** cross, cross-through [B54], crossover, cross move [B49], cross-under; German: überkreuzen **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V2 for cross-throughs on traverses; V4 for big crosses and cross-unders (judgement).
- **What it is:** Moving a hand past the other hand to a hold on the far side. Passing over the holding arm is a crossover or cross-through; passing beneath it is a cross-under.
- **How to do it** (LH holds, RH crosses to a hold left of LH):
  1. Make the holding hold one you can lean on; a sidepull or undercling is better than a flat edge (judgement). For LH this is a sidepull on your left with its edge facing left, so you can hang to its right while RH crosses.
  2. Place your off foot where it resists the rotation, roughly 90° to the line from the control hand to the main foot, and keep it loaded through the cross [B45].
  3. Choose how to rotate [B49]:
     - **Roll:** turn the torso perpendicular to the wall, so the chest is out of the way and the hips are sideways. This is the default on steep ground; it keeps the body line straight and weight over the feet, and limits barn-door swing.
     - **Swim:** rotate flat to the wall and narrow the shoulders. Use it on slabs, with wide hand positions, or when the feet are too poor to turn the hips. It gives more reach but strains the shoulder more.
  4. Look back toward the control hand as you move; this rolls the shoulders and adds reach [B46].
  5. Cross-under: when the target is level with or below the holding hand, or the holding arm is high and straight, pass RH beneath the left arm, usually with a hip turn **(unverified; no source describes the cross-under specifically)**.
  6. If you add a drop knee, drop the knee on the reaching-hand side even when the arms cross [B20].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The next hold is farther to the side than your current hand, on a traverse or a diagonal line [B54].
  - The current hold is too small to match.
  - The target faces the right way for the crossing hand.

  Failure symptoms:
  - Your chest or shoulder blocks the cross [B49].
  - You swing open as the reaching hand lets go.
  - The off foot slides at the moment you cross [B45].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Staying square, so the chest blocks [B49].
  - An unloaded or misplaced off foot [B45].
  - Crossing so far that the next move becomes impossible (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** Long arms cross far, but a broad-shouldered climber must "get narrow" (roll or swim) rather than rely on shoulder mobility [B49].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, 15° overhang traverse, two panels.
  - *(a) Roll:* LH on a sidepull at shoulder height, edge facing left. RF on its outside edge under LH; LF flagged left. Torso turned perpendicular, right hip at the wall. RH crossing over the left forearm to a hold 50 cm farther left.
  - *(b) Swim:* a slab version, chest flat to the wall, shoulders narrowed, RH crossing.
  - *Markings:* arrows show the torso rotation direction in each panel; the off-foot placement is circled.
- **Related:** Side-on / turning the hip in; Matching hands; Barn door; Reaching.
- **Watch:** Hooper's Beta, "Why Do Climbers Struggle with Cross Moves? － Coaching w/ Dan #5", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3Es610RvBI4
- **Sources:** [B20, B45, B46, B49, B54]

### Matching hands, and leaving room to match
- **Also called:** match, matching, hand match; the related hand swap appears as "hand swapping" in BMC FUNdamentals 1 [B2].
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V1 for matching on jugs. Leaving room on small holds matters from V3 (judgement).
- **What it is:** Bringing the second hand onto the hold the first hand is on, to free the first hand for the next move or to rest [B54].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Decide *before* you reach a hold whether you will match it (judgement).
  2. If you will, catch it on the part away from the incoming hand. A left hand arriving first on an edge that the right hand will join should take the left half **(unverified; coaching convention, no source found)**.
  3. On small holds, take the first catch with fewer fingers, or stack the incoming fingers over the first hand. Alternatively, swap hands by sliding the first hand out as the second slides in **(unverified for the techniques; FUNdamentals 1 lists "hand swapping" as a core skill [B2])**.
  4. Keep your hips under or beside the hold so the match does not need a big body shift; set a foot under the hold first if one is available (judgement).
  5. After matching, move your weight to the hand that stays before letting the other go (judgement).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The hold is big enough for two hands: a jug, rail, long edge or volume.
  - The next hold is on the same side as your current hand and crossing is awkward.
  - The next hold faces the wrong way for the crossing hand.
  - You need to rest or chalk [B54].

  Failure symptoms:
  - You arrive on a hold you planned to match and there is no room.
  - You end up crimping a corner.
  - You cannot free the first hand.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Catching in the middle of the hold.
  - Matching when a cross would save a move.
  - Matching with low feet and bent arms (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** Nothing specific.
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* close-up from the front of a horizontal edge 20 cm wide, three panels.
  - *(a) Wrong:* LH centred, no room.
  - *(b) Right:* LH on the left half, RH arriving on the right half.
  - *(c) Stacked match:* RH fingers placed over LH fingers on a small edge.
  - *Markings:* a small arrow shows the direction each hand comes from.
- **Related:** Hand crossing; Bumping / using intermediates.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B2, B54] (most how-to detail is judgement)

### Bumping / using intermediates
- **Also called:** bump, double move; "intermediates" are intermediate holds; German: Zwischengriff **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3 (judgement).
- **What it is:**
  - Bumping means moving the same hand twice in a row while the other hand stays: first to a poor intermediate hold, or a poorer part of the target hold, then on to the target **(unverified; definition from general usage, no source found)**.
  - Using intermediates means taking smaller holds between the big ones so you can move in short, controlled steps [B22].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Keep the holding arm straight and the hips set, turned in on steep ground (see Side-on).
  2. Go to the intermediate quickly and weight it only long enough to keep your balance (judgement).
  3. Continue straight to the target, without letting the intermediate take full weight for longer than needed (judgement).
  4. On a single big hold, catch the first part you can reach, then bump to its best part (judgement).
  5. When pumped, look again for "smaller, lower, closer holds" that let you build up in small steps rather than lunging for the biggest one [B22].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The target is out of static reach but a small hold sits on the way.
  - Crossing is impossible, or the target faces the wrong way for the other hand.
  - You must arrive at the target with a particular hand for the next move.

  Failure symptoms:
  - You are fully stretched and cannot reach.
  - You lunge for the biggest hold and arrive off balance [B22].
  - You reach the target with the wrong hand.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Hanging on the intermediate too long, so you pump.
  - Fixating on the biggest holds [B22].
  - Ignoring intermediate *footholds*, which are often what makes the hand intermediates usable (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** A tall climber can often skip intermediates. Skipping can leave you stretched, with your feet far below and no way to make the next move; a shorter climber's sequence with intermediates may be easier (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical wall, two-frame sequence.
  - *Frame 1:* LH on a jug at chest height; RH moving to a small crimp halfway to a distant jug up and right.
  - *Frame 2:* RH continuing from the crimp to the jug while LH and the feet stay put.
  - *Markings:* numbered arrows 1 and 2 on the RH path; the crimp shaded as "intermediate".
- **Related:** Matching hands; Reaching; Hand crossing.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B22] (definition and most how-to are judgement)

### Reaching: turning and extending the hip, "long body" positions
- **Also called:** long body, getting long, extension, stretching (*The Self-Coached Climber*) [B10].
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V3 (judgement), on moves set at the limit of static reach. Lattice's client data show ape index has "barely" any relationship with grade [B66], so how you use your length matters more than the length itself.
- **What it is:** Making the distance between the holding hand and the reaching hand as long as possible by choosing the right standing foot, turning the hip and shoulders, and straightening the limbs.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Step up before you reach [B22].
  2. Stand on the foot on the reaching-hand side, on its outside edge or in a drop knee, and turn that hip to the wall. The twist raises the reaching shoulder [B54, B19].
  3. Turn your shoulders so their line runs from the control (holding) hand toward the target. A cue: look back at your control hand as you move. Hooper says this rolls the shoulders, drives the knee forward and can add about a shoulder width of reach [B46].
  4. Keep the holding arm straight, or lock it very low in a twist lock [B41].
  5. An inside flag finishes with all limbs straight for a long reach from a low foot [B21].
  6. Rise onto the toe of the standing foot at the very end (judgement).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The target is at your limit.
  - The holding hand is a hold you can lean on.
  - There is a foothold on the reaching side for an outside edge.

  Failure symptoms:
  - You are a few centimetres short when square-on.
  - You bend the holding arm to gain reach.
  - Your shoulder drops as you reach [B46].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Reaching square-on.
  - Biasing the shoulders away from the control hand [B46].
  - Reaching before standing up [B22].
  - Lifting the heels off low feet [B22].
- **Tall climber note:**
  - Reach is a tall climber's advantage, but long static reaches can leave you strung out with no way to move the feet. Check the *next* move before taking the long option (judgement).
  - A climber writing on Climbing.com (most likely Carlo Traversi) says tall climbers still gain a lot from learning dynamic movement [B34].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind, vertical wall, two frames.
  - *(A) Square-on:* feet level on two footholds, RH holding at head height, LH stretching for a hold and falling 15 cm short.
  - *(B) Hip turned in:* LF on its outside edge, left hip at the wall, shoulders rolled toward RH, head turned toward RH, LH on the target.
  - *Marking:* a dimension line showing the reach gained.
- **Related:** Side-on / turning the hip in; Twist lock; Inside flag; Bumping.
- **Watch:**
  - Hooper's Beta, "Fix Your Shoulder Orientation － Coaching w/ Dan #2", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3uKDpIUJ_8o
  - Hooper's Beta, "Don't Blame Wingspan! These Mistakes Limit Your Reach", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bj25GdNX5B8
- **Sources:** [B10, B19, B21, B22, B34, B41, B46, B54, B66]

### Body tension (core) on steep ground
- **Also called:** tension, core tension, keeping your feet on; German: Körperspannung **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V4 (judgement).
  - Movement's course for "that V5 plateau" lists body tension [B63].
  - Hooper shows full-body tension already deciding a V3 cave [B52], so on caves and roofs it matters earlier.
- **What it is:** The chain of tension from the toes through the legs, trunk and shoulders to the fingers that keeps the feet loaded on a steep wall while a hand moves. Hooper argues it is a mix of technique, coordination and strength, and that core strength is rarely the limit [B51]. *The Self-Coached Climber* drills aim for "a line of tension from toe to upper spine" [B10].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Press the toes into the footholds and keep that pressure while moving. The hamstrings and adductors keep the foot pressing into the wall [B71].
  2. Keep the hips at the balance point, neither sagging nor jammed into the wall. A sagging core loses opposition [B31]; being too tight unloads the feet [B48].
  3. Engage the shoulder blades on catches [B50].
  4. Before a hand moves, check that both feet are loaded, then relax the arm that will move [B48].
  5. If a foot cuts, control the swing (Hooper's "scorpion": arch and curl the legs, control through the shoulders) and replace the foot quietly [B53]. MacLeod uses a quick hip flick to stop the legs swinging out during foot swaps on overhangs [B58].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Roofs, caves and overhangs steeper than about 30°.
  - Footholds far below the hands.
  - Small or outward-sloping footholds.
  - Toe and heel hooks.
  - Any move where releasing a hand makes the feet want to pop.

  Failure symptoms:
  - Your feet cut as you reach.
  - You swing after a foot cut [B53].
  - Your hips sag.
  - One foot keeps popping.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Treating it as a core-strength problem (planks) rather than a technique and coordination one [B51].
  - Being too tight to the wall [B48].
  - A loose free leg [B26].
- **Tall climber note:** A long body is a long lever between the hands and the feet, so a tall climber needs more tension to keep the feet on in a roof. Bringing the feet higher shortens the lever (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* side profile, 45° overhang.
  - *Holds and limbs:* both hands on two holds at the top; both feet on small footholds 1.4 m lower.
  - *Markings:* a highlighted line of tension from the toes along the hamstrings, glutes and trunk to the shoulders and fingers, and a small "toes pull in" arrow at each foot.
  - *Ghost:* the feet cutting, with a swing arc.
- **Related:** Sitting / sagging into a position; Opposition; Compression; Straight arms.
- **Watch:**
  - Hooper's Beta, "Why Do Climbers that "Stay Tight" Have Poor Body Tension? － Coaching w/ Dan #4", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EGdusH97XcY
  - Hooper's Beta, "Why Climbers Should Rethink Core Strength Training", https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jwyd40EBnlI
- **Sources:** [B10, B26, B31, B48, B50, B51, B52, B53, B58, B63, B71]

### Compression / squeezing
- **Also called:** compression, squeezing, hugging; the foot version is the "bicycle" [B56]; German: pressen **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Body positioning and balance
- **Becomes important at:** V5 (judgement). Movement's intermediate course for breaking "that V5 plateau" lists compression [B63]. Wide volume problems appear indoors earlier, at V3–V4.
- **What it is:** Holding yourself between two holds or surfaces that face away from each other (or two sides of one feature) by squeezing them toward each other; the squeeze creates the friction that holds you up.
- **How to do it** (the steps are judgement unless cited):
  1. Hands go on opposite sides of the feature or on two outward-facing holds, roughly at chest-to-head height.
  2. Squeeze inward with slightly bent arms, elbows pointing down and out, using chest and lats as if hugging **(unverified)**.
  3. Make the feet squeeze too, on opposite sides of a hold or feature: a toe on top with the other toe hooking under (bicycle) [B56], or toe and heel pressing inward. Pressing the off foot into the wall can turn round or smeary holds into clamps [B45].
  4. Keep the hips close to the feature and the core tight, so the squeeze in the hands and feet connects (see Body tension) [B31].
  5. Release one contact only when the other three are squeezing, and move it quickly [B69].
  6. On pinch-like features, turning side-on into a layback reduces thumb demand [B24].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Two holds or volumes facing away from each other.
  - An arête you can hold from both sides.
  - Big round volumes with no positive edges.
  - Double-sided features.

  Failure symptoms:
  - You slide down between the holds.
  - Your feet pop because only the hands squeeze.
  - Your arms burn because you pull down instead of in.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling down instead of squeezing in.
  - Passive feet.
  - Compression heel hooks without first activating the hamstrings; Gresham lists them among high-risk bouldering moves [B25].
- **Tall climber note:** Wide spans are more comfortable for a tall climber, and compression is often said to suit long limbs **(unverified)**. Lattice found little link between ape index and grade [B66].
- **Illustration brief:**
  - *View:* from behind.
  - *Holds and limbs:* two large volumes at chest height, 1 m apart, their sloping faces facing outward. Hands on the outer faces, elbows slightly bent and pointing down. LF toe pressing on the left side of a lower volume; RF heel pressing on its right side.
  - *Arrows:* inward from both hands and both feet.
  - *Body:* CoG centred and close to the wall.
  - *Ghost:* arms pulling straight down and the body sliding.
- **Related:** Opposition; Body tension; Layback.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [B24, B25, B31, B45, B56, B63, B66, B69]

## 4. Reading the wall: body-position decision rules (summary for the grade guides)

Judgement, synthesised from the entries above; each rule cites its main sources.

1. **Check the wall angle first.**
   - Slab or vertical: stay square, shift your weight over each foot, and stand tall [B17, B44, B8].
   - Overhanging 10° or more: turn a hip in, with a backstep or twist lock [B44, B15].
   - Steeper than about 30° with wide feet: drop knee [B20].
   - Roof: body tension and hooks [B20].
2. **Name the holding hand and the standing foot for the next move.**
   - Same side: expect a barn door. Swap feet, flag across the hinge, or add a pushing off-foot [B11, B35, B45, B59].
   - Diagonal: you are stable [B11].
3. **Read the holding hold's orientation.**
   - Downward-pulling edge or jug: front-on works.
   - **Sidepull** (edge facing away from your body, so you lean away from it): turn in the hip on the reaching side, which is the side opposite the holding hand, and backstep the reaching-side foot [B14, B38]. Example: RH on a right-facing sidepull means LF backsteps, the left hip turns in and LH reaches.
   - **Gaston** (edge facing your midline, pulled outward with the elbow out): inside edge of the opposite foot, stay squarer [B14]; avoid backsteps [B38].
   - Undercling: feet high, arm straight, hip turned [B30].
   - Sloper: hips low, arms straight on vertical, core tight [B31].
   - Two holds facing away from each other (left one facing left, right one facing right): squeeze inward, i.e. compression or opposing sidepulls [B14].
   - Two surfaces facing each other: push them apart, i.e. a stem or opposing gastons [B29, B14].
   - Vertical edge or arête: layback, or compression from both sides [B54, B24].
4. **Locate the footholds relative to "the line"** (the vertical line under the holding hand) [B38–B41].
   - On it, or beyond it toward the holding-hand side, at knee height or higher: backstep.
   - Behind it, with a second foothold wide: drop knee.
   - All footholds on the holding-hand side and too high or small to backstep: reverse flag or back flag [B42].
   - No foothold where the free foot would help: side flag or back flag [B26].
5. **Read the distance to the target.**
   - At your limit: turn the hip, roll the shoulders, straighten the limbs [B46, B19].
   - Beyond static reach with a small hold on the way: bump.
   - Hold too small to share: cross instead of matching.
6. **Read the symptom after a failed attempt.**

| Symptom | Likely missing technique |
|---|---|
| Swung open like a door as a hand let go | Opposite pairing, flag, off-foot press [B11, B13, B45] |
| Rotated out of a twist despite cross-body tension | Foot behind the line; use a drop knee [B39] |
| Pumped on easy steep ground, biceps burning | Straight arms, hip turned in [B22, B36] |
| Hand's width short on a reach | Hip turn and shoulder roll, drop knee [B19, B46] |
| Feet blow off when pulling hard | Sit back and load the feet; you are too tight [B47, B48] |
| Feet cut when a hand moves on a steep wall | Body tension, higher feet [B51, B71] |
| Hips feel "dragged off" on an overhang | Frog with wide feet, or turn side-on [B61, B22] |
| Slipping down between two volumes | Squeeze with the feet as well as the hands [B56, B45] |
| No room on the hold you meant to match | Leave room: take the far half of the hold **(unverified)** |

## 5. Gaps, doubts and disagreements

- **NICAS / NIBAS:** I could not find a public skill-by-level list. Technique-level grade evidence therefore rests on the BMC FUNdamentals syllabi, US gym curricula and coaches' angle and grade remarks [B1–B5, B44, B63, B64].
- **Drop knee grade:** the UK coaching pathway treats it as a fundamental [B1], while Touchstone teaches it to V5–V6 boulderers [B64].
- **Flag naming:** "Outside flag" means the side flag in US usage [B59] but appears to mean the crossing-behind flag in UK coaching [B21, B10]; the second reading is mine **(unverified)**. The A–Z index should cross-reference both.
- **Drop knee foot:** Gresham says the front point of the toe [B20]; Rowland says the outside edge [B40]. The "start on the toe, pivot onto the outside edge" version reconciles them [B41].
- **Straight arms:**
  - The coaching consensus favours straight arms [B12, B22, B36].
  - Hooper warns against passive hanging [B50], and Gresham against hanging fully straight [B23].
  - Lab data show that sinking onto straight arms *raises* finger load on slab and vertical walls [B8].
- **Hips to the wall:** right on steep walls [B28, B16], wrong on slabs [B17]. Elite climbers on a test wall kept their CoG farther out than novices [B9], and Hooper warns against being too tight [B48].
- **Thin areas:** matching technique, bumping and the cross-under have no strong written source; those entries are mostly judgement. Compression how-to is also mostly judgement.
- **Tall climbers:** the only data source is the Lattice ape-index analysis [B66]. All tall-climber advice in this dossier is judgement.
- **Inaccessible sources:** UKClimbing articles returned a paywall (HTTP 402). B23 rests on the search-result summary only.
- **Books:** *The Self-Coached Climber* is cited only through a reader's notes [B10]. I could not verify content from Kettle, Beal, MacLeod's book, Hörst's books or *The Climbing Bible*, so I do not cite them.
- **Videos:** found as embeds on pages I fetched, not as search results. Titles and channels were confirmed through YouTube's oEmbed endpoint. Their content is not verified beyond the accompanying write-ups.
