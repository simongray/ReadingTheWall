## 1. Tall climbers

### 1.1 What the evidence says about height and ape index

- **Definition.** Ape index is arm span minus height (e.g. +5 cm), or arm span divided by height (e.g. 1.03). A value of 0 cm / 1.00 means span equals height. US Army survey data put the typical male ratio at about 1.03–1.04, so a "neutral" man already has a slightly positive index [G6].
- **How much body shape explains.** In Mermier et al. (2000), 44 sport climbers from 5.6 to 5.13c: trainable variables (strength, endurance) explained 58.9% of performance variance; the anthropometric component (height, arm span, body mass, body fat) explained 0.3%; flexibility 1.8% [G3]. Laffaye et al. (2016), 41 novice-to-elite climbers: trainable variables explained 46%; anthropometry and muscle characteristics together under 4% [G4].
- **Lattice client data (2026).** "Thousands" of client records showed only a weak link between ape index and self-reported best grade. Within any one grade, the spread of ape index was much larger than the difference between grades [G1].
- **Where sources disagree.** Ozimek et al. found elite Polish boulderers differed from controls most in arm span and lean body mass, and were light with low fat mass [G5]. Magiera and Ryguła (2007) found ape index statistically significant for predicting performance (summarised in [G6], original not read). Small, homogeneous samples explain part of the disagreement: if all tested climbers have long arms, a study cannot detect the effect [G6]. Climbers at the top level include people with negative ape indexes, such as Brooke Raboutou and Lynn Hill [G6].
- **Elite height.** Adam Ondra puts the average competition climber at about 170 cm for men and 160 cm for women. In his view tall climbers gain reach but usually have less body and finger strength relative to their size [G2].
- **Bottom line for the book:** height changes *which* problems feel easy or hard. It does not predict grade. At V4–V5 technique and finger strength dominate [G1, G3, G4] **(judgement for the V-band)**.

**Practical measurement (judgement):** ape index matters less than *standing reach* and *hip mobility* together. Have the reader measure (1) span, fingertips to fingertips, arms horizontal with the back to a wall; (2) standing one-arm reach, flat feet, side to the wall. Use standing reach to judge whether a move is "a reach" for them, not the setter's intent.

### 1.2 Advantages indoors

- Reach past intermediates and skip holds; fewer moves need to be dynamic to span a gap [G2; search-result summary of forum posts, G7, G8].
- On beginner and lower-intermediate problems height mostly helps. Several forum posters report the advantage fades from around V3–V5, once problems become more technical. This is anecdotal [G7, G8; claim also in search-result summary] **(unverified as a general rule)**.
- More options for keeping a foot low while reaching high, which helps on vertical walls **(judgement)**.

### 1.3 Disadvantages indoors

- **Scrunchy, compressed moves.** When hand- and footholds are close together, a long femur and shin push the knees into the wall or the chest, and the hips get forced out from the wall. Forum climbers hypothesise that short climbers grade scrunchy problems easier and morpho (reachy) problems harder, and tall climbers the reverse [G7]. One tall poster reports that tension is harder to keep when fully extended [G8].
- **Low starts and sit starts.** Sit-start geometry crushes a long-legged climber: knees at the chin, back rounded, hips far from the wall **(judgement)**. One unverified blog claim says gym setting is "generally aimed" at about 165 cm, and that the most versatile height is 172–180 cm [G66] **(unverified: page blocked, search snippet only)**.
- **High feet with long femurs.** A foot at waist height needs deep hip flexion plus abduction and external rotation, with the knee fully flexed. Lutter et al. (2020) describe exactly this "high step" position as a knee-injury mechanism. Of 77 acute knee injuries, 20.8% came from high steps and 16.9% from drop knees, both loading the medial meniscus [G9]. Injured climbers in that study ranged from 154 to 194 cm, and height did not differ between mechanisms [G9].
- **Bigger levers.** A longer body puts the hips further from the hand- and footholds. On overhangs more torque goes through core and fingers, and when the feet cut there is more swing (reasoning from mechanics, not measured). Tall climbers are usually also heavier [G2].
- **Dynos.** A tall climber describes failing a dyno because he could not keep his bum close enough to the wall [G8].
- **Matching hand and foot.** Putting a foot where a hand just was is hard for tall climbers (search-result summary of forum and blog posts) **(unverified)**.
- **Spotting.** A large, heavy climber is harder to spot, and the size difference matters more as height grows [G10]. The DAV says spotters must consider weight differences [G11].

### 1.4 Concrete technique adaptations (mostly judgement; anchored where possible)

1. **Fold sideways, not forwards, on scrunchy moves.** Rotate the hip into the wall (drop knee or backstep), so the long thigh lies along the wall instead of pointing into it. Use the outside edge of the shoe. This keeps the hip close without the knee hitting the wall **(judgement)**. Caution: drop knees load the medial meniscus, so do not twist the knee under full load. Turn the whole hip, and keep the other foot solid. Lutter et al. suspect injuries happen when a hand is released or the opposite foot slips, adding load [G9].
2. **Skip the intermediate footholds.** If the next foot is at knee height, check whether a lower foot with a straighter leg works. Long legs let a tall climber stand on footholds a short climber has to pass **(judgement)**.
3. **Sit starts.** (a) Sit as close to the wall as the start holds allow, bum on the mat. (b) Place both feet *before* taking the hands. (c) Open the knees wide (hips externally rotated, "frog"), so the chest fits between the knees. (d) Or stagger the feet: one high, one low, so only one knee comes to the chest. (e) Lift off by pulling the hips toward the wall with the feet (toe pressure or a heel hook on the start foot), not by pulling up with the arms **(judgement)**.
4. **High steps.** Place the foot, turn the knee out over the toes, then move the hips over the foot. Push down with the hand on the side of the stepping foot (a mantle press) and drop the opposite shoulder toward the foot. Do not stand up from a loaded, fully flexed knee with a twist [G9 mechanism; technique cues are judgement].
5. **Avoid hugging the wall on big moves.** Hooper's Beta argues that staying pinned tight to the wall blocks rotation and makes the climber swing off when a hand lets go. The fix is to find the balance point (the "plumb line"), moving away from the hand that is about to move [G64]. This matters more with long levers **(judgement)**.
6. **Use reach statically on vertical walls; keep hips close on steep walls.** A tall climber can often lock off less and reach more, but on overhangs every centimetre the hips sag adds torque **(judgement)**.
7. **Learn from different body sizes.** Ondra recommends climbing with people of different heights, and not avoiding styles that do not suit you [G2]. Copy body *positions* from shorter climbers on scrunchy problems, not their sequences **(judgement)**.
8. **Seek out your weakness on purpose.** Short, compressed and low-start problems are the tall climber's "eliminate" practice [G2, G7] **(judgement on prescription)**.

### 1.5 Mobility needs

- **Which ranges matter:** hip external rotation with abduction (frog, turnout, high steps), hip flexion (high steps), hip internal rotation (drop knees), and hamstring length (heel hooks and high feet) **(judgement, consistent with the positions described in [G9])**.
- Lattice tells beginners that working on hip flexibility and activating the upper body improves movement [G26]. A DAV gym's beginner course ends its third session with hip flexibility and shoulder mobility [G56].
- 99Boulders' hip stretches: wide squat with hip circles; kneeling hip-flexor stretch; groin stretch on hands and knees with knees wide, pelvis pressed down (close to a frog stretch); pigeon (for high heel hooks); glute stretch against a wall. It credits hip flexibility with stepping further and higher [G63]. No studies cited.
- Mermier's flexibility component explained only 1.8% of variance [G3]. Flexibility is a style enabler, not a grade predictor.
- A mobility background such as yoga is an asset **(judgement)**.

## 2. Heavier climbers

### 2.1 Mechanics: why mass matters for fingers and feet (reasoning, not a cited study)

- The load on the fingers is the share of body weight the feet do not carry, plus any dynamic extra. On a vertical wall with good feet, most of the weight sits on the feet. On overhangs, and whenever a foot cuts, the hands take most or all of it.
- More mass means more force per finger and per foothold for the same position. It also means more momentum to stop after a dynamic move or a foot slip.
- The finger structures themselves adapt to load. In high-level climbers, palmar-plate thickness was associated with body weight at the 10-year follow-up [G25].
- **Implication:** a heavier climber's best lever is keeping weight on the feet. Every foot readjustment, slip or foot-cut costs them more finger load than it costs a lighter climber.

### 2.2 Why precise footwork matters more (practice cues)

1. **Load the best part of the foothold with the toe.** Beginners often stand on holds with the middle of the foot. The tip of the toe allows more freedom to pivot [G58].
2. **Silent, sticky feet.** Place each foot without sound and do not readjust it once placed. These are the 99Boulders "silent feet" and "sticky feet" drills, done 2–3 grades below max [G53].
3. **Keep the hips over the feet before moving a hand.** If the weight is not over the feet, the hands carry it **(judgement)**.
4. **Prefer static, controlled movement while finger tolerance is still developing.** Use dynamic moves when needed, but not as the default **(judgement)**. In a small study, a preference for dynamic climbing was associated with shoulder apprehension [G19].
5. **Use hooks to share load.** A toe hook or heel hook lets the leg carry part of the weight on steep terrain **(judgement)**. Note the knee risk of heel hooks in [G9].
6. **Downclimb rather than drop from the top.** Landing energy scales with mass times height. Falls from the top third were more severe than falls from the bottom third (OR 0.18 for severe injury from the bottom) [G14].

### 2.3 Injury considerations

- **Body weight and injury count.** In a Czech questionnaire study (n = 389), body weight/BMI had no significant effect on total injury count or severity [G21]. In a small Brazilian indoor bouldering study, mean BMI was 22.2, with no association with injury reported [G19].
- **Knees.** In Lutter et al., non-competitive climbers had more medial meniscal tears and needed more surgery. The authors speculate this may be linked to that group's higher age and body weight. This is a hypothesis, not a measured effect [G9].
- **Old fingerboard guidance.** Hörst (2011) listed being more than about 20 lb (9 kg) overweight as a reason to delay fingerboard training, together with under six months of climbing and ongoing pain [G34]. This is a dated expert rule of thumb, not evidence.
- **Spotting.** Spotters must account for weight differences [G10, G11].

### 2.4 Weight and grade: what the evidence says

- Body mass sits inside the anthropometric component that explained 0.3% (Mermier) or under 4% (Laffaye, with muscle characteristics) of the variance in climbing ability [G3, G4].
- At elite level, boulderers are light with low fat mass [G5]. The drive for a high strength-to-weight ratio is the stated reason climbers diet, and the route to low energy availability [G40].
- A Mountain Project poster cites Matt Fultz as a comparatively heavy climber who has done many hard boulders. Another argues that being lighter at the same strength is a clear advantage. Both are anecdotal [G7].
- **Synthesis (judgement):** at V3–V5, technique and finger strength limit progress far more than body mass. Losing weight is the slowest and riskiest way to gain a grade at this level. Better footwork lowers finger load immediately.

### 2.5 A sane approach to body composition (no crash-diet advice)

- **RED-S in climbing.** Joubert et al. (2022) surveyed 114 elite female competition climbers via the IFSC. 15.8% reported current amenorrhoea. 22.2% of the amenorrhoeic group reported a current eating disorder, and 17 of 114 respondents reported a current eating disorder or disordered eating. Of the amenorrhoeic group, 44–50% had restricted food (sources within the paper differ). BMI did not identify menstrual dysfunction: only 4 of the 18 amenorrhoeic climbers had a BMI under 18. The authors describe a climbing culture that values thinness and often normalises amenorrhoea [G40].
- **Policy.** The IFSC ran BMI screening at World Cup semi-finals, with a critical margin of 18.0 for women in 2021. The authors argue BMI should not be the only health parameter [G40]. A 2019 paper notes that an Austrian sport-climbing body bans competitors with low BMI [G41].
- **Recreational and youth climbers.** In a pilot of 22 adolescent climbers, 82% ate below their estimated energy target and 86% below their carbohydrate target. Protein was mostly adequate, a pattern the authors read as unintended high-protein energy restriction [G41]. A Swedish longitudinal study (CLIMB) is tracking disordered eating, bone health and RED-S indicators in elite and sub-elite climbers [G42] (search-result description only).
- **What the book should say (judgement, consistent with the sources above):**
  - Do not diet to climb harder. Climb better and the grade follows.
  - If body-fat loss is a personal health goal, make it slow, never cut food on climbing days, and keep carbohydrate and protein around sessions. Get advice from a doctor or registered dietitian, not from a climbing forum.
  - Warning signs that warrant medical help: food preoccupation, guilt about eating, falling energy, frequent illness, injuries that will not heal, loss of libido (or, for women, missed periods), and a mood that tracks the scale.
  - RED-S affects men as well as women. The IOC consensus says so, but I could not open it **(unverified)**.

## 3. Adult-onset climbers

### 3.1 Tendons and pulleys adapt more slowly than muscle

- Kjaer et al. (2009), in their review of human tendon loading, state that tendon tissue takes longer to adapt to chronic loading than the contractile elements of muscle [G23].
- Magnusson and Kjaer (2019) report human tendon collagen synthesis of only about 0.2–1% per day across studies. Adult human tendon is far less responsive than small-animal tendon. The core of adult tendon appears largely stable, formed by late adolescence, with a much smaller pool that turns over fast. After injury, the tendon's loading response is not normal again until some time after 6–12 months. Ageing brings only a limited decline in tendon cells' synthesis capacity, and in rats training counteracts several age-related changes (human data limited). Immobilisation reduces tendon stiffness within 1–2 weeks [G24].
- **Climbing-specific.** Fröhlich et al. (2021) re-scanned 31 high-level climbers 10 years after a baseline study. A2 and A4 pulley thickness and flexor-tendon thickness had kept increasing (p < 0.001), while palmar plates had not. They interpret this as mechano-adaptation of pulleys and tendons that continues over years of climbing [G25].
- Lattice tells beginners that tendons and ligaments may take five times longer than muscle to develop strength. That is a coaching heuristic, not a measured figure [G26].
- **Implication for the reader (judgement):** after a year, muscles and motor skill can already demand more than the pulleys have adapted to. The danger is a sudden spike in finger load: full crimps on small edges, dynamic catches, campus moves, hard sessions on consecutive days.

### 3.2 Common bouldering injuries: what the epidemiology shows

Two pictures emerge, depending on the data source.

**Overuse and upper body (surveys, clinics):**
- **Prospective, indoor climbers.** In van Middelkoop et al. (2015), 426 recreational indoor climbers were followed for one year. 42.4% had a climbing injury, a rate of 13 per 1000 climbing hours. Fingers accounted for 36.0% of injuries. Risk factors: higher age (OR 1.03 per year), previous injury (OR 3.05), campus-board use (OR 2.48), higher middle-finger strength (OR 1.12) and, oddly, doing a cool-down (OR 2.02) [G17].
- **Climbers aged 35 and over.** Lutter and Schöffl (2022), 198 patients with 275 injuries: most complaints were upper-extremity overuse. The top five diagnoses were finger pulley injuries, finger capsulitis, shoulder impingement, finger tenosynovitis, and shoulder SLAP tears. Age did not correlate significantly with overuse injury within that group [G18].
- **Small indoor bouldering cohort.** Mena et al. (2024), n = 35: 25.7% shoulder injury, 22.9% finger pulley lesion. A preference for dynamic movement was associated with shoulder apprehension [G19].
- **Bouldering in particular.** Josephsen et al. (2007), a one-year cohort of indoor and outdoor boulderers: finger and ankle injuries were most common. Outdoor bouldering raised finger risk, and traditional preventive measures were largely ineffective [G20]. Kovářová et al. (2024): bouldering injuries were mostly upper extremity (hands, fingers, wrists, elbows) plus ankles. Injury count rose with hours spent bouldering, levelling off above about 60 h per year [G21].
- World Cup data cited in [G21]: 1.47 injuries per 1000 h for bouldering vs 0.29 for rope climbing.

**Acute, lower body, from falls (emergency departments, gym accident statistics):**
- **Munich ED data.** In a retrospective study at a level-1 trauma centre (TUM), 430 patients had 447 acute bouldering injuries. The ankle was most affected (36.7%), then knee (16.8%), elbow (12.3%), spine (7.2%) and shoulder (6.3%). 61.1% were lower limb; 53.0% sprains, 22.8% fractures, 11.9% dislocations; 19.9% needed surgery. Finger injuries were rare in the ED [G15] **(figures from search-result abstract; journal and authors unverified)**.
- **DAV/KLEVER/VKÖ 2025.** Of 252 serious gym accidents, 174 (69%) were bouldering, and 155 of those (89%) were mat falls. Of the bouldering cases, 46% were ankle or leg rolls ("Umknicken") on landing and 27% were falls onto the arm. Children mostly injured arms, adults mostly legs [G12, G13].
- **Fall survey.** Beurienne et al. (2025), 245 injured boulderers: 67% lower-limb injuries, with ankle sprain the most common [G14].
- **US ED data.** Madsen et al. (2025): climbers aged 35–50 and over 50 were admitted to hospital more often than younger ones (19.7–20.2% vs 7.7–11.4%) [G22]. Data from all climbing, not bouldering only.
- **Knees.** Lutter et al. (2020): 46.8% of acute knee injuries came from indoor bouldering. Mechanisms: heel hook 40.3%, fall 22.1%, high step 20.8%, drop knee 16.9%. All ACL tears came from uncontrolled falls [G9].

**Why the two pictures differ:** finger and shoulder problems are mostly treated outside emergency departments, while ankle and knee trauma from falls lands in the ED. The book should give the reader both: protect fingers and shoulders from overload, and protect ankles and knees by falling well (section 5).

### 3.3 Warm-up guidance

**Evidence (thin):**
- No study I found shows that a warm-up reduces bouldering injuries. Among Lutter's knee-injury patients, having a warm-up routine did not differ between injury mechanisms [G9].
- A small Korean study (9 sport climbers) found that foam rolling, pre-exhaustion and static stretching did not change strength, endurance or climbing performance. The authors call for warm-ups built around climbing postures [G39] **(abstract via search result only)**.
- Warm-up guidance is therefore consensus practice from federations and coaches [G10, G11, G36, G37, G38].

**Consensus content:**
- The DAV's first indoor-bouldering rule is to warm up thoroughly to protect muscles, ligaments and tendons, in suitable areas, not under other climbers [G11]. Petzl: raise muscle and body temperature to prepare joints, muscles and tendons [G10].
- 99Boulders: 5–15 min pulse raiser; dynamic (not static) mobility; at least 20–30 min of progressively harder easy problems that rehearse the session's movements, with precise footwork. It notes that static stretching before exercise reduces strength [G37].
- VDiff: warm up on big holds before small ones, and warm up again after any break longer than about 15 minutes [G38].
- For climbers over 40, Climbing.com (with a physical therapist): 5–10 min cardio, about 5 min dynamic stretching, gradually harder easy problems, plus shoulder circles and finger/wrist tendon glides [G36].
- Hooper's Beta: submaximal finger "recruitment pulls" on a 20 mm edge *before* climbing, ramping effort over 5–8 seconds, as a warm-up [G32].
- Outdoor-Magazin: include a few jumps and landings in the warm-up to "wake up" the ankles [G57].

**Suggested 25–30 min protocol for the book (judgement, built from the above):**
1. 5 min easy cardio (rowing, skipping, brisk walking up stairs).
2. 5 min dynamic mobility: arm circles, shoulder pass-throughs with a band, hip circles, deep squat with hip rotations, leg swings.
3. 3–5 easy jumps from standing onto the mat, landing and rolling (section 5).
4. 10–15 min on problems 3–4 grades below max: jugs first, then smaller holds, steep only at the end. Silent feet on every problem.
5. 2–3 problems one grade below the session's target, rehearsing its moves (crimps, heel hooks, dynamic catches).
6. Warm up again after any break over 15 minutes.

### 3.4 When finger-strength training makes sense (about one year in)

**Traditional view (wait, build up):**
- Hörst (2011, via Nicros): the fingerboard is not for a true novice. Start after more than six months of climbing, with no finger, elbow or shoulder pain and no more than about 20 lb overweight. Begin with continuous "moving hangs", no more than three days a week [G34].
- Climbing.com (2021): it makes more sense to climb a year or two first. Novices can deadhang at low loads, but beginners should not add weight. Use a bigger edge to reduce load [G30].
- Lattice beginner guide (2022): skill is the highest priority. Before hangboarding, ask whether you have climbed for over six months, then test a 7-second bodyweight hang on a 20 mm edge. Favour long, light hangs over maximal ones [G26].

**Newer view (low-intensity loading is fine earlier):**
- Gilmore et al. (2024), a retrospective study of 526 Crimpd app users: low-intensity, feet-on-the-floor hangs ("Abrahangs": about 10 min, 10 s holds, cued to a small forearm strain, 18–22 mm edge) gave about +2.5% strength-to-weight (a non-significant trend). Max hangs gave about +3.2%; both together about +5.8%, apparently additive. Climbing alone gave no change. Limits: not randomised, almost all men, mostly "advanced" climbers, training history unknown. The authors note high-intensity hangs may carry overload risk for novices with little loading history [G28].
- Devise et al. (2022), randomised, advanced climbers: hangs at 60% of max gave smaller strength gains than 80–100%, but the authors suggest 60% for climbers worried about injury or returning from a layoff [G29].
- Lattice (Torr, 2026): a fan of Abrahangs, but complete beginners should first learn to fingerboard safely. Always keep at least one day a week with no tendon loading [G27].
- Dave MacLeod (BMC Q&A): grade is irrelevant; circumstances decide. Fingerboarding can suit even relative novices who cannot climb often. Overuse and poor form are the bigger worries [G31].
- Hooper's Beta: start strength work once a week [G32]. Block pulls ("no hangs") are relatively gentle on the fingers and likely best for beginners [G33] **(search-result snippet only)**.

**Where sources disagree:** thresholds range from six months [G26, G34] to one or two years [G30] before hangboarding. The newer camp treats *low-intensity* loading as a separate category that can start earlier [G27, G28, G31]. Nobody in these sources recommends max hangs with added weight or campus boarding for a one-year climber. Campus-board use carried OR 2.48 for injury in van Middelkoop [G17].

### 3.5 Recovery and rest with age

- Tom Randall (Lattice), writing about training past 40: accept that some sessions need more recovery. He cut back on double-session days and moved from "2 on, 1 off" to "1 on, 1 off" or "2 on, 2 off" [G35].
- Climbing.com's plan for over-40s schedules two rest days a week and active-rest cardio (hiking, cycling) [G36].
- Tendons respond slowly to loading [G24], so pain that shows up the day *after* a session is the signal to heed **(judgement)**.
- Previous injury roughly triples injury odds (OR 3.05) [G17], so the first injury is worth avoiding. Return gradually after one.
- Tendon stiffness drops within 1–2 weeks of immobilisation [G24]. After a holiday or illness, restart with easier sessions rather than picking up where you left off **(judgement)**.
- On hard attempts within a session, rest a little longer than you think you need [G26].
- **Suggested default (judgement):** 3 bouldering sessions a week, never more than 2 days in a row. One of the three should be a lower-intensity "technique volume" session (section 4).

## 4. Learning technique efficiently

### 4.1 What skilled climbing looks like (so the reader knows what to practise toward)

- Orth, Davids and Seifert (2016) reviewed 42 studies. Skilled coordination means few prolonged pauses, a relatively simple path, and smooth transitions between moves. It rests on better perception of climbing opportunities (which hold offers what), optimised body-to-wall coordination, and *less* exploratory touching of holds. Elite climbers are better at seeing opportunities both from the ground and on the wall [G43].
- Research measures fluency three ways: **jerk** (smoothness of hip motion; it relates to the number of sub-movements [G45, G46]), **geometric entropy** (how convoluted the hip path is [G45]) and **immobility time** (long pauses) [G45].
- **Practical translation (judgement):** the reader can self-check the same three things on video: (1) how often the hips jerk or stall, (2) whether the hip path is direct or zig-zags, (3) how many seconds pass motionless before each move.

### 4.2 Deliberate practice

- Ericsson et al. (1993) defined deliberate practice as practice with a clear goal, immediate feedback, repeatable tasks and progressively harder levels, usually designed by a teacher [G50].
- In a sports-only meta-analysis, deliberate practice explained about 18% of performance variance overall, and only about 1% among elite performers. It is necessary but not sufficient [G50].
- Orth et al. (2016): practice gains depend on task novelty and on route difficulty *relative to the climber's ability* [G43].
- **Applied (judgement):** each session gets 1–2 named technique goals ("today: backsteps on the 45°"). Pick problems where the goal technique is needed. Get feedback from video or a partner. Repeat until it is smooth.

### 4.3 Variable practice and exploring different beta

- Seifert et al. (2015): 9 intermediate climbers practised three routes with identical hold positions but different hold orientations. Holds that could be gripped two ways ("double-edge") induced exploration while offering a fall-back to a known grip. Over four sessions, exploratory touches fell from 0.4 to 0.1 per hold and ascent time from 116 to 93 s. A new transfer route raised exploration again (1.7) [G44].
- Orth et al. (2018), 7 beginners, 42 trials over 7 weeks: climbers who could already vary body orientation (face-on to side-on) improved gradually. Those locked face-on improved *suddenly*, after a period of exploration, by discovering side-on positions. The authors recommend holds that invite side-on positions, safe exploration with a fall-back option, and fluency feedback [G45].
- General motor-learning work on contextual interference: varied practice can depress performance during practice but improves retention and transfer. Evidence is mixed across studies [G51].
- Pijpers et al. (2006): anxiety narrows how climbers perceive and use action possibilities [G49]. Orth et al. cite it to say that feeling unsafe restricts exploration [G45]. **Implication:** practise new movements low down and on easy terrain.
- **Drills (judgement, grounded in [G44, G45]):**
  - Re-climb a sent problem three ways: face-on, side-on (backsteps or drop knees), and with different feet.
  - Climb it with "no foot readjustment" (sticky feet) [G53].
  - Swap one hold for an "eliminate": forbid a key foot, or hand-only-on-volumes.
  - Rotate wall angles and hold types every session. Lattice tells beginners to climb a wide range of styles [G26].

### 4.4 Focus of attention: body cues vs external cues

- General motor-learning research (Wulf's 2013 review) found that cues directing attention to the *effect* of a movement on the environment (an external focus) usually beat cues about body parts (an internal focus), for both learning and performance [G52] **(unverified: abstract not accessible; DOI resolves)**. I found no climbing-specific attentional-focus study before the search budget ran out.
- **Reconciling with the book's style (judgement):** the brief rightly asks for body-part instructions. Use them to *understand and set up* a position on the ground or while reading. When actually executing, give one external cue:
  - "push the foothold down and back into the wall" rather than "straighten your knee";
  - "turn your belly button toward the left hold" rather than "rotate your hips";
  - "keep the shoe rubber pressed on the volume";
  - "pull the hold toward your hip".
- The handbook's technique entries could each carry one external "execution cue" alongside the body-part steps **(suggestion)**.

### 4.5 Video self-analysis and feedback

- Orth et al. (2018) gave learners feedback on jerk, entropy, immobility and their climbed trajectory after sessions, alongside the instruction to climb "as fluently as possible" [G45].
- Motor-learning work on feedback (the guidance hypothesis): too much augmented feedback during practice creates dependency. It boosts practice performance but harms transfer. Reducing feedback frequency helps learning [G51].
- I found no climbing-specific study on phone-video self-analysis **(gap)**.
- **Applied (judgement):**
  - Film the attempt from behind and about 3–4 m back, or side-on for steep walls.
  - Watch it after 2–3 attempts, not after every one.
  - Check one thing per viewing: (1) hips over feet before each hand move; (2) count of foot readjustments; (3) pauses over 2 s; (4) any barn-door swing or foot cut; (5) audible foot placements.
  - Compare with a stronger climber's video of the same problem for *positions*, not sequence.

### 4.6 Flash vs repeat; reading before climbing

- **Preview.** Sanchez et al. (2012) studied pre-ascent route inspection in indoor sport climbing. Seifert et al. cite it as showing that visual inspection is key to performance, and allowed 3 minutes of preview before each ascent [G48, G44]. Orth et al. (2016): elite climbers detect climbing opportunities better from the ground [G43].
- **Repeating builds fluency on that problem.** Exploration and time fall with repeats [G44]. Novelty drives exploration and transfer [G43, G44].
- **Applied (judgement):** each session has both parts.
  - **Flash block:** 3–5 new problems at or just below the flash grade. Read each for 1–2 min first: name every hand and foot move, decide where the hips will be, decide where you might fall. Then climb and compare with the plan. A DAV gym's beginner course runs exactly this "discuss first, then climb and compare" exercise [G56], and Petzl lists visualising the moves as a pre-climb step [G10].
  - **Repeat block:** 2–3 recent sends, repeated for smoothness (fewer pauses, no readjustments, silent feet).

### 4.7 Downclimbing as practice

- Downclimbing protects tendons, ligaments and joints, and trains hand-foot coordination because the moves run in reverse [G55].
- 99Boulders' downclimbing drill: four problems 2–3 grades below max, each climbed twice and downclimbed, without extra holds where possible [G53].
- Safety bodies say to downclimb rather than jump [G11, G12, G62].

### 4.8 Climbing below max for volume

- Lattice argues that low-intensity continuous climbing (ARC) teaches technique and stops climbers over-gripping. It says the benefit is mainly for route climbers but can also help boulderers. The evidence it cites is about forearm oxygenation, not technique [G54].
- 99Boulders runs all its footwork drills 2–3 grades below max [G53].
- Practice effects depend on difficulty relative to ability [G43], and anxiety blocks exploration [G49].
- **Applied (judgement):** one session a week where the reader climbs 25–40 problems two to three grades below max, each with a single technique focus.

### 4.10 Learning from watching others

- Boschker and Bakker (2002): inexperienced sport climbers may perceive and use new action opportunities just by observing a model. Seifert et al. cite it on dual grasping and arm crossing [G47, G44] **(original abstract not accessible)**.
- Ondra: climbing with people of different heights gives useful insight [G2].
- **Applied (judgement):** watch for *where the hips go* and *which way the climber faces* before each hand move, not just the hold sequence. A tall climber should study both another tall climber (reach solutions) and a short one (compression and high-foot solutions).

## 5. Falling and landing safely indoors

### 5.1 Why this is a core skill

- **DAV/KLEVER/VKÖ 2025:** 89% of serious bouldering accidents were mat falls. 46% were ankle or leg rolls on landing ("Umknicken"), 27% were falls onto the arm, and 17% happened at or near the top hold. The DAV says downclimbing, correct falling and landing, and spotting must be trained, adapted to age [G12, G13].
- **Beurienne et al. (2025, 245 injured boulderers):**
  - 85% of falls were involuntary: foot slips 29%, dynos 22%, missed holds 21%.
  - 62% of falls involved rotation, and 74% ended feet-first.
  - 47% of injuries came from the top third of the wall; falls from the bottom third carried much lower odds of severe injury (OR 0.18).
  - 94% of injured climbers landed on the pads: the mats were there, the landing went wrong. Only 3% landed between pad and wall and 2% between two pads.
  - Voluntary falls were less severe [G14].
- **Lutter et al. (2020):** all ACL tears in their series came from uncontrolled falls. Fall injuries were concentrated in less experienced climbers [G9].
- **Schöffl et al. (2013):** over 515,337 gym visits, serious injuries were rare, and most bouldering injuries came from falls onto the mat [G16] **(via secondary summaries; original not read)**.

### 5.2 How to land (consensus, with disagreements)

1. **Look down as you come off.** Find the landing; the body follows the eyes [G59].
2. **Push slightly away from the wall** so you do not scrape down it or catch a foot on a hold [G10].
3. **Land on both feet at once, knees bent and springy.** Sources disagree on stance: the DAV says feet as close together as possible [G11]; Bergzeit says shoulder-width [G55]; Outdoor-Magazin and Advnture say wide [G57, G60]. **(Judgement: about hip-width.** Feet together reduces the risk of one foot landing on a hold-shaped bump or a mat seam; a slightly wider stance resists sideways tipping. Both sources agree on two feet at once.) Landing on one leg is a listed mistake [G57].
4. **Keep absorbing: squat, sit back onto the bum, then roll backwards onto a rounded back** [G59, G60, G58]. Petzl: break the fall with legs, then bum, then back, rolling and tucked [G10].
5. **Arms bent and tucked in front of the chest; chin tucked; neck muscles tense ("Babyboxer").** Bergzeit describes the Babyboxer as arms bent close to the body, with the neck muscles holding the head off the mat [G55]. Beurienne et al. recommend falling with arms crossed in front and rolling onto the back [G14].
6. **Never post the hands behind you or land on straight arms.** Several sources say this outright [G10, G57, G58, G60]. Beurienne et al. suspect upper-limb injuries come from climbers instinctively bracing with their arms after leaning-back starts [G14]. The DAV statistics confirm that falls onto the arm are a major category [G12].
   - **Disagreement:** VDiff says to let the hands and arms absorb the rest of the impact with elbows bent [G38]. Petzl allows a judo-style arm slap [G10]. The majority view, and the arm-injury data, favour tucking the arms [G10, G12, G14, G55, G57, G58].
7. **Body tension: neither limp nor rigid.** Limp risks whiplash; rigid risks tendon and ligament damage [G60].
8. **Falling off the back on roofs or steep walls:** let go cleanly rather than grabbing at holds. Keep limbs in, tuck the chin, land on the back or bum and roll [G60]. Before committing to a heel hook, ask whether you could release it in time if a hand slipped [G38].

(2) Both feet touching the mat about hip-width, knees bent about 90°, arms bent and crossed in front of the chest, chin tucked; centre-of-gravity dot above the heels. (3) Bum touching the mat just behind the heels, back rounding. (4) Rolling onto the upper back, knees toward the chest, arms still in front, head off the mat. ### 5.3 Downclimbing vs jumping

- The DAV's fourth rule: downclimbing comes before jumping. Use the marked descent holds, learn to jump down and land, and land with feet together, rolling if necessary [G11]. The BMC's indoor poster messages are "Climb down" and "Do not walk under other climbers" [G62]. Petzl says that indoors, downclimbing on descent holds is preferable [G10].
- Every jump loads tendons, ligaments and joints; downclimb whenever you can, using any holds [G55]. Before starting, check that descent holds exist or that a jump would be safe [G55]. Only climb as high as you are prepared to fall [G55].
- The DAV statistics led to two recommendations: setters should avoid hard-to-control cruxes (especially dynamic sideways moves) at maximum height, and climbers should downclimb rather than jump [G13]. Lutter et al. advise downclimbing or an easy top-out with a previously injured knee [G9].
- **Applied (judgement, for a tall climber):** downclimb until the feet are about 1 m above the mat, then drop with the landing technique above. A tall climber's centre of mass starts higher for the same foothold, so each jump is a bigger drop.

### 5.4 Mats, gaps and the fall zone

- **Clear the fall zone.** No bottles, brushes, chalk bags or people on the mat under a climber. Do not boulder too close beside or above others. Judge the landing zone generously [G11, G38, G10].
- **Mat edges, seams and the wall gap.** Avoid landing on mat edges or between pads, which can twist an ankle. Check pad position after every fall [G38]. In Beurienne's sample only 3% landed between pad and wall and 2% between pads [G14]. Most injuries happen on good matting through poor landings, but the base of the wall and mat seams are where an ankle rolls **(judgement)**.
- **Collisions.** Hitting the wall accounted for 3% of DAV bouldering accidents, in 3 cases from landing on or colliding with a volume during a dynamic move [G12]. Spot volumes and big holds below a dyno before trying it **(judgement)**.

### 5.5 Spotting indoors

- **Purpose:** spotting guides a falling climber so they land in a good position, mainly protecting the head and upper back. It is not for catching or stopping the fall [G10, G38, G61].
- **Technique:**
  - Stand with feet staggered and knees soft, in a dynamic stance.
  - Arms bent, hands at hip level, aiming at the climber's pelvis and upper back [G10].
  - Tuck the thumbs in to avoid dislocating them [G38].
  - Use multiple spotters on traverses, overhangs and big heights [G10].
- **Indoor debate:** the Climbing Wall Association compilation says modern bouldering floors make systematic spotting generally unnecessary. Spotting does not prevent the common ankle and wrist injuries, and untrained spotters can cause harm or be hurt. It still helps in low, steep caves and on moves with inversion risk (roofs, heel and toe hooks). The most popular expert recommendation was teaching falling technique instead [G61]. The DAV: learn to spot properly, spot only when needed, and mind weight differences [G11]. Josephsen et al. found spotting associated with few injuries [G20].
- **Tall/heavy note:** a tall, heavier climber is hard for a smaller partner to redirect. The greater the height, the more a size difference is felt [G10]. It is usually better to rely on falling technique and choose problems accordingly **(judgement)**.

### 5.6 How to practise falling (progression)

1. On the mat, from standing: tip backwards into the Babyboxer (arms bent in, chin tucked) and roll [G55].
2. Jump up, land on both feet, tip back and roll [G55].
3. Partner drill: face each other holding hands, knees bent, lean back against each other's pull, then let go and land in the Babyboxer [G55].
4. On the wall: drop off from low holds, then gradually higher. Slightly overhanging walls are fine; roofs are not [G55].
5. Practise falls regularly so the right reflexes become automatic; include a few landings in every warm-up [G57].
6. Also practise rolling sideways and backwards from sitting and squatting, arms bent in front [G67] **(search snippet only; the page returned 404, unverified)**.

A DAV gym's beginner course teaches a "falling school" (Babyboxer and rolling) in its first session and repeats it in every session [G56]. The CWA experts suggest a mandatory falling orientation for new boulderers, akin to a belay test [G61]. A video, "The Art of Falling" (Futurist Climbing and Veracity Insurance), is used by some US gyms for falling orientation [G65] **(seen only in search snippets)**.

### 5.7 Recognising a dangerous fall before it happens (reading the wall)

- Hard moves at the top of the wall: 17% of serious DAV bouldering accidents happened at or near the top hold [G12]. Falls from the top third were more severe [G14].
- Heel hooks above the head or roof moves: will the heel release if the hand slips [G38]?
- Dynos and sideways jumps: they cause twisting falls [G38, G14], and the DAV calls dynamic sideways cruxes at maximum height a setting hazard [G13].
- Volumes below the crux are collision hazards [G12].
- **Failure symptom (judgement):** if you have already landed with a "stutter step", a sideways tip or a hand reaching back, practise the roll before trying that move again.
