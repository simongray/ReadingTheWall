# Hands: a drawing reference

This page is the reference for the hand generator and for anyone checking its
drawings. It has four parts:

1. What photographs of real hands on holds show, grip by grip, with joint
   angles read off each photo. The half crimp and full crimp come first.
2. Anatomy numbers for a skeleton with soft tissue.
3. How artists build a hand and keep it the same from drawing to drawing.
4. The rules that follow for the code.

Sizes are in cm of the model hand, 19 cm from the wrist crease to the tip of
the middle finger, as in `hand-proportions.md`. That page's ratios still hold,
and this page doesn't repeat them.

The photos were looked at, not copied: none is in the repository. Where only a
Bing thumbnail could be fetched, its link is given next to the original's.
Two images were rejected because they look generated (impossible fingers): the
LEDGE "close-up of a climber's hand gripping a small crimp" and the Dreamstime
jug.

## How to read the angle tables

- **Joint angles** are flexion from straight, in degrees. A negative angle is
  bent back (hyperextended). MCP is the base knuckle, PIP the middle joint and
  DIP the end joint, all of the index unless a row says otherwise.
- **Back to wall** is the angle between the wall and the line along the back of
  the hand from the wrist to the knuckles. Positive means the knuckles lean in
  toward the wall. Only true side views give it; oblique views say so.
- **Wall** gives the side the wall is on in the photo. The book draws the wall
  on the right, so a photo with the wall on the left is a mirror image of the
  book's view.
- The angles are read by eye from the photos and are good to about ±10°. Where
  a joint is hidden, the table says so rather than guessing.

**The half crimp pose from these photos.** With the wall on the right:

- The back of the hand stands upright, within 10° of the wall's line.
- The index's first segment carries on up the line of the back of the hand,
  with the knuckle bent only 5–25°.
- The middle joint bends 75–100°, so the middle and end segments run
  level into the wall.
- The end joint is straight or bent back up to 10°, so the end segment lies
  flat on the edge.
- The thumb is never over the nail. It's either low, off the hold (H1), or
  upright against the palm side of the index's middle segment or the hold's
  front (H2–H6).
- From the thumb side, its nail faces you.

**Measured studies agree.** For the half crimp in the holding phase, Fischer et
al. 2024 (*Bioengineering* 11(4):370,
[doi:10.3390/bioengineering11040370](https://doi.org/10.3390/bioengineering11040370),
Table 5) give MCP 9–15°, PIP 66–75°, DIP −6 to −13° and the wrist bent back
about 15°, for the middle and ring fingers. That reading came from a research
helper and wasn't checked against the table itself.

**Measured studies.** Fischer et al. 2024 (Table 5, as above) give for the crimp:

- MCP 23–44°, PIP 85–89° and DIP −11 to −28°, mean about −20°.
- The wrist is bent back 28–37°.
- PIP varied from 58 to 106° between climbers.

Two definitions agree:

- Schweizer 2001 (*J Biomech* 34:217, PMID 11165286, abstract): PIP about 90°
  with the DIP bent back as far as it goes.
- Bayer et al. 2012 (*J Hand Surg Am* 37:899,
  [doi:10.1016/j.jhsa.2012.02.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhsa.2012.02.016)):
  MCP 0–45°, PIP 90–100°, DIP bent back 0–10°.

The photos show more PIP flexion (100–120°) than the lab, as these are
demonstrations on small edges.

**What you see of a full crimp from the thumb side.** These points hold in
every photo above:

- **The middle joint is a round knob, not a peak.** With the joint bent past
  90°, the back of the finger wraps round the head of the first phalanx. That
  makes a curve with a radius of about half the finger's depth, 0.8–0.9 cm.
  The two straight backs of the segments meet at 60–80°, but the corner
  between them is that curve, never a point.
- **It rises about 2–3 cm above the edge.** That works out from the angles:
  with MCP 30° and PIP 105–110°, the middle segment (2.4 cm) drops at 45–60°
  below the level. The end segment (1.9 cm), bent back 20°, then slopes down
  about 25° to the edge. So the joint's axis sits 2.3–2.9 cm above the end
  joint's level at the edge. In the photos the top of the knob stands one and
  a half to two finger depths above the hold's top, a little less in the
  views from above. It's a knuckle, not a tower.
- **The middle joints make a crest, not a single point.** The middle finger's
  joint stands 0.3–0.5 cm higher than the index's, just behind it. The ring
  finger's may show as a third bump.
- **The index's middle segment drops steeply,** 45–70° below the level, from
  the knob to the edge. The end segment then lies much flatter, 0–25° below
  the level, because the end joint is bent back.
- **The thumb crosses, it doesn't stack.** It rises from the ball of the thumb
  and lies across the index's end segment at 45–60° to it. Its pad presses the
  index's nail and its tip reaches the edge, at or just past the index's tip.
  From the thumb side you see the thumb's back and nail, not its profile. It
  looks broad, 2.0–2.2 cm, with an oval nail facing you, unlike any finger in
  the drawing, which are all seen from the side.
- **The thumb hides** the index's end joint, its end segment and its nail, and
  the edge under them. The index's middle segment, the middle joint and the
  first segment stay visible.
- **A small dark gap** shows below the knob. It is bounded by the index's
  middle segment, the thumb and the palm, about 0.5–1 cm across. It's the
  clearest sign that the thumb is a separate digit.
- **The back of the hand stands upright.** The wrist is cocked back 20–35° and
  the forearm leans in from below, the elbow further from the wall than the
  wrist.

**What the current figures get wrong.** Compare
`book/figures/full-crimp.svg` and the atlas tile with the points above:

- The index's middle joint is drawn as a tall pointed apex.
- The thumb is drawn in profile, as a tube as narrow as a finger, which is why
  it reads as a second index.
- Drawn with the thumb seen from its back, broad, with the nail facing the
  reader, plus the gap, the two digits can't be confused.

## 2. Anatomy numbers

A research helper collected these, and every number keeps its source. Numbers
from an abstract, a secondary quote or the helper's own arithmetic are marked.

**Scale.** Buryanov & Kotiuk 2010 (*Int J Morphol* 28(3):755–758, Table I, 66
adults, X-rays; [PDF](https://www.scielo.cl/pdf/ijmorphol/v28n3/art15.pdf))
comes out at about the model hand's size. Its middle finger measures 8.0 cm
from the web to the tip. `hand-proportions.md` gives the model 19 − 11.02 =
7.98 cm. So its millimetres can be used as they are.

### The knuckle arc and the joint rows

- **How far each knuckle sits behind the middle one** along the hand's length.
  These are the helper's arithmetic and weak:
  - index 0–0.3 cm
  - ring 0.5–1.0 cm
  - little 1.1–1.6 cm
- They rest on Buryanov's metacarpal lengths and on one abstract, Okoli et al.
  2022 (*Hand* 17(1),
  [doi:10.1177/1558944720919897](https://doi.org/10.1177/1558944720919897)).
  It puts the index and middle knuckles level, with cascade angles of 24° to
  the ring and 27° to the little finger.
- `hand.py` has the little knuckle 0.65–1.0 cm behind, at the low end of this.
- **Artists' rule:** the knuckle row, and every joint row in front of it, lie
  on arcs centred near the base of the thumb. The arcs curve more toward the
  tips (Bridgman, *Constructive Anatomy*, 1920, p. 19 and the thumb section;
  Loomis, *Drawing the Head and Hands*, 1956, Plate 79; Hampton, *Figure
  Drawing: Design and Invention*, 2009, p. 175).
- **Across the back,** the knuckles make a low dome peaking at the middle
  knuckle (Bridgman, CA p. 19: the second knuckle is "larger and higher").
- **No numbers were found** for the depth of the transverse arch, or for the
  joint rows when the fingers curl.

### Thumb

- **Base joint position.** No source gives the thumb's carpometacarpal joint
  from the wrist crease. The helper's anatomical estimate is 1.5–2.5 cm beyond
  the distal crease on the thumb side, about 1 cm nearer the wrist than the
  index metacarpal's base. That estimate is weak.
  - Buchholz, Armstrong & Goldstein 1992 (*Ergonomics* 35(3):261–273,
    [doi:10.1080/00140139208967812](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139208967812))
    regresses the joint's position on hand length. It is paywalled and would
    settle this.
- **Rotation.** Cooney et al. 1981 (*JBJS Am* 63(9):1371–81, PMID 7320028,
  abstract) found the trapezium's axes at median 48° flexion, 38° abduction
  and 80° pronation relative to the middle metacarpal. That 80° is why the
  thumb's nail faces sideways, and why from the thumb side you see the thumb's
  back and nail while the fingers are in profile.
- **Resting angle to the index.** No anatomical source found. The artists:
  - Seen from the back, the thumb leaves the palm as a narrow wedge at about
    25–30° (Hogarth, *Drawing Dynamic Hands*, 1977, p. 25).
  - Seen from the side, it is tipped 30–35° from the plane of palm and fingers
    (Hogarth p. 71).
- **Where the tip reaches,** with the thumb laid alongside the hand:
  - The index's middle joint (Bridgman, CA p. 51).
  - Nearly there (Loomis p. 133).
  - About 1 cm short of it (`hand-proportions.md`, from ANSUR 1988).
  - `hand.py` rests it at the middle of the index's first segment, which is
    shorter than all three.
- **Thumb ranges** (Barakat, Field & Taylor 2013, *Hand* 8:179,
  [doi:10.1007/s11552-013-9492-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11552-013-9492-y),
  100 adults, abstract):
  - base joint: palmar abduction 61°, radial abduction 63°
  - MCP: flexion 60°, bending back 8°
  - IP: flexion 88°, bending back 12° (0–45°)

### How the joints curl together

- **End joint and middle joint.** In free curling, the end joint follows the
  middle joint:
  - DIP = 0.76 × PIP on average, varying widely (Hahn et al. 1995, *J Hand
    Surg Br* 20:696,
    [doi:10.1016/s0266-7681(05)80139-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0266-7681(05)80139-1),
    34 index fingers).
  - Hume et al. 1990's functional means give 39/60 = 0.65 (*J Hand Surg Am*
    15(2):240–3,
    [doi:10.1016/0363-5023(90)90102-w](https://doi.org/10.1016/0363-5023(90)90102-w)).
  - The ⅔ rule of computer animation, credited to Rijpkema & Girard 1991, was
    not checked.
- **Use 0.7 × PIP for a curled finger that isn't loaded.** A finger pressed on
  an edge breaks the coupling: the end joint bends back in the crimps and
  stays near straight in the half crimp.
- **Knuckle and middle joint.** No fixed ratio. In grasping, the fingertip
  follows a logarithmic spiral (Kamper et al. 2003, *J Neurophysiol* 90:3702,
  [doi:10.1152/jn.00546.2003](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00546.2003)).

### Soft tissue

- **Pulp beyond the bone tip** (Buryanov): fingers 0.37–0.40 cm, thumb 0.57
  cm.
- **Pulp on the palm side of the end segment:** up to 0.64 ± 0.08 cm, over a
  bone 0.4 cm high (Dallard et al. 2018, *J Biomech* 67:166,
  [doi:10.1016/j.jbiomech.2017.11.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2017.11.024),
  MRI). Only five subjects, so weak.
- **Skin on the back:** 0.12 cm over the back of the hand and the index's
  first segment (Li et al. 2018, *Arthritis Res Ther* 20:181,
  [doi:10.1186/s13075-018-1686-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-018-1686-9),
  healthy controls). Skin only, without tendon or fat, so the back of a finger
  is thin and bony.
- **Nail plate:** 0.04 cm, index to little finger (Szymoniak-Lipska et al.
  2021, *Front Med* 8:686470,
  [doi:10.3389/fmed.2021.686470](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2021.686470)).
- **The ball of the thumb,** derived from `hand-proportions.md`: the palm is
  4.5–4.9 cm deep with it and 3.4–3.9 cm from the little-finger side without
  it, so it adds about 1.0 cm in front of the palm.
- **Not found:**
  - palm-side tissue at the first and middle segments
  - tissue over the middle joints
  - the depth of the ball of the thumb and of the little-finger side
  - the web between thumb and index
  - Karademir 2023
    ([doi:10.1177/17531934221130669](https://doi.org/10.1177/17531934221130669))
    has the thenar and hypothenar depths but is paywalled.

**The artists' version** fills the gaps in shape, if not in number:

- The palm has three fleshy masses, the ball of the thumb, the little-finger
  side and the pads under the fingers (Loomis p. 135; Hogarth pp. 17–19).
- They form a triangle pointing up the arm, and the ball of the thumb is the
  largest (Hogarth pp. 17–19; Proko, hand bones notes).
- The palm-side pads of a finger are of equal length, so the creases don't line
  up with the joints (Bridgman, CA, "The Fingers").
- The back of the hand and fingers is bone under skin (Hampton, side view;
  Proko, details notes).

## 3. How artists build a hand

A second research helper read the archive.org texts of Bridgman, Loomis,
Hogarth and Hampton and Proko's lesson notes. The OCR page numbers can be off
by a page. Loomis is cited by plate, which is reliable. Bridgman (1920) is
public domain; the others are paraphrased.

### Construction order

- **Bridgman** (*Constructive Anatomy*, 1920, "The Hand" pp. 18–19,
  "Construction" p. 35):
  1. Two masses only, the hand proper and the thumb.
  2. Choose the action side, which has the greatest angle, and the inaction
     side, which runs straight with the arm.
  3. Draw construction lines through the wrist, the widest part of the hand,
     the index knuckle and the second knuckle, joining at the little finger.
     They place the fingers and fix the proportions.
- **Loomis** (*Drawing the Head and Hands*, 1956, Part Five, pp. 134–135,
  Plates 78 "Block forms" and 80 "Construction"):
  1. Get the palm right first, then attach and align the fingers to it.
  2. Block the back of the hand in three planes: one from the thumb down to the
     index knuckle, and two tapering to the wrist.
  3. Block the palm as three masses round the hollow: the heel, the ball of the
     thumb, and the pad under the fingers.
- **Hampton** (*Figure Drawing: Design and Invention*, 2009, "The Whole
  Process: The Hand", about pp. 170–177):
  1. Gesture S-curves through the forearm and the middle three fingers.
  2. A C-curve where the fingertips end.
  3. The wrist's ends, radius and ulna.
  4. The palm box, half the hand long.
  5. Each finger: spheres at the joints and lines for the bones, then
     cylinders with T-shaped overlaps, then C-curve undersides.
- **Proko** ([How to Draw Hands from
  Imagination](https://proko.com/lesson/how-to-draw-hands-from-imagination-step-by-step)):
  1. The palm box, as tall as it is wide. Shorten its little-finger side and
     curve its front so the middle finger comes out longest.
  2. The fingers, a cylinder per segment and a box at each joint.
  3. The thumb: a triangular box at the base, a cylinder, a scooped wedge for
     the tip.
  4. Set the hand a little below the forearm, so there's a step at the wrist.
- **Hogarth** (*Drawing Dynamic Hands*, 1977; from a summary, not checked word
  for word):
  1. A rod-and-ball action sketch (p. 21).
  2. The big masses and the finger lines before the thumb (p. 31).
  3. An arc across the knuckles to place the fingers (pp. 98–100).

### The palm, joints, fingers and thumb

- **The palm is a wedge.** It is bevelled from knuckles to wrist on the edge
  and widens from wrist to knuckles on the flat (Bridgman, CA p. 19). Hogarth
  calls it a spatula-like wedge, thick in the middle and thinner where the
  knuckles come out (p. 14). The palm is hollow and the back convex; without
  the hollow the hand looks flat (Loomis p. 134, Plate 81).
- **Joint rows lie on concentric arcs** round the base of the thumb (see part
  2).
- **Fingers step down and taper.** The joints are square and the shafts are
  narrower than the joints. The tips are triangular and the middle joint the
  largest (Bridgman, CA p. 57). The segments are never in a straight line: each
  step down from one segment to the next is bridged by a wedge (CA pp. 58–59).
  Proko (details notes) makes the first segment roundest, the middle flatter
  and the tip a wedge tilting up.
- **The thumb's base is part of the palm.** Bridgman (CA, thumb section) makes
  it a pyramid of muscle reaching the wrist, the life line and the index base.
  It joins the hand at an angle, never flat with it. Hogarth (pp. 17–19, 60)
  sets the thumb as a narrow triangular block on the egg-shaped ball. Hampton
  (about p. 166) and Proko give a door-wedge on the side of the palm box. All
  agree: a wedge from the wrist to the index knuckle, never an oval.
- **Curled fingers.** The tighter the fist, the more the fingers arch across
  the knuckles. The thumb lies against the index or across the middle finger
  (Bridgman, *The Book of a Hundred Hands*, 1920, about pp. 136–142). Fingers
  close from the little finger to the index, thumb last (Hogarth pp. 77–79,
  summary).

### What makes a mitten

- **Parallel contours.** Fingers drawn with parallel sides look like sausages.
  Hampton (p. 170) gives each a straight, crisp top on the bone side and
  C-curves underneath. Each C-curve runs from behind one joint sphere to the
  centre of the next.
- **No contrast between back and palm side.** The back of a finger is hard,
  with straighter lines, flat planes and sharp corners. The palm side is curved
  and cushioned (Proko, details notes; Hogarth, the same).
- **No step at the joints.** There's a step at every segment, with square
  joints and narrower shafts (Bridgman, CA pp. 57–59), and a step down from the
  forearm to the hand (Bridgman; Proko).
- **Equal lengths and spacing.** Stagger the tips as in the lengths table.
  Preston Blair places the fingers unevenly (second-hand, via [Gurney
  Journey](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2012/03/drawing-cartoon-hands.html)).
- **No knuckle bumps.** The middle knuckle is the highest (Bridgman, CA p. 19).
- **No overlap.** Hampton's T-overlaps show where a finger cylinder meets a
  joint sphere.
- **Too many lines** (Proko, details notes):
  - On the back: one line at the end joint, about three at the middle joint,
    and the base knuckles subtle.
  - On the palm side: one crease at the end joint and two at the middle joint.

### The hand from the thumb side

- The hand is a wedge seen edge-on (Bridgman, CA p. 19; Hogarth p. 14).
- A muscle mass bulges between the index knuckle and the thumb. Beyond it, the
  web changes with the thumb's movement, from a half-moon blade to dimples
  (Bridgman, BHH about p. 66).
- The thumb is tipped about 30–35° from the side plane of palm and fingers
  (Hogarth p. 71). Pressed against the index, its nail is about 90° to the back
  of the hand (Hogarth p. 72, summary only). That agrees with the photos: the
  thumb shows its nail when the fingers show their sides.
- The upper hand is mostly bone and the palm fleshy (Hampton, side view).
- Each finger segment steps down through a wedge (Bridgman, CA pp. 58–59).

### Keeping a character's hand the same

- **Model sheets** exist so that many artists draw a character the same way,
  and they often include hands ([Model
  sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_sheet)). One example is a Les
  Clark Mickey Mouse hand sheet from about 1932, seen only as a caption
  ([Animation Resources](https://animationresources.org/?p=2308)).
- **The proportions stay fixed;** only bulk, dimples and knuckle size change
  with age (Loomis Plates 91–92).
- **Ratios are guides,** not laws (Hampton p. 163).
- **Pictograms:** ISO 3864-3 has an annex on drawing the hand and fingers and
  hand profiles, and a clause on consistency within a symbol family. Only its
  table of contents was seen
  ([sample](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/iso/iso-3864-3-2024/b44bb44e082a4a58a58b615da8a6b40b/iso-3864-3-2024.pdf)).
- **Ligne claire and instruction manuals:** no source was found on how Hergé,
  Jacobs or IKEA simplify hands. The Gubbies jug drawing (J2) is a modern
  clean-line example:
  - one line weight
  - each fingertip a separate bump
  - the thumb in its own closed outline
  - no creases

## 4. Rules for the code

1. **One hand, one set of lengths.** Derive every length from the 19 cm hand
   and one segment rule, and never rescale a hand per figure (Loomis Plate 92;
   model-sheet practice).
2. **Build from masses in order:**
   - the palm wedge, thicker at the wrist and thinner at the knuckles;
   - the ball of the thumb as part of it;
   - the thumb's wedge;
   - the knuckle arc;
   - the finger rows;
   - the details.
3. **Joint rows on concentric arcs** centred near the thumb's base, with the
   middle knuckle highest across the back.
4. **Square joints, narrower shafts, a step at every joint.** Straight backs
   and C-curve undersides, never parallel outlines.
5. **The thumb from the side is seen from its back.** It is rotated about 80°
   to the fingers, so in a thumb-side view draw its nail facing the reader. It
   is 2.0–2.2 cm wide and its outline is broad and short, unlike the finger
   profiles.
6. **Show at least a sliver of a second finger.** In side views, show the
   middle finger's middle joint or tip, 0.3–0.5 cm behind and above the
   index's, per the visibility table. That breaks the single lump.
7. **Half crimp:**
   - back of the hand upright;
   - MCP 10–20°, PIP 80–95°, DIP −5 to −15°;
   - middle and end segments level into the wall;
   - thumb upright against the palm side of the index's middle segment or the
     hold's front, never over the nail.
8. **Full crimp:**
   - MCP 25–40°, PIP 100–115°, DIP −15 to −30°, wrist bent back 20–35°;
   - the middle joint a round knob, radius 0.8–0.9 cm, about 2–3 cm above the
     edge;
   - the middle finger's joint a little higher behind it;
   - the thumb crossing the index's end segment at 45–60°, pad on the nail,
     tip at the edge;
   - the small gap between thumb, index and palm.
9. **Open hand and sloper:**
   - fingers nearly straight, with only the end joints bending, 20–50°, over
     an edge;
   - the thumb straight along the palm side and free on an edge, or its pad on
     the hold on a sloper;
   - on a sloper, the palm side of every segment follows the hold and the
     wrist sits below it.
10. **Curl unloaded fingers with DIP ≈ 0.7 × PIP.** Fingers loaded on an edge
    follow the grip's own angles instead.
11. **Keep within the joint ranges:**
    - MCP to 90°
    - PIP to about 110°
    - DIP to about 80°, and −30° under load
    - wrist extension to about 70°
12. **Lines:** one weight for outlines. Nails only where a nail faces the
    reader. One crease at the end joint and up to three at the middle joint on
    the back. Keep minor creases light or leave them out at 2.6 mm to the cm.
