# Typography principles

The rules the book's design follows, each with its reason. They draw on
Robert Bringhurst's *The Elements of Typographic Style* (chapters 2–4 on
rhythm, proportion and structural forms, chapter 8 on the page), Richard
Rutter's *Web Typography* (the chapters on hierarchy and scale and on
vertical rhythm) and the page layout of John Kettle's *Rock Climbing
Technique*. How the design carries them out is in `typography-notes.md`.

## Hierarchy

1. **One modular scale.** Every size comes from one ratio (1.2) on the
   text size. Reading and reference matter take the half steps next to the
   text size, headings take whole steps. Sizes from one scale look designed
   together; Bringhurst's first rule of size is not to compose without one.
2. **Each level differs by size and one more signal.** Chapter, grade
   guide, section, entry and subhead each step up in size and also differ in
   weight, space, a new page or a grade marker. Rutter warns that size alone
   is a weak signal.
3. **Change one thing at a time; larger is lighter.** Large heads are
   regular weight, small heads bold, labels letterspaced small caps. A head
   that is bigger and bolder and italic shouts.
4. **Flush-left heads, centred openings.** Every head below the chapter is
   set flush left. Only the title page, part pages and chapter openings are
   centred, keeping the exception at the top of the hierarchy, as
   Bringhurst advises for mixed series.

## Vertical space

5. **The line is the unit.** The text's line pitch is the unit of vertical
   space; every space between blocks adds whole or half lines to it. Space
   in measured intervals brings the text back on the beat after each
   interruption (Bringhurst's "measured intervals", Rutter's vertical
   rhythm).
6. **More space between blocks than inside them.** Lines of a paragraph sit
   closest, list items a little apart, lists half a line from the text,
   boxes, tables and figures a full line, subheads one and a half lines,
   sections two and a half. The space then shows what belongs together.
7. **More space above a head than below it.** A head belongs to the text it
   introduces, not to the text before it.
8. **Indent paragraphs, don't space them.** Running paragraphs are marked by
   an indent; the first after a head, list or box starts flush (Bringhurst
   2.3). Extra space is kept for the boundaries between blocks, so that it
   means something.
9. **Big divisions start a page.** Chapters, grade guides, grade sections
   and every technique entry start a page, as every exercise does in
   Kettle's book. On an e-reader a link then lands on the start of what it
   points to. A page that ends short at the end of an entry is normal, and
   nothing is tightened to save a page.
10. **Margins are part of the page.** The foot margin is deeper than the
    head, with the running head in the head margin, so the text block hangs
    from the top instead of running into the foot (Bringhurst 8.5).
11. **A long measure needs open leading.** The text block stays wide enough
    for a figure beside the steps, about 90 characters. That is long for
    continuous prose but workable for reference text with generous leading
    (Bringhurst 2.1.2).

## The grade

12. **A badge marks a heading; it never is one.** Every heading, a grade
    guide's or a grade section's included, is a real heading in the heading
    type with its own text. The grade sits beside it on a black tab, as a
    marker; no headline is set inside a box.
13. **One badge, four sizes.** The tab has one shape at every size: in
    running text, in an entry's title band, beside a grade section and
    beside a grade guide. It grows with the rank of the heading it marks.

## Rules and ornament

14. **Two weights of rule, horizontal only.** A heavy rule opens a unit (an
    entry's title band, the top of every box and table); a light rule
    divides or closes one (the running head, a table's head, the foot of
    boxes and tables, an entry's references). No frames, double rules or
    vertical rules: Bringhurst asks for as little furniture as possible,
    running in the reading direction.
15. **Every box is the same box.** The reading box, notes, reminders and key
    points share one set of rules, inset and surrounding space; only the
    reading box is tinted. The label says what a box is.
16. **One family of ornaments, each with a meaning and a fixed place.** An
    ornament must belong to the book's subject and to its drawings, or it
    is decoration for its own sake. The ornaments are holds, drawn by the
    figure renderer like every hold in the figures, and each part and
    chapter has its own, chosen for what it is about, so the opening says
    where you are before the title does. They mark only openings: the
    title page, the part pages and the chapter openings, where a small hold
    sits between two short light rules. Reference matter shares one quiet
    foothold, and chapters end without an ornament.
17. **An icon marks an aside that recurs.** The asides found in nearly
    every entry (the reading box, the common mistakes, the note for tall
    climbers) each have a small icon beside the label, so you find them at
    a glance. An icon passes the ornament rule: it is drawn like the
    figures, with their own signs, and shows what its aside is about. It
    hangs in the margin and takes no room, so the label stays flush left
    and the spacing is unchanged. The grade note has none, since the grade
    already has its tab.

## The e-ink screen

18. **Nothing faint.** No rule under 0.5 pt, all text black, a tint only
    behind the reading box, and running text no smaller than 10.5 pt.
