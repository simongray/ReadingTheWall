# Dossier A: Feet and legs (footwork)

Research dossier for the indoor bouldering technique handbook. Category:
**Feet and legs (footwork)**. Source IDs use prefix **A** (list at the end).

Conventions used here:

- Claims carry source IDs in square brackets. A step or cue with no ID is
  marked **(judgement)**: my own reading-the-wall inference built from the
  sourced mechanics, flagged so the author can check it or cut it.
- **(unverified)** marks something seen only in a search snippet, or a
  term I could not confirm in a fetched page.
- Body-positioning techniques that use the feet but belong to another
  chapter (flagging, backstep, drop knee, stemming, mantle) are only
  cross-referenced here.

## Grade placement: what the evidence says

There is no public syllabus that ties named footwork techniques to V-grades.
What I found:

- **NICAS Bouldering (formerly NIBAS), UK.** Five levels: New, Foundation,
  Competent, Skilled and Performance Boulderer. The public overview describes
  levels only in general terms, such as "an introduction to equipment and
  movement skills" at Level 2 and "a wide range of skills" at Level 4. It
  names no footwork techniques [A46]. A centre's Level 3 page says only
  "learning more complex techniques", and the same centre runs separate
  "Fundamentals of Footwork" and "Performance Footwork" courses [A55].
  NICAS levels therefore cannot anchor footwork to grades.
- **"FUNdamentals 2" coaching syllabus** (a .docx found online; the file
  names no organisation, but the title matches the UK FUNdamentals coaching
  workshops **(unverified)**). It puts "base of support" and its relation to
  the centre of gravity at this stage, and lists "flagging and drop knee"
  under "Steep rock techniques" [A47]. So by the second coaching stage, foot
  basics are assumed and the steep-wall leg techniques get taught.
- ***The Climbing Bible*** (Mobråten & Christophersen).
  Its table of contents files edging (p. 32), smearing (p. 33),
  "volumes and large footholds" (p. 33) and climbing shoes (p. 35) under
  basic **Footwork**. Heel hook (p. 68) and toe hook (p. 70) sit under
  **Specific techniques**, and "Foot placement" (p. 138) and "Going footless"
  (p. 129) appear in the training chapter [A48]. I verified only the table
  of contents (publisher sample PDF), not the content of those pages.
- **Gym clinics tied to grades.** Touchstone's "Gateway to Advanced
  Technique" clinic lists drop knees, heel and toe hooks, rockovers and
  mantles and dynos as skills "commonly found in V5–V6 boulders", for
  climbers already at V5–V6 [A42]. Movement Gyms' intermediate series
  teaches heel and toe hooks, flagging, body tension and compression to help
  climbers "overcome that V5 plateau" [A43]. Sportrock files heel-hook
  purposes and timed foot swaps under "intermediate", and calls the bicycle
  and flagging "advanced" [A25, A26].
- **Coach fault lists by level.** Neil Gresham lists these indoor faults for
  beginner-to-intermediate climbers: "scuffing and banging feet", making
  excessively high steps instead of "building" feet, and looking for hands
  more than feet. For intermediate-to-advanced climbers he lists
  over-testing feet, foot-sequence mistakes, "double foot-swaps instead of
  flagging", a wrongly placed counterbalancing foot, and cutting loose
  [A7].
- **Forum consensus (anecdotal).** Mountain Project posters: "V3/6B is the
  first 'real bouldering' grade in most gyms". V2 can often be forced with
  muscle and V3 usually can't, so "one needs to develop basic footwork and
  body positioning skills to get there". Heel and toe hooks and mantles
  become "obligate and technical/tricky" from V3 [A44]. Another poster's
  first mandatory heel hook was on a "3-" [A45].
- **Friction Labs** says toe hooks are "typically found on moderate to
  difficult routes and problems" [A14], and advises learning the drop knee
  before the kneebar [A17].

**Working conclusion (judgement):** the inside edge, toeing, precise
placement and basic smearing are V0–V2 skills. Indoors, the outside edge on
steep walls, foot swaps, rockovers and the first real heel hooks become
necessary around V3. Toe hooks, toe-pulling on overhangs and controlled foot
cuts start deciding problems at V4–V5. Bicycles, heel-toe cams and knee bars
are V5+ indoors, and the last two are uncommon. Gym grades vary a lot, so
treat every grade below as ±1.

A compact summary of the cues in the entries below. Each line is detailed
and sourced in its entry.

### Precise foot placement and quiet feet

- **Also called:** precision feet, silent feet, quiet feet, "glue feet"
  (holding the placement still); DE: *präzises Treten*, *leises Treten*
  [A27], *Fuß-Auge-Koordination* (foot-eye coordination) [A29].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V0 to learn, and *needed* by about V2–V3, when
  footholds shrink to screw-on jibs and problems can no longer be muscled
  through. Forum posters put the break at V3 [A44]. Gresham lists
  scuffing and banging feet as the typical beginner-to-intermediate fault
  [A7]. **(judgement on exact grade)**
- **What it is:** Choosing the exact best spot on a foothold, looking the
  foot onto it, setting the right part of the shoe there first time, and
  weighting it before the body moves, so that the foot does not slip or need
  readjusting.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Before lifting the foot, pick one *point* on the hold: the most
     positive part, such as the corner of an edge, the deepest bit of a
     pocket or the incut lip of a jug foot [A3, A35, A2].
  2. Look at that point and keep looking until the foot is set [A3, A27].
     Keep your eyes on the foot for about two seconds after placement
     before looking up [A24]. Only lift your eyes to the next hold once the
     foot is placed [A29].
  3. Use the **big toe**: place the inside of the toe end of the shoe (under
     the big-toe knuckle) on that point [A2, A4, A5]. Use the toe end of
     the edge, not the rounded middle near the arch, with the foot at about
     45° to the wall so the ankle does not touch it [A6].
  4. Bring the shoe in slowly so it meets the hold and the wall at the same
     time. Keep the ankle relaxed as it lands so the foot absorbs the
     weight, because a rigid ankle makes the noise [A5]. Do not scuff the
     wall above the hold [A7].
  5. Shift some body weight onto the foot *before* you stand up on it [A36].
     Lower your centre of gravity and bend the knee while pressing, which
     drives more force through the shoe [A2].
  6. While the body moves past, keep the foot still and hinge at the ankle.
     No pivoting, tilting or readjusting unless you mean to pivot (see
     Pivoting) [A3, A33, A35].
  7. Only now move a hand.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Always, but it decides problems when footholds are small, sloping or
    slippery, and when they sit below your eye line or out of view.
  - Failure symptoms:
    - Feet "keep popping off" [A44].
    - A foot thuds or squeaks as it lands.
    - You re-place a foot more than once.
    - A foot skates the moment you look up at the next hold [A30].
  - When you start to struggle, look down: "When the going gets tough,
    focus on the feet!" [A35].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Bouncing the foot around the hold instead of choosing a spot [A2].
  - Over-testing and readjusting [A7].
  - Heel too high on a hold, which steepens the foot angle so it pops, or
    too low, which moves weight to the back of the foot and cuts friction
    at the toe [A2].
  - Stepping on an unweighted foot [A36].
  - Looking for hands instead of feet [A7].
- **Drills:**
  - Silent feet, with a partner calling out any squeak [A5, A24].
  - Imagine the wall has wet paint: "will your foot leave lines or dots?"
    [A5].
  - Run tape across the sole under the toes so you can feel where you
    stand [A5].
  - "Blinking": close your eyes just before contact and find the hold by
    feel [A3].
  - Silent traverse for 5–10 minutes [A25].
  - Hörst's "target practice", on terrain "about one full number grade
    below your onsight ability" [A35].
  - Do all of these slowly in the warm-up and speed up as you improve
    [A7, A3]. Accuracy at speed is the goal, not slowness itself.
- **Tall climber note:** Your eyes are further from your feet than most
  climbers', and Hörst notes that this distance, together with the poor
  feedback feet give, is why feet need more attention than hands [A36].
  Bend at the hips or turn your head to see the hold; don't place by
  memory. On a project, a small chalk tick above a key foothold is
  acceptable (brush it off after) [A35]. **(judgement on applying to
  height)**
- **Illustration brief:** Close-up side profile of one foot and lower leg
  against a vertical wall. Small square-cut edge. Shoe placed with the toe
  end of the inside edge on the hold's outer corner, the foot angled about
  45° to the wall, a gap between ankle and wall. A dashed eye-line comes
  down from the top edge of the frame to the contact point. Ghost (dotted)
  shoe hovering 10 cm above, showing the approach path as a short straight
  arrow. A small "dot" mark at the contact point next to a crossed-out
  "smear line" mark (wet-paint image).
- **Related:** Edging (inside), Pivoting, Smearing, Foot-first sequencing.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A2, A3, A4, A5, A7, A24, A25, A27, A29, A30, A33, A35, A36,
  A44]

### Pivoting on the foot

- **Also called:** swivelling, rolling the foot.
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V3, when hips start turning between
  facing in and side-on on steep walls. **(judgement)**
- **What it is:** Rotating the shoe on the foothold, typically from inside
  edge to outside edge or the reverse, so the hips and torso can turn
  without lifting the foot.
- **How to do it:**
  1. When you first place the foot, stand on the *near* side of the hold to
     leave room to swivel. Otherwise you will spin off the far side [A6].
  2. Keep the big toe as the fulcrum and rotate the body over it [A5].
  3. Turn the heel while the toe stays on its point. Inside edge becomes
     outside edge as the hip turns into the wall [A2, A6].
  4. Weight stays on the foot throughout. You are steering, not
     re-placing.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Traversing.
  - Switching from a front-on step to a backstep or drop knee on the same
    foothold.
  - Setting up a foot swap [A6].
  - Symptom: you take a foot off and put it back on the same hold just to
    face another way, which Gresham calls "less neat" [A6].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Placing on the far edge of the hold, so the pivot rolls you off [A6].
  - Pivoting off the big toe (the foot pivots off or needs resetting)
    [A5].
- **Illustration brief:** Top-down view of one foothold (a 4 cm deep
  rectangle) with two shoe outlines. Solid: inside edge on the near-left
  part of the hold, toe pointing right. Dotted: same toe point, heel swung
  outward, outside edge now on the hold. A curved arrow around the big-toe
  point labelled "pivot". A small inset shows a climber from behind, hips
  square (ghost) turning to hip-in (solid).
- **Related:** Edging (outside), Foot swap, backstep and drop knee (other
  chapter).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A2, A5, A6]

### Edging: inside edge

- **Also called:** inside edging, front step (when facing in) [A23],
  instep [A5]; DE: *Innenrist*, *Innenkante* [A27, A29].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V0. The inside edge under the big toe is "the
  most intuitive edge" and the one new climbers use most [A4]. Gripped puts
  toe-on standing first, before other techniques [A1].
- **What it is:** Standing on a foothold with the inside (big-toe) edge of
  the shoe, near the toe.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Choose the most positive part of the edge [A3].
  2. Turn the hip out (external rotation) so the inside edge faces the
     hold [A5].
  3. Place the *toe end* of the inside edge, under the big-toe knuckle, on
     the hold. Line the shoe edge up with the hold edge, and hold the foot
     at about 45° to the wall rather than parallel to it [A6, A33].
  4. Keep the ankle at about 90° and wrap the toes over the hold [A3].
  5. On a downward-sloping edge, lift the heel and point the toe down. On
     an upward-sloping edge, drop the heel [A6].
  6. Keep the hips and centre of gravity close to the wall on vertical
     ground [A27]. Keep most of your weight on this leg while the other
     foot moves [A5].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Vertical and slightly less-than-vertical walls with hips square to the
    wall.
  - Small edges angled slightly sideways [A29].
  - Footholds below and in front of you.
  - Symptoms that you need something else:
    - On an overhang, square-hip ("frog") inside edging leaves the arms
      doing everything and you unbalanced; switch to the outside edge
      [A7, A50].
    - The inside edge limits how far you can turn on the hold [A27].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Standing on the middle of the shoe near the arch [A6].
  - Foot parallel to the wall, so the ankle hits it [A6].
  - Heel too high or too low for the hold's slope [A2, A6].
- **Illustration brief:** Two panels. (1) Climber from behind on a
  vertical wall, both feet on small edges using inside edges, knees turned
  slightly out, hips close to the wall, centre-of-gravity marker over the
  feet. (2) Close-up from above of the shoe on an edge: the contact patch
  shaded under the big toe at the front of the inside edge, the shoe at 45°
  to the wall line.
- **Related:** Precise foot placement, Edging (outside), Pivoting, Smearing
  (smedging).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, A6, A7, A27, A29, A33, A50]

### Edging: outside edge

- **Also called:** outside edging, backstep (the outside edge plus the hip
  turned in) [A41, A33]; DE: *Außenrist*, *Außenkante* [A27, A29];
  *eindrehen* (turning in) [A29].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V3, the point where gym problems move onto
  overhanging walls and square hips stop working. American Alpine Institute
  calls it "the utility move for climbing steep walls" [A50]. Gresham says
  many climbers get the inside edge right but the outside edge wrong [A6].
  **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Standing on the little-toe side of the shoe, usually
  with that hip turned into the wall.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Use the *toe end* of the outside edge, just in front of the base of
     the little toe, not the side of the foot [A6, A33].
  2. Turn the hip into the wall (internal rotation) so the outside edge
     faces the hold [A5].
  3. Point the toe down and raise the ankle, and drive from the big-toe end
     of the shoe. This drops the knee and lifts the hip, which is useful on
     overhangs [A5].
  4. Turn the heel out to about 45° when stepping through, laybacking or
     using side-pulls [A6].
  5. On steep walls: turn the hips in, step across the centreline, and
     brace the spare foot against the wall to make a tripod, so the body's
     rotation powers the reach [A7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The foothold is on the far side of your centreline from the foot.
  - The handhold is a side-pull or layback facing the same way as the
    turned hip.
  - The wall is overhanging [A6, A7, A50].
  - Sit starts: outside edge on the active foot, hips at about 90° to the
    wall, bum close to the wall, "not in a 'frog' position" [A7].
  - It also gives the arch and calf a rest [A4].
  - Symptoms:
    - On an overhang you feel square to the wall, arms bent and pulling
      hard.
    - You keep swapping feet to reach holds on the other side [A7, A50].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Standing on the side of the foot instead of its toe end [A6].
  - Climbing "frog-style", hips parallel to the wall, on steep ground
    [A50, A7].
  - Over-rotating so far that the foot is about to roll off (see Pivoting).
    **(judgement)**
- **Tall climber note:** Gresham's sit-start advice (outside edge, hips at
  90°, bum in) avoids the deep frog squat that long femurs make awkward
  [A7]. **(judgement on height relevance)**
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind on a 20° overhang. Right
  foot on a small hold to the left of the body centreline using the outside
  edge (little-toe side shaded), right hip turned into the wall, right knee
  dropped slightly. Left foot smeared against the wall to the left as a
  brace (tripod). Left hand on a side-pull facing left, right hand
  reaching up. A curved arrow at the hips showing rotation in.
  Centre-of-gravity marker close to the wall. Ghost overlay of the same
  climber with square hips and bent arms, labelled "frog".
- **Related:** Pivoting, Edging (inside), backstep, drop knee and flagging
  (other chapter), Toe pull on overhangs.
- **Watch:** none found. (American Alpine Institute's "Outside Edge, Flag
  and Drop Knee" page refers to a video but gives no title or URL [A50].)
- **Sources:** [A4, A5, A6, A7, A27, A29, A33, A41, A50]

### Toeing (front-pointing)

- **Also called:** toeing-in [A1], front-pointing, standing on point; DE:
  *frontal antreten*, *frontal stehen* [A29], *Spitze* (tip) [A27].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V0–V1 for pockets and frontal edges (Gripped:
  learn it before other techniques [A1]). Steep-wall toeing (pulling with
  the toe) is a separate entry, needed at about V3–V4.
- **What it is:** Standing on a hold with the very tip of the shoe, foot
  pointing straight into the wall.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Point the foot straight at the hold. For a pocket, lean back first to
     read its shape, then rotate the foot to match it [A6].
  2. Put the pointed toe into the deepest part of the pocket. One toe's
     width is often enough, so do not cram the whole foot in [A6, A3].
  3. Press down with the forefoot and raise the heel slightly to engage the
     calf [A3].
  4. Placing the foot takes abdominal tension and holding it there takes
     lower-back tension, so keep the trunk engaged [A1].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Pockets, holes, frontal ledges [A29], and tiny pockets where the front
    point keeps you neutral and adds reach on tiptoe [A33].
  - Small footholds directly below you on steep walls.
  - The trade-off: edging usually keeps the hips closer to the wall, so on
    vertical ground prefer an edge if the hold allows [A1].
  - Symptom: the toe pops out of a pocket, which means it was placed loose
    or at the easiest angle rather than the deepest one [A6].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Jamming the toe in at whatever angle is easiest [A6].
  - Standing on point for reach on a smear [A6].
- **Shoes:** Downturned, pointed shoes help in pockets; stiffer shoes help
  for long periods on the toe. Soft shoes give more feel but are harder to
  stand on [A1, A6].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile close-up: a shoe pointing straight
  into a round pocket. Toe tip in the bottom-front of the pocket, heel
  slightly raised, a calf-muscle shading line, a downward arrow on the
  forefoot. Ghost foot at a skewed angle with a cross through it ("easiest
  angle").
- **Related:** Precise foot placement, Toe pull on overhangs, Edging
  (inside).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A1, A3, A6, A27, A29, A33]

### Smearing

- **Also called:** smear, friction step; DE: *Reibungstritt*, *auf Reibung
  antreten* [A29], *Reibung* [A27]. Hybrid: **smedging**
  (smear + edge) [A6].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V1–V2. Gym slabs and volumes demand it early,
  and it becomes decisive on slab and volume problems at V3+. Basic smearing
  is filed under basic footwork in *The Climbing Bible* [A48].
  **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Pressing the sole against blank wall, a volume face or a
  sloping hold and relying on friction, not an edge.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Pick the spot precisely ("a millimetre can decide the move"). Decide
     whether to smear in the best body position or on the most incut bit of
     texture, and consider both [A6].
  2. Put the toe on first, "as if placing a stamp", with the foot nearly
     perpendicular to the wall rather than parallel [A30].
  3. **Drop the heel** on a uniform slope or flat wall to get more rubber
     on the surface [A2, A3, A6, A33]. On a flat wall, also bend the toes
     upward so the forefoot engages [A3]. On a small scoop, a *higher* heel
     keeps pressure on the front of the foot [A33].
  4. Keep your weight over the big toe [A33] and press *into* the wall, at
     right angles to its surface [A30].
  5. Knees bent and pointing at the wall. Don't pull the hips tight to the
     wall and don't lean back. Stay upright over the feet [A29, A30]. Don't
     lean in too far [A6].
  6. Apply steady, even pressure and shift weight smoothly from foot to
     foot. Several small steps beat one big one [A6].
- **Variations:**
  - **Volumes:** actively lower the heel. Bergfreunde also advise standing
    on the "outer edge" of the volume [A27] (which edge they mean is
    unclear; check before quoting). Lattice: dropping the heel increases
    the contact area on volumes [A2]. Soft shoes help on volumes and
    slopers [A28].
  - **Sloping footholds:** drop the heel, push the toes and forefoot
    down, and stay high on the hold [A3].
  - **Smedging:** on tiny irregular edges, use the front of the toe and
    compress the rubber so it moulds to the edge. The foot is more
    perpendicular and the heel slightly lower than when edging [A6].
  - **Swapping feet on a smear:** press the heel down into the wall or you
    slip [A26].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Slabs, the flat faces of volumes, dual-texture panels, blank wall
    where you need a push or a brace but no hold exists, and footholds too
    sloping to edge.
  - Rule of thumb: "you can smear an edge, but you can't edge a smear"
    [A33].
  - Symptom: the foot skates at the end of a move, typically when you look
    up at the next handhold and your weight shifts away from the wall
    [A30].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Heel too high, so less rubber is in contact [A2, A6].
  - Pressing straight down instead of into the wall [A30].
  - Leaning back while pushing down [A30].
  - Fidgety test placements [A6].
  - Looking up too early [A30].
- **Tall climber note:** On slabs, high steps upset balance and small steps
  are more efficient [A34]. A long-legged climber is tempted to take big
  steps, which is exactly what smearing punishes [A6]. **(judgement on
  height relevance)**
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile of a climber on a 70° slab (wall
  leaning back). Right foot smearing on a featureless panel: toe on, heel
  dropped well below the toe, the sole contact patch shaded, a force arrow
  perpendicular into the wall surface (not straight down). Knees bent
  towards the wall, hips slightly off the wall, torso upright, centre of
  gravity marker directly over the feet. Inset close-up of the shoe on a
  volume face with the heel-drop angle marked; second inset showing a
  higher heel in a small scoop.
- **Related:** Precise foot placement, Edging (smedging), slab technique
  (other chapter), Foot swap.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A2, A3, A6, A26, A27, A28, A29, A30, A33, A34, A48]

### Foot swap (foot match)

- **Also called:** foot switch, swapping feet, matching feet, foot change;
  DE: *Fußwechsel* [A27], *Umtreten* [A28]. Methods: hop and swap, slice,
  piano switch [A24]; drop and swap, roll and swap [A22]; pivot method,
  ballerina method [A6]; *Umspringen* (hop), *Nachrücken* (shuffle) [A27].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V3 on traverses and sideways sequences.
  Refined swaps (no hip shift, no swing on steep ground) matter at V4+.
  Gresham counts double foot-swaps instead of flagging as an
  intermediate-to-advanced fault [A7]. Sportrock teaches swap timing as
  "intermediate" footwork [A26]. **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Replacing one foot with the other on the same foothold.
- **How to do it** (three families, from least to most refined):
  1. **Hop** (*Umspringen*; "hop and swap"). Put the free foot directly
     above the toes of the standing foot, briefly take more weight on the
     hands, whip the standing foot out and let the other drop in [A27,
     A25]. It is quick but inaccurate, so use it only on big holds and easy
     ground [A24]. Philadelphia Rock Gyms calls hopping the common
     beginner habit: it misses small holds, shifts the hips sideways and
     shock-loads the hold, which knocks you off slopers and smears [A23].
  2. **Slide** ("slice"; controlled swap). Bring the unweighted foot down
     at an angle over the weighted one and slide it in between the wall
     and the weighted foot [A24]. PRG's five-step version:
     1. Twist the lower foot out until the knee nearly faces the wall.
     2. Bring the upper foot's heel in front of the lower shin.
     3. Rest the upper big toe lightly directly above the lower big toe,
        without pushing down.
     4. Pop the lower foot straight back with a knee-only "mule kick", no
        hip movement.
     5. Drop the upper toe onto the exact spot the lower toe just left
        [A23].
  3. **Peel / roll** (piano switch, pivot method, drop and swap, roll and
     swap). Roll one foot off the side of the hold as the other rolls on
     [A24, A6]. Drop the outgoing foot down to free space, or rotate the
     incoming foot outward so it lands right beside the leaving one [A22].
     **Ballerina** variant: knees turned out (plié), hover the incoming toe
     pointing down over the weighted foot, then roll out and switch [A6].
  4. **Shuffle** (*Nachrücken*). On a big hold, place the first foot at one
     end so the second fits beside it, then rotate the first out of the
     way [A27]. Gresham: on large holds, keep the inside corner free for a
     swap [A6].
  5. **Wall step.** Step the leaving foot briefly onto the wall (a smear)
     to free the hold. This is much harder on overhangs [A27].
  - In every method, minimise the time your feet carry less weight [A6].
    On overhangs, "a flick of the hips" stops the legs swinging out
    during the swap [A22].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Your hand sequence needs the *other* foot on the hold you are standing
    on. The weighted foot should usually be opposite the hand that stays
    on, i.e. on the side of the hand you are about to release
    (cross-body tension) [A32].
  - On traverses with only one usable foothold.
  - When crossing your feet would tangle them.
  - Before swapping, check whether a flag or backstep does the job, which
    Gresham says is smoother and often avoids having to swap back [A6,
    A7].
  - Symptoms: you are on the wrong foot for the next reach, so you barn
    door as the hand lets go; or your legs are crossed and locked.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - "Jump and clump" [A6].
  - Hips moving sideways [A23].
  - The incoming toe landing a few millimetres off the old spot [A23].
  - Pushing down with the top foot before the bottom one has left [A23].
  - Legs swinging out into a "cut loose position" on steep ground [A22].
  - Swapping on small holds, which Gresham advises against [A6].
  - Swapping when a flag would do [A7].
- **Drills:** 20 swaps per traverse lap, 3–5 sets, progressing from big
  holds to small holds and smears [A24].
- **Illustration brief:** Three-frame close-up, side-on to the wall, of one
  medium edge. Frame 1: left foot standing on the edge (inside edge), right
  foot hovering directly above it, right big toe just over left big toe.
  Frame 2: left foot popping straight back (arrow from the knee,
  "mule kick"), right toe beginning to drop. Frame 3: right foot on the
  exact same spot, left foot free. A small inset from behind shows the hips
  staying level (horizontal dashed line across both frames). Label "no
  hop".
- **Related:** Pivoting, Precise foot placement, flagging and backstep

### High step

- **Also called:** high foot, high-stepping; DE: *hoch antreten* [A28].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V1–V2 (gym problems with big holds far apart).
  By V3–V4 the skill is often *not* high-stepping: Gresham lists
  "excessively high steps instead of building feet" as a beginner fault
  [A7]. **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Placing a foot on a hold well above the other foot,
  roughly from mid-thigh to waist height relative to the lower foot, that
  is, above the height where you could "shift and sit" on your heel [A31].
- **How to do it:**
  1. First check for an intermediate foothold. A smaller step in between
     usually costs less strength than one big step [A28, A29].
  2. Keep both hands on and arms as straight as the holds allow. Do not
     pull the foot up with the arms [A31]. **(arms straight: judgement)**
  3. Open the hip and turn the knee out so the foot travels up outside the
     knee, then look the foot onto the hold and set the toe precisely.
     **(judgement; see tall note)**
  4. Move the hips toward the wall and towards the high foot until your
     centre of gravity is over it. This "lead with your hips" cue is
     attributed to Bob Gaines' book in a forum post **(unverified)**.
  5. If static weight transfer fails, push off the lower foot and "hop"
     the hips onto the high foot [A31].
  6. Finish with a rockover (next entry).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A large foothold at hip height or above, the next handhold out of reach
    from your current stance, and no intermediate feet.
  - Volume tops and ledges, and the start of a mantle [A31, A34].
  - Symptoms:
    - You are at full arm stretch with feet low and cannot reach.
    - You are pulling with bent arms and over-gripping to stay on [A31].
  - **Counter-cue:** on slabs, high steps disrupt balance, so take small
    steps [A34].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Yarding the foot up with the arms [A31].
  - High-stepping when smaller intermediate steps exist [A7, A28].
  - Getting the foot up but leaving the hips out from the wall, so you
    cannot rock over. **(judgement)**
- **Tall climber note:** A high step is defined relative to *your* leg
  [A31]. A tall climber will often reach past holds that shorter climbers must
  high-step to, but in problems set for average height you meet the
  opposite case: footholds close below the hands, so the knee jams against
  the wall or your chest. Then the limit is hip mobility, turning the knee
  out so the thigh fits beside the torso. Heel hooks close to the pelvis
  likewise need knee and hip flexibility, and tight hips "cost reach"
  [A10]. Climbing.com suggests frog pose and pigeon pose for this [A10].
  **(judgement except the A10 points)**
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind on a vertical wall. Left
  foot on a low edge, right foot raised onto a large hold level with the
  left hip, right knee turned out to the right so the thigh clears the
  torso. Hands on two holds at shoulder height, arms nearly straight. An
  arrow from the hips toward the right foot, labelled "hips over the high
  foot". Ghost of an intermediate foothold between the two feet with the
  label "check for this first".
- **Related:** Rockover, Foot-first sequencing, mantle (other chapter),
  Smearing.

### Rockover

- **Also called:** rock over, rock-over move [A41], rocking over; with the
  foot on a handhold: **hand-foot match**, foot-on-hand-hold [A31].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V3 for basic rockovers onto high feet. The
  hand-foot match becomes regular at V3–V4. Touchstone places "rock overs
  and mantles" among skills "commonly found in V5–V6 boulders" [A42], so
  sources differ. My reading is that the *hard* versions are V5+.
  **(judgement)**
- **What it is:** Transferring the body's weight onto a raised foot and
  standing up on it to reach a higher hold [A41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Get the high foot set precisely (see High step).
  2. Move your weight *across* toward the foot first, not up. Once the
     weight is over the foot, standing up is easy [A21].
  3. Bring the hips over the foot and keep the chest over the foot [A20].
  4. Stand by straightening that leg. The leg does the work, not the arms
     [A20, A34]. Avoid putting a knee down on the hold, which makes
     standing up harder [A34].
  5. **Hand-foot match:**
     1. Turn the pull on the handhold into a push.
     2. Straighten the arm(s).
     3. Put the foot on the shelf level with the hands, shuffling the hand
        aside to make room [A31, A34].
     4. Rock over the foot and push to standing [A31].
  6. **Rocking over a heel:** pull with the heel while pushing with the
     lower leg and pressing with one or both arms, linked in one flowing
     movement [A8].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A good high foothold beside the hip.
  - A ledge or volume top you are already pressing down on.
  - The next hold far above, with nothing to pull on to get there.
  - Symptom: your foot is high but you are stuck in a squat with your
    backside hanging out and your arms pulling. That means the weight has
    not been moved across. **(judgement)**
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pushing upward before the weight is across [A21].
  - Kneeling on the shelf [A34].
  - Pulling instead of pushing on the handhold during a hand-foot match.
    **(judgement)**
- **Tall climber note:** Deep rockovers ask for more knee and hip flexion
  the longer your thighs are. Climbing.com warns that heel hooks near the
  pelvis while rocking over can strain the LCL without enough knee
  flexibility and warm-up [A10]. **(judgement except the A10 point)**
- **Illustration brief:** Two-pose sequence, climber from behind. Before
  (ghost): left foot on a waist-high ledge-like hold, left hand also
  pressing on the same ledge beside the foot (hand-foot match), right hand
  on a side-hold, hips low and to the right of the left foot. After
  (solid): hips shifted left directly over the left foot, chest over the
  knee, left leg straightening, right hand reaching up. A horizontal arrow
  from the ghost hips to the solid hips labelled "across first", then a
  vertical arrow labelled "then up". Centre-of-gravity markers on both.
- **Related:** High step, Heel hook, mantle (other chapter), Foot-first
  sequencing.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A8, A10, A20, A21, A31, A34, A41, A42]

### Heel hook

- **Also called:** heel, heelhook (German sources seen in search mostly
  use the English word **(unverified)**; DE *Fersenhaken* **(unverified)**),
  hooking (*Hooken* [A29]). Variants: high heel, low heel, side heel, compression heel, heel
  drag on volumes [A8, A15]; "heel perch" (rocking onto the heel) [A1].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V3. Forum posters put heel and toe hooks as
  "obligate" from V3 [A44], and one met a mandatory heel hook on a "3-"
  [A45]. More demanding heels (high, compression, tracking along a roof)
  are V5–V6 skills in gym clinic syllabi [A42, A43]. *The Climbing Bible*
  files it under "Specific techniques" (p. 68) [A48].
- **What it is:** Placing the back of the heel on or behind a hold and
  pulling with the leg, mostly the hamstring and calf, to pull the hips in
  or up, like a third arm [A9, A15, A41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Find the spot.** Look for dips, bumps or an in-cut lip where the heel
     rubber will catch, and choose the part with most surface. Check for
     heel options before you start, because they are often out of sight
     [A8, A9].
  2. **Place it as precisely as a toe** [A8].
  3. **Orient the foot:**
     - Point the toes down and away from the heel. This turns the knee
       outward and lets the calf and hamstring pull harder [A9].
     - Turn the ankle into the wall, rotate the little toe outward, and
       keep the foot about 10–45° off the wall [A10].
     - Keep the toe aligned with the direction of pull [A1].
     - By heel height: a high heel above you has the toe pointing up, a
       side heel the toe out, a low heel the toe down. Rotate the foot
       through these as you gain height, by up to 180° on big moves [A8].
  4. **Engage before you weight it.** Tighten the glutes and press the heel
     into the hold, *then* pull with the hamstring [A11]. Pull the heel
     toward your backside [A9]. "Never just slap your heel on something and
     expect it to hold or help" [A10].
  5. **Use it.** Hips close to the wall [A9]. Keep pulling as you move each
     hand. On a roof, track the heel along the edge as the hands shuffle
     [A8, A9].
  6. **Release:** first get the other foot on a solid hold, brace the core,
     and plan where the foot goes next. Then release *slowly*, because a
     fast release swings the feet off [A9, A15].
- **Variations:**
  - **Pure heel vs heel-toe press:** a pure heel holds by hamstring
    tension alone. If you add a toe pressing up against rock above the
    heel, it becomes a heel-toe cam (own entry) [A1, A8].
  - **High heel** (level with the torso or above the head) for reach;
    **low heel** (below the waist) for control and to free the hands
    [A15].
  - **Foot kicked out sideways** for lateral leverage when using side-pulls
    [A15].
  - **Compression heel on arêtes and prows:** wrap the heel round the back
    of the feature and hold it with the hamstring. This stops
    barn-dooring. On a steep arête you can use two opposing heels [A8].
  - **Volumes and slopers:** heel a screw-on on the side or top of a
    volume, or drag the heel on a flat surface [A8]. Gresham (indoor
    tips): use heel- and toe-hooks on volumes to stop yourself swinging off
    [A7].
  - **Top-outs:** on a rounded or undercut top, swing a heel up first to
    regroup [A8].
  - **Heel on a handhold:** shuffle or spread the fingers to make room, or
    put the leg over the arm on broad holds [A8].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Hold shape and position: an edge, lip or feature at waist height or
    higher, off to the *side* of your body. As a rule of thumb, heels suit
    holds off to the side and toe hooks holds near your midline [A21]. A
    foothold that faces sideways [A19].
  - Wall angle and features: steep walls and roofs, roof lips, arêtes,
    prows, volume edges, rounded top-outs [A8, A19, A21].
  - Handholds: poor (slopers, bad side-pulls) on steep ground [A9].
  - Failure symptoms:
    - You swing open (barn door) off an arête or side-pull [A8].
    - Your hips sag away from the wall on a roof.
    - You can only do a big move by jumping, whereas a heel would let you
      do it statically [A9].
    - You are short of reach: a 160 cm author uses heels "to make up for
      reach" [A9].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Slapping the heel on without engaging the leg [A10].
  - Imprecise heel placement on finicky features [A8, A9].
  - Giving up when the heel slips instead of changing the foot angle by a
    few degrees [A8].
  - Loose shoes or dead space in the heel [A8, A9].
  - Releasing abruptly [A9].
  - Overstretching on roofs, which forces you to cut loose [A8].
  - Skipping the warm-up [A10, A11].
- **Injury note:**
  - Heel hooks often cause hamstring strains, because they recruit muscles
    climbing rarely trains [A11]. Climbing.com links weak lower bodies to
    hamstring and LCL injuries. Heels near the pelvis with the foot turned
    out, done without enough knee flexibility, risk the LCL [A10].
  - Vagy (via Climbing.com) emphasises glutes first and the hamstring
    second, plus hip-mobility warm-ups [A11]. The five-tips article
    emphasises knee and hip flexibility and strength work [A10]. These are
    complementary rather than conflicting.
  - A UKC article on reducing heel-hook injury risk exists but was
    paywalled to me (see gaps).
- **Tall climber note:** Short climbers use heel hooks mainly to gain
  reach [A9, A15]. A tall climber will use them more to keep the hips in and
  stop swings, and less to reach. The heels you will find hardest are the
  scrunched ones near your hip, which demand knee and hip mobility [A10].
  **(judgement except the A10 point)**
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind on a 30° overhang. Right
  heel hooked on a large edge at chest height to the right, toes pointing
  down and away from the heel, right knee turned out (arrow showing knee
  rotation outward), a curved force arrow along the hamstring pulling the
  heel toward the backside. Left foot toeing a small hold under the body.
  Left hand on a sloper, right hand reaching up-right. Hips close to the
  wall, with the centre-of-gravity marker between the heel and the left
  foot. Inset: side profile close-up of the heel on the edge showing the
  shoe heel rubber catching the lip and the foot 10–45° off the wall.
- **Related:** Heel-toe cam, Toe hook, Rockover, Cutting feet, mantle and
  compression (other chapter).

  embedded on American Alpine Institute's page; uploaded 2013),
  https://www.youtube.com/embed/YxGpiR0KCms [A49].

### Toe hook

- **Also called:** toe-hook, toehook (also German usage), double toe hook
  [A12], toe scum (a variant) [A1]. Related: toe drag (laces pressed on the
  wall to keep the hips in on long reaches) [A4].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V3–V4. Forum posters list it with heel hooks
  as needed from V3 [A44]. Friction Labs: "typically found on moderate to
  difficult routes and problems" [A14]. Movement Gyms teaches it for the
  "V5 plateau" [A43]. *The Climbing Bible*, p. 70 [A48]. **(judgement:
  V4 is where problems first *require* one)**
- **What it is:** Hooking the *top* of the foot (toes, laces or velcro
  area) under or behind a hold so the leg holds the body in [A13, A41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Contact.** Use the top of the foot, not the toe tip. Fit the shoe's
     profile into the hold "like a puzzle piece". On deeper holds, hook
     higher up on the laces or velcro area. Try shallow vs deep, and swivel
     side to side [A13].
  2. **Ankle.** Here sources disagree:
     - Friction Labs and 99Boulders: flex the ankle toward the shin (the
       top of the foot at a right angle to the shin) and keep it flexed
       [A14, A21]. Gresham: hold the hook "by pulling with the shin
       muscle", and engage with full commitment [A12].
     - Kris Hampton (Power Company): do *not* flex hard and curl the toes
       to pull. He calls that instinct the main cause of failure, along
       with treating the toe hook as a pulling move like a heel hook [A13].
     - Synthesis: flex enough to *set* the hook, but hold it with leg
       tension and opposing pressure rather than toe curl. **(judgement)**
  3. **Leg.** Straighten the hooking leg if the geometry allows, by
     lowering the hips so gravity pulls against the toe. Bend the knee
     slightly on close hooks. Rotating the leg inward (as in a drop knee)
     helps [A13]. Once set, keep the leg rigid, because any relaxation
     lets the toe slip [A14].
  4. **Oppose it.**
     - Put the other foot under your centre of gravity and push away from
       the hook [A14, A15].
     - Or squeeze the hook and the *opposite hand* together, with the body
       hollow [A13].
     - Or stand on the same hold with the other foot and clamp (bicycle)
       [A12].
  5. **Stabilise, then move.** A toe hook is mainly a stabiliser. Once it
     is solid, move a hand or the other foot [A13].
  6. **Release.** Plan the release as small foot steps. If you must cut,
     take one foot off first, let it dangle and tense the core to deaden
     the swing [A12]. Alternatively, drive through the other leg, or grip
     hard and resist the swing with shoulders and core [A14].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Hold position: on or near your midline. Heels suit holds off to the
    side [A21].
  - Hold shapes and features: the underside of an undercut foothold, an
    undercling or flake [A4, A15]; a roof lip ("turn the lip" feet first)
    [A12]; arêtes and boulder sides, where it prevents barn-dooring [A12];
    ramps (track the toe up the ramp) [A12]; sideways moves across volumes
    or features [A20].
  - When a heel hook is too far away or awkward to reach [A13].
  - Failure symptoms:
    - When the same-side hand lets go, you swing open like a door. A toe
      hook under an undercut foothold transfers that load to the legs and
      core [A15].
    - On a roof, the feet drop as soon as you move a hand.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Curling the toes and pulling with the hamstring [A13].
  - Hooking tentatively, so the foot creeps out [A12].
  - Relaxing the leg [A14].
  - Hips held too high to find a stable position [A13].
  - Placing the other foot too far from your centre of gravity [A14].
  - Missing hooks that are outside your field of view [A12].
  - Going out hands-first under a big roof, so the feet swing off [A12].
  - Assuming you lack shin strength when the technique is the problem
    [A13].
- **Shoes:** rubber over the top of the toe box makes "a big difference"
  [A37, A25]. Velcro shoes and slippers hold toe hooks better than lace-ups
  [A14].
- **Tall climber note:** Hampton's "straight leg, hips low" set-up [A13]
  favours long legs, because you can reach toe hooks far out to the side.
  The cost is a longer lever, so more core is needed to stop a swing on
  release. **(judgement)**
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind under a 45° overhang with a
  large volume to the left. Left foot toe-hooking the underside of a hold
  on the volume's lower edge, near the climber's midline, left leg almost
  straight, the top of the shoe (laces area) shaded as the contact surface.
  Right foot on a small foothold below the hips pushing away (arrow), the
  right hand on a hold and the left hand releasing. A dashed "barn door"
  arc shows the swing that the hook prevents. Inset: side close-up of the
  shoe top fitting under the hold edge, ankle angle about 90°.
- **Related:** Bicycle, Heel hook, Heel-toe cam, Cutting feet, Toe pull on
  overhangs.
- **Watch:** "Essentials of Climbing: Heel and Toe Hooks" (Joe Kinder),
  https://www.youtube.com/embed/YxGpiR0KCms [A49]. Listen: Power Company
  Climbing Podcast, "TAPED TIPS | How To Toe Hook",
  https://powercompanyclimbing.podbean.com/e/taped-tips-how-to-toe-hook-the-most-misunderstood-climbing-technique
  [A54].

### Bicycle

- **Also called:** bicycle clamp; **reverse bicycle** (variant) [A1].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V4–V5. Sportrock calls it "advanced" [A25].
  Indoors it appears on steep walls with large holds or volumes.
  **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Clamping one foothold between two feet. One foot pushes
  down on top of the hold (toe on), and the other toe-hooks its underside
  [A19, A41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put the toe of one foot on top of the hold and press down [A19, A25].
  2. Toe-hook the underside of the same hold with the other foot [A19].
  3. Squeeze the feet toward each other. "All that matters is one foot is
     pulling while the other is pushing" [A21]. Keep the core tight.
  4. Try both arrangements (left on top and right underneath, then the
     reverse) before deciding it does not work. Which foot goes on top
     changes how easy the next reach and the release are [A12, A21].
  5. **Reverse bicycle:** one foot toes in on a *lower* hold while the
     other toe-hooks a *higher* hold. The top foot pulls away from the
     bottom one, and the bottom foot pushes away from the top one [A1].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - One large hold, flake, volume edge or roof feature where you can reach
    both the top surface and the underside.
  - A single toe hook keeps popping, or your shin tires. Clamping reduces
    the shin demand [A12].
  - Steep walls where both feet keep swinging off a big foothold.
  - If the push-foot ends up just above the toe hook, you are doing a
    bicycle whether you meant to or not [A14].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pushing and pulling without squeezing, so the feet don't oppose each
    other. **(judgement)**
  - Trying only one foot arrangement [A21].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile close-up of a 45° overhang with a
  big jug-shaped foothold. Right shoe toe-on, on top of the hold, arrow
  pointing down/into the hold. Left shoe below it with the top of the foot
  hooked under the hold, arrow pointing up. Two inward arrows labelled
  "squeeze". Second small panel: reverse bicycle with two separate holds,
  the lower foot toeing in and the upper foot toe-hooking, with arrows
  pointing apart.
- **Related:** Toe hook, Toe pull on overhangs, Heel-toe cam.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A1, A12, A14, A19, A21, A25, A41]

### Heel-toe cam

- **Also called:** heel-toe, heel-toe lock, foot cam [A33]; heel-toe jam;
  in cracks, foot jam [A41].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V5+ indoors, and uncommon. It needs a slot or
  feature of the right depth, which setters provide only occasionally.
  **(judgement)**
- **What it is:** Locking the foot between two surfaces: the heel presses
  on one, and the toe or top of the foot presses on the opposite one.
  Wikipedia describes it as a combination of heel hook and toe hook [A41].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Heel down, toe up:** set the heel on a positive hold, then rotate or
     press the toe sideways and up so it cams under a feature above. The
     foot locks in place [A1, A8].
  2. **Toe hook plus heel:** at the back of a roof, set a toe hook, then
     cam the heel against the wall below it to partly jam the toe [A12].
  3. Example: Rock + Run describes camming the right heel and toe in a gap
     beside a left toe hook on a steep problem [A37].
  4. Expect it to take a lot of weight off the hands [A1].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A gap or slot about one foot-length deep. Indoors, look for a hold
    sitting just under a roof lip, a hold tight against a volume, the
    inside corner between two volumes, or a deep jug with a "ceiling".
    **(judgement)**
  - Symptom: a heel alone slides off and a toe hook alone pops, at the
    same spot. **(judgement)**
- **Common mistakes and safety:**
  - Some cams stay locked in a fall and can damage joints [A1]. VDiff
    warns you "may break your ankle" if you fall from a strong heel-toe
    lock [A33].
  - Get a careful spotter when bouldering [A8].
  - Have an exit plan: un-cam before you jump off. **(judgement)**
- **Illustration brief:** Side-profile close-up of a hold set 15 cm below
  a roof edge. The shoe heel sits on the hold top (arrow pressing down),
  the shoe front (toe box) rotated up against the underside of the roof
  edge (arrow pressing up), with the two arrows forming a "cam" couple.
  Inset: the second form, a toe hook at the back of a roof with the heel
  pressed down against the wall below it.
- **Related:** Heel hook, Toe hook, Knee bar.

### Knee bar and knee scum

- **Also called:** kneebar, knee lock, knee clamp [A51]; light version:
  **knee scum** [A16, search snippet]. DE: *Knieklemmer* **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V5–V6 indoors, and rare. Friction Labs:
  learn it after the fundamentals, and the drop knee first [A17].
  Philadelphia Rock Gyms: knee bars are rare because they need a narrow
  range of conditions [A18]. Outdoors they are far more common [A16, A17].
  **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:**
  - Knee bar: wedging the leg between a foothold (toe) and an opposing
    surface pressed by the lower thigh just above the knee, so weight goes
    through the leg and one or both hands can rest [A16, A17, A41].
  - Knee scum: the knee only lightly pressed on rock. It takes some weight
    or sets up the next move [A16 (wording seen in search snippet)].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Find two opposing surfaces: a foothold, and a flat surface about one
     shin-length away (the distance from the top of your knee to your big
     toe) [A17, A16].
  2. Place the foot first, then wedge the knee and load it [A19].
  3. Contact goes just above the kneecap, on the lower quad or higher on
     the thigh. Never on the kneecap bone [A17].
  4. Dig the big toe into the foothold and lift the heel toward the knee
     surface, like a weighted calf raise. Engage arch, calf, quad and glutes
     [A17]. Use only "just enough force from your toe to your thigh to stay
     in" [A16].
  5. Let the weight sink in slowly [A18].
  6. Make a tripod: knee, toe and free leg on the wall. Flag the opposite
     leg against the wall to move [A16, A17]. Keep the core tight on steep
     ground [A17].
  7. **Adjusting the length:** point the toes to lengthen the leg. Rock
     onto the foot and angle the shin to one side to shorten it [A16].
  8. **Exit:** tighten the core, plan where the feet go, toe in hard, pull
     down on the hands. Walk the feet higher before letting go to reduce
     the swing [A16].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Indoors: large feature holds or volumes with a foothold about one shin
    below or in front of them [A17].
  - Two volumes close together [A7].
  - Horizontal roofs, where a good bar lets you use handholds you
    otherwise couldn't [A18].
  - Look for "a toehold to push against, opposite a flat surface" [A16].
  - In bouldering it is usually a way to free a hand or control a swing
    on a roof rather than a long rest [A18].
  - Symptom: your knee keeps bumping a volume or the roof as you step up.
    That is often the knee bar telling you it's there. **(judgement)**
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Using the kneecap [A17].
  - A pad that slips "a few millimeters" kills a technical bar [A16].
  - No exit plan, so the body swings out [A16].
  - Positions where the leg could stay jammed in a fall [A17].
- **Knee pads:** strap-on pads suit bouldering. Wear them on bare skin
  over the lower thigh, just above the knee, and secure with tape if they
  slip [A16]. Rubber pads add friction (Friction Labs ranks bare skin
  "bad", trousers "okay", pads "best") [A17].
- **Practice:** a double knee bar under a small flat roof 18–30 inches
  above a shelf, leaning back slowly into a no-hands rest [A18].
- **Tall climber note:** Whether a bar fits depends on your "shin-dex",
  your knee-to-ankle length [A19]. A tall climber's long shins fit gaps
  others can't use and miss the tight gaps setters size for average
  climbers. Use the toe-point and shin-angle adjustments [A16] to tune the
  length. **(judgement except A16/A19 points)**
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile of a 40° overhang with a large
  volume protruding from the wall. Right foot toeing a hold on the wall
  just below the volume's lower edge, right lower thigh (just above the
  knee) pressed up against the volume's underside. A straight double-headed
  arrow from toe to thigh labelled "bar = shin length". Left leg flagged
  against the wall. Both hands off and shaking, or one hand off. Inset
  close-up marking the contact zone above the kneecap, with the kneecap
  crossed out.
- **Related:** Heel-toe cam, Toe pull on overhangs, drop knee (other
  chapter).

### Toe pull on overhangs (keeping feet on)

- **Also called:** pulling with the toes/feet [A7], "drilling the toe" [A6],
  bearing down through the toes [A38], toeing in on steep ground.
  Related: **paddling** [A6].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V3–V4, once problems move onto steep walls
  with small footholds. Gresham: "On steep walls you must 'pull' with your
  feet rather than pushing" [A7]. **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Actively driving the toe into a foothold, and pulling
  back on it, with whole-body tension so the feet stay on and carry weight
  on overhanging terrain.
- **How to do it:**
  1. Place the toe precisely on the most positive part of the hold. Don't
     slap the wall with the balls of the feet [A38].
  2. Tense hamstrings, glutes, abs and lower back together and "drill" the
     toe into the hold. Concentrate on making the foot stick as much as on
     catching the handhold [A6].
  3. Bear down and squeeze, "almost pulling on the holds like you have
     prehensile feet" [A38]. Press hard through the toes [A37].
  4. Keep the hips close to the wall and don't let them sag. Move the hips
     before you crank for the next hold [A38, A37]. Turn the hip in and use
     the outside edge, or a drop knee, to keep the weight on the legs [A7,
     A37].
  5. Keep the feet loaded at the moment a hand moves, especially on poor
     footholds [A7].
  6. **Paddling:** with a big foothold out to the side, put the whole sole
     on a large feature (or the toe tip on a small one), engage the
     hamstring and pull your body in or steer it [A6].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Overhanging walls with small footholds below the body, where pushing
    would push you off.
  - Symptoms:
    - The feet pop as you reach.
    - You cut loose on moves that others do with feet on.
    - The hips sag away from the wall.
    - Hampton's example: a foot "picks off as soon as you try to weight
      it" [A40].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Standing passively on poor footholds [A6].
  - Loose hips or a soft core [A38].
  - Habitually jumping and cutting. Hampton notes gym setting often
    rewards "choosing instability" [A40].
  - Thinking more core workouts will fix it. Hampton says the fix is
    practising tension on the wall [A40].
- **Drills:**
  - Body-tension foot moves: keep the hands fixed on two holds on a steep
    board and make 10+ foot moves, 3–5 sets [A24].
  - "Hang and hold" and "bump it up" [A39].
- **Shoes:** downturned shoes help you use the foot "like a claw" [A6, A7,
  A37].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile, climber on a 35° overhang. Left
  toe on a small foothold well below the hips, a curved arrow at the toe
  showing it pulling back toward the climber's body (not pushing down).
  Shaded tension line running from toe up through the hamstring, glute,
  lower back and shoulder to the opposite hand. Hips close to the wall.
  Ghost overlay: the same climber with hips sagged out and the foot
  sliding off, labelled "push = pop".
- **Related:** Toeing, Edging (outside), Toe hook, Bicycle, Cutting feet.
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A6, A7, A24, A37, A38, A39, A40]

### Cutting feet and getting feet back on

- **Also called:** cutting loose [A41, A7], foot cut, going footless [A48];
  climbing with the feet off: campusing [A41].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V4. Gym V4–V5 problems on steep walls often
  force a cut, and controlling the swing decides whether you stay on.
  Gresham lists cutting loose, whether done "gratuitously" by strong
  climbers or through poor tension by weaker ones, as an
  intermediate-to-advanced fault [A7]. **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Letting both feet leave the wall, deliberately or not,
  then controlling the swing and getting the feet back on.
- **How to do it:**
  1. First ask whether you have to cut at all. Building the feet up in
     small steps, toe hooks or heel hooks often avoids it [A12, A7].
  2. If you must: release one foot before the other [A7, A12].
  3. Arch the back, bend one leg behind you and keep the other in front
     [A7]. Bend the knees to keep the hips close [A4]. Avoid hanging
     straight-legged like a "limp noodle" [A4].
  4. Tense the back and core hard to kill the swing [A7, A12].
  5. Use the reverse swing to lift the feet back to the wall, letting the
     momentum carry the foot in [A7, A4].
  6. As the foot arrives, pulse the lower abs so the toes stick first time
     [A4]. Aim to re-lock on the first attempt, because repeated tries
     drain energy fast [A7].
  7. From a knee bar or toe hook, walk the feet higher before letting go,
     which shortens the swing [A16, A12].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The next foothold is far below or behind you.
  - A roof with no usable feet past the lip.
  - A big dynamic move where the feet will come off anyway.
  - A foot is in the way of the next placement.
  - Symptoms:
    - Your hands rip off when your feet cut. One coach-poster asks exactly
      this to diagnose V2–V3 plateaus [A44].
    - You swing so far that you need two or three tries to get a foot back.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Cutting both feet at once.
  - Relaxing the core during the cut.
  - Straight legs [A4].
  - Fishing for the foothold repeatedly [A7].
  - Cutting as a habit when a foot-on solution exists [A7, A40].
- **Drills:** "climb with intention" (repeat problems you normally cut on
  until you can do them with feet on), "hang and hold", "bump it up / bump
  it down" [A39]. *The Climbing Bible* has a "Going footless" training
  section (p. 129) [A48].
- **Tall climber note:** A longer body is a longer pendulum, so expect a
  bigger swing and use the bent-leg, arched-back shape early. Long legs
  also reach the wall sooner on the return swing. **(judgement)**
- **Illustration brief:** Three ghosted poses of a climber from the side
  under a 45° overhang, hands on two jugs. Pose 1: right foot leaving first,
  left still on. Pose 2: both feet off, back arched, left leg bent behind,
  right leg in front, a short swing arc drawn under the hips with a
  "tense here" marker at the lower back. Pose 3: on the return swing, right
  toe landing on a foothold with an arrow showing the swing direction.
  Centre-of-gravity markers on each pose, swing arc labelled.
- **Related:** Toe pull on overhangs, Toe hook, Heel hook, dyno and
  campusing (other chapter).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A4, A7, A12, A16, A39, A40, A41, A44, A48]

### Foot-first sequencing ("feet follow hands")

- **Also called:** foot sequencing, building feet [A7], intermediate feet
  (DE: *Zwischentritt* [A29]), walking the feet up [A32].
- **Category:** Feet and legs (footwork)
- **Becomes important at:** V0 for the basics. It becomes decisive at
  V3–V4, where the best foot is no longer obvious and the order of foot
  moves can decide an onsight [A32]. Gresham treats planning the foot
  sequence as the third, "more experienced" level of route reading [A7].
  **(judgement on grade)**
- **What it is:** Planning and moving the feet so that each hand move
  starts from the best possible stance, rather than reaching first and
  dragging the feet after.
- **Rules of thumb (sourced):**
  1. **Legs do most of the work** (overhangs are the exception), and push
     with one foot at a time, like a ladder, not both together [A36].
  2. **The hand sequence decides the foot sequence.** When you pull off the
     left hand, the right foot carries the weight, and vice versa. Default
     to feet that preserve this cross-body tension [A32].
  3. **Choose foot height by the next reach:** look for footholds that put
     the next handhold "about an inch within reach", usually at knee height
     or below. High feet feel secure but cost energy to get onto and off
     [A32].
  4. **Keep feet low and move them often.** The Andersons suggest about
     three foot placements per hand move, adding intermediate feet as
     needed [A3]. Small, frequent steps save energy [A27]. Use an
     intermediate foothold instead of one high step [A28, A29].
  5. **Back-steps must come from feet in front of the handhold you are
     using.** A foothold behind that line calls for a drop knee and an
     opposing foot [A32].
  6. **On roofs, go out feet first** and hook the lip before the hands go
     [A12].
  7. **In route reading, read the feet too.** Assume unchalked rubbered
     holds are footholds, and plan the feet on a third pass [A7]. Map the
     feet before shifting them, for example: a high left handhold may call
     for a right-side foot to push off [A4].
  8. **On a project, memorise the feet** as well as the hands. The most
     common error is using different feet on the next attempt [A32].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Before every attempt, trace the foot path from start to the crux, not
    just the hands.
  - Symptoms:
    - You arrive at a hold at full stretch with your feet below your knees.
    - You keep "looking for hands" [A7].
    - You can't repeat a move you did last go (different feet) [A32].
    - You barn-door because the weighted foot is on the same side as the
      released hand [A32].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Fixating on handholds [A7].
  - Making big high steps instead of building the feet [A7].
  - Not looking at the feet when tired [A35].
- **Drills:**
  - Downclimb warm-ups, letting the feet lead and lowering with the legs
    [A3, A24].
  - Move a hand only after each foot is placed silently. If it made a
    noise, put it back and redo it **(unverified: seen in a search snippet
    from a gym article)**.
- **Tall climber note:** Your reach makes it tempting to skip intermediate
  feet and pull through on the arms. On V3–V4 that works until the
  footholds shrink. Rule 3 (next handhold "an inch within reach") gives a
  tall climber a concrete test for *lower* footholds: if the hold is
  already well within reach, the foot is probably too high and costing
  energy [A32]. **(judgement on applying to height)**
- **Illustration brief:** A wall "map" diagram from the front (no
  climber): six handholds (circles) and nine footholds (small triangles),
  numbered in sequence. Hands numbered H1–H4 and feet F1–F9, showing two
  or three foot moves between each hand move. Small inset of a climber
  from behind at one step: left hand releasing, right foot weighted (a
  bold diagonal line from left hand to right foot labelled "cross-body
  tension").
- **Related:** Precise foot placement, High step, Foot swap, route reading
  (other chapter).
- **Watch:** none found.
- **Sources:** [A3, A4, A7, A12, A24, A27, A28, A29, A32, A35, A36]

## Shoe choice as it affects technique (brief)

- **Downturned / asymmetric:** concentrates pressure on the toe (*Vorspannung,
  Downturn, Asymmetrie* [A27]). Helps you pull with the toe "like a claw" on
  steep walls [A6, A7] and helps keep the feet on as the wall gets more
  horizontal [A37]. Pointed downturned shoes suit pockets [A6].
- **Stiff vs soft:** stiff shoes are easier to stand on small edges for a
  long time but feel "numb". Soft shoes give more feel and smear better but
  are harder to stand on [A1, A6]. Gresham: stiffer shoes for beginners and
  long edging routes, softer shoes for bouldering [A6]. Soft shoes help on
  volumes, slopers and blank wall [A28].
- **Hooking:**
  - Toe hooks: rubber over the toe box matters [A37, A25]. Slippers and
    velcro hold toe hooks better than lace-ups [A14]. Toe-hooking wears out
    triple-strap shoes quickly [A25].
  - Heel hooks: ample heel rubber and a fitted heel with no dead space;
    tighten fully [A8, A9].
- **Fit:** snug with no air pockets, but not painful [A4].
- **Coverage gap:** I could not run the planned search on flat-vs-downturned
  evidence (search budget exhausted). The claims above are coach opinion,
  not testing.

## Evidence note: research

I found no peer-reviewed study testing footwork cues (quiet feet, heel
drop and so on). Adjacent research:

- **Hacques, Komar & Seifert (2021).** Over 10 practice sessions,
  climbers' exploratory hand movements and visual fixations decreased.
  Gaze became more goal-directed, and on transfer routes climbers explored
  more visually from a distance [A52]. Supports the idea that reading and
  rehearsal reduce on-wall fumbling. Not about feet.
- **Mitchell et al. (2020).** Expert climbing coaches made fewer, longer
  fixations than novices and looked at "distinctly different areas" of the
  climber [A53]. The abstract does not say which body areas.
