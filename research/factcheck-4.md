# Fact-check 4: the 22 studies added after the third edition

Checked 2026-11-17. The 22 studies and reviews that came in after the third
edition (E82, E85–E87, F66, F67 and G80–G95) had mostly been read as abstracts.
Each was read again, in full where a free copy could be had, and every sentence
of the book that cites it was set against the paper.

Method. Four agents took five or six studies each. For each study they looked
for a free full text through the DOI, the publisher's open-access page, Europe
PMC, PubMed Central and university repositories. They used no service that asks
for a login or an email address. They read who took part and how many, what was
measured, the numbers behind the finding and the limits the authors state, and
judged each citing sentence: it holds, it holds with a caveat the reader needs,
it is overstated, or it could not be checked past the abstract.

Result. 19 of the 22 were read in full. Eight sentences hold as they were
written and 13 were changed. No sentence was simply wrong. The usual fault was
a claim a step wider than the paper: "agree" where the trials are few, "any
sport" where a review looked only for randomised trials, "beginners" where the
paper says students.

## 2. What the papers say, where it differs from the book

- **E86.** A narrative review of 35 studies with no pooled figures. With loads
  of 50–90% of maximum, three to five minutes between sets kept repetitions up
  where one minute did not. It also reports conflicting findings: one minute
  was often enough between single maximal lifts.
- **F66.** Eleven trials with 225 climbers, but the comparison with climbers
  who only climbed pools four of them: 80 experienced climbers, a standardised
  difference of 0.41 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.80). Most of the six trials without a
  control group found no significant gain.
- **F67.** Nine studies and 149 participants aged 18 to 38, five of them in
  Chinese and four master's theses. It pools the change before and after in
  the climbing groups only, with no controls. Grip strength rose on average
  (0.81), but four of the seven studies that measured it show no gain. It
  never calls the participants beginners.
- **G80.** Thirteen studies of seven ways to land. All but slapping the mat
  reduced the impact. The authors' own caveat is that the studies used young
  people who fell on purpose. The four pooled studies whose abstracts were read
  were topples from standing or kneeling height onto mats, and for squatting
  the measure was the speed of impact, not its force.
- **G81.** 507 adult indoor boulderers followed for a year: 222 reported 305
  injuries. Eleven habits were recorded once each, none of which lowered the
  injury rate. Practising falls was not among them. The authors call for
  prevention aimed at falls and landings.
- **G82.** Thirty-four studies in the review, but only four looked at warming
  up, all observational. Three found no effect and one found more injuries with
  a finger warm-up. The authors rate the evidence as limited.
- **G83.** Looked only for randomised trials in English, up to about 2014.
  All 31 measured performance and none measured injury. A later trial exists
  (handball players, 2017) and is not in the book.
- **G85.** 22 recreational climbers, three warm-ups, no condition without one.
  Shoulder flexion rose 7–14°. The gains in arm strength were statistically
  significant but trivial in size, and elbow-flexor strength was slightly lower
  after two of the three warm-ups.
- **G86.** The −4.6% is the average over strength and power tests, for 60
  seconds or more of static stretching per muscle group in total, measured
  minutes later with no further warm-up. Where dynamic activity followed, the
  review found no clear loss.
- **G88.** Compares random with blocked order of the same skills. In
  laboratory tasks the gain is 0.92. In applied sports settings it is 0.23 to
  0.35 with intervals that reach just below zero. The abstract's "almost
  negligible" is stronger than the estimates.
- **G89.** 61 papers reporting 75 experiments. The pooled difference favoured
  less feedback but was not significant (0.19, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.43). The
  authors call the result inconclusive: they can rule out neither no effect nor
  a small one.
- **G90.** The 18% is the share of the differences in performance between
  athletes that goes with the hours of practice they recalled. It is a
  correlation, and the studies counted almost any practice meant to improve.
- **G92.** 64 studies of learners picking up a new movement, many on
  laboratory tasks. Watching a model plus practice against practice alone:
  0.77 for form, 0.17 for outcome.
- **G95.** 84 male students aged 18 to 19, two years of climbing. The
  programme added 15 minutes to each session: push-ups, pull-ups, forearm work
  and climbing technique, after a seminar. Shoulder injuries were 3 against 21.
  The injuries were reported by people who knew their group.

## 3. The sentences that changed

All are in `book/chapters/09-considerations.typ`.

| Source | Was | Is |
|---|---|---|
| E86 | Research on strength training agrees on long rests. | Research on strength training mostly agrees on long rests. |
| F66 | Trials agree that finger training works: across 11 of them, climbers who trained their fingers gained more finger strength than climbers who only climbed. | Trials suggest that finger training works: in four taken together, experienced climbers who trained their fingers gained more finger strength than those who only climbed. |
| F67, F66 | Climbing alone makes beginners' grip stronger, though finger training on top adds more. | On average, climbing made students' grip stronger within months, though experienced climbers gained more with finger training on top. |
| G80 | In laboratory tests, squatting and rolling cut the force of a landing. | In laboratory tests with young adults who fell on purpose, squatting and rolling cut the impact of the landing. |
| G81 | No study yet shows that practising falls lowers bouldering injuries: one followed 507 indoor boulderers for a year and found no measure that protected them. | No study yet shows that practising falls lowers bouldering injuries. One that followed 507 indoor boulderers for a year found no habit that protected them, but it didn't ask about fall practice. |
| G82, G83 | A review of 34 studies found no link with overuse injuries, and nobody has tested warm-ups against arm and shoulder injuries in any sport. | A review found no link with overuse injuries in the four studies that looked at warming up, and a 2015 review found no trial of warm-ups against arm and shoulder injuries in any sport. |
| G85 | In 22 recreational climbers, ten minutes of warm-up raised shoulder mobility and arm strength, though not finger strength. | In 22 recreational climbers, ten minutes of warm-up raised shoulder mobility. Arm strength rose only slightly and finger strength not at all. |
| G86 | Stretches held for a minute or more cut strength by about 5%. | Stretches held for a minute or more per muscle cut strength and power by about 5% just afterwards. |
| G88 | In laboratory tasks, people who mix up what they practise remember it better, but in real sports practice the benefit is close to nil. | In laboratory tasks, people who mix up the order of what they practise remember it better, but in real sports practice the benefit is small and uncertain. |
| G89 | … but 61 studies taken together show no such effect. | … but 61 papers taken together neither confirm this nor rule it out. |
| G90 | In sport it explains a real but modest share of skill, about 18%. | In sport the hours spent on it explain a real but modest share of the differences between athletes, about 18%. |
| G92 | Across sports, watching a model improves how people move more than it improves their results. | Across many skills, watching a model improves how learners move more than it improves their results. |
| G95 | In one trial with 84 student climbers, a strength programme for the shoulders cut shoulder injuries to about a sixth. | In one trial with 84 male student climbers, 15 minutes of exercises each session cut shoulder injuries to about a sixth. |

## 4. Still open

- **G80, G84 and G86 were read as abstracts only.** Their full texts are behind
  a login. For G80 the book now uses the caveat the abstract itself states
  (young people, falls on purpose). The heights of the falls come from four of
  the 13 pooled studies and are not in the book.
- **G81** was read as an abstract plus the first author's doctoral thesis on
  the same cohort, which has the same numbers.
- **G89** was read as the preprint, not the journal version.
- **G92's** manuscript lacks the two tables that list its studies.
- **G86 and the warm-up table.** The loss was measured without a warm-up after
  the stretching. The table holds the stretch-free advice anyway, since the
  dynamic movements it lists are what the same review recommends.
- **A newer trial on warm-ups and shoulder injuries** (660 handball players,
  2017) turned up while checking G83. It is not cited. If it is added, the
  sentence about "no trial" needs its date kept.
- Details the papers' own texts get wrong were left alone: G94's table lists
  116 women where its text says 115, G87's abstract counts six studies where
  its table has five, and G88's Discussion gives 0.28 where its Results give
  0.35.
