## Part 1 — Concepts

### 1.1 Static vs dynamic movement

- **Static** movement means reaching a hold slowly and under control, so you can
  stop halfway and reverse the move (a lock-off is the classic example) [D9, D18].
- **Dynamic** movement uses body momentum, mostly generated by the hips and legs,
  to carry you to a hold that is out of static range, or to reach it more cheaply
  [D2, D18]. The trade-off is that a missed dynamic move is hard to reverse
  without falling off [D18].
- Terminology (theCrag glossary): with one or both feet still on, the move is a
  **deadpoint**. When both feet leave, it is a **dyno** [D2]. *Bouldering
  Essentials* splits it three ways: a **slap** keeps at least two points of
  contact, a **jump** keeps one hand on while both feet leave, and a **dyno** is
  a full leap with no contact. It also notes that people use "dyno" loosely for
  any dynamic move [D20, D21]. Philadelphia Rock Gyms' coach points out that
  many climbers see no difference between a single dyno with a foot left on and
  a deadpoint [D15]. Expect the terms to blur in conversation.

**When each is more efficient**

| Situation | Better choice | Why | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steep wall, big-ish holds, a long move | Dynamic (deadpoint) | You spend less time hanging, and the arms get a moment of relief during the throw. The steeper the wall, the more efficient deadpoints become | D13 |
| Target is small, precise or fiddly (crimp you must hit exactly, small pocket, narrow slot) | Static, or a very small deadpoint | Precision holds punish aiming errors. Dynos to small pockets are flagged as a common cause of finger injuries | D20, D21 |
| A fall from the move would be dangerous (awkward landing, swing into a volume) | Static | "the control is worth the extra effort" (paraphrase of D20) | D20, D21 |
| Move needs precise foot or body placement to finish (slab, volume balance) | Static, or controlled momentum | Lock-offs are for moves that require precision | D13 |
| Climber strong on crimps who habitually moves statically | Learn dynamic | Such climbers "rarely cut loose" or pogo, which limits them on big moves | D40 |

Coaches broadly agree that momentum saves energy. Louis Parkinson calls it "a
HUGE energy-saver" [D38], and PRG's Dave Rowland argues dynamic movement is
generally more efficient than static climbing [D13]. Lattice's advice is to
"Match your climbing speed to the problem's style, whether it's controlled or
fast and dynamic" [D41].

### 1.2 The deadpoint idea, and the Güllich attribution (verified: partly wrong)

- **Idea.** Throw the hips up the wall and grab the hold at the instant the body
  stops rising and has not yet started to fall. For that moment you are almost
  weightless, so the hold carries the smallest possible load as you latch it
  [D1, D13]. PRG compares it to a ball thrown upward pausing at the top of its
  arc [D13].
- **Origin of the word.** The common claim that Wolfgang Güllich *coined* or
  *invented* the deadpoint **is not supported by what I found**:
  - John Gill used the word in print in 1969. His essay "The Art of Bouldering"
    in the *American Alpine Journal* describes a "dynamic layback" that "places
    the climber's hand on a hold at the high deadpoint of the swing" [D4].
  - Climbing.com's dyno history says the term comes from engineering's "dead
    center", and that Gill set the controlled deadpoint apart from the all-out
    "lunge" [D5].
  - English Wikipedia says only that Güllich "studied the use of the
    'deadpoint'" while training for *Action Directe* (1991). It cites a June 1987
    interview by Beth Wald, reprinted in *Vantage Point* (2018) [D3]. Güllich
    co-wrote *Sportklettern heute* (1986) with Andreas Kubin [D3]. I could not
    check whether that book describes the deadpoint **(unverified)**.
  - **For the book:** credit Gill with the first published description and the
    word. Credit Güllich with studying the deadpoint and building it into
    sport-climbing training, notably alongside his invention of the campus
    board [D3, D4, D5].
- German: *toter Punkt*, or the English loan word "Deadpoint" [D47].

### 1.3 Power from the legs and hips, not the arms

These points come up in nearly every source:

1. **Arms are a pivot, legs are a spring.** Both *Bouldering Essentials* and
   Gripped use the phrase "arms as a pivot and your legs as a spring"
   [D20, D21, D30]. Climbing.com's McColl article says to concentrate "fully on
   the push from your legs, not your arms" [D6].
2. **Start with straight arms.** Bent arms at launch cost power, and starting a
   dynamic move from a bent arm is a typical beginner error [D6, D13, D16].
   Rowland frames the dyno as rotating around the hands, as on a pivot, rather
   than jumping [D16].
3. **Hips lead.** Gripped: "Think of your hips as your centre of gravity". The
   hips steer the body during dynamic moves [D28]. Juliet Hammer (Climbing.com)
   writes that whether you stick a hold or fall away "often comes down to where
   the hips are in space" [D10].
4. **Order of operations.** Hammer's sequence for a deadpoint [D10]:
   (a) sag the hips away from the wall, opposite to the direction of travel;
   (b) push with the legs and drive the hips back into the wall toward the
   target; (c) pull with the arms only *after* the hips have started moving;
   (d) snap the hand to the hold ("Snappy hips are happy hips").
5. **Hips close to the wall and turned.** Twist the hips toward the wall rather
   than facing it square. This keeps you closer on overhangs and transfers force
   better [D6]. On one-arm throws, twist the hips toward the leading arm [D7].
   If the hips drift away from the wall, you come up short [D17, D54].

### 1.4 Momentum and timing

- **No pause.** Once the move starts, any stop "kills momentum" [D13, D15, D16].
- **Pre-launch pumps.** Sources disagree. Connor Griffith says to take "only one
  pump per dyno", because more pumps waste energy [D22]. Rowland says to avoid
  pumping altogether, or at most take one well-timed upswing [D16].
  *Bouldering Essentials* says there is no consensus and to do what feels right
  [D21].
- **Hands leave late.** Move the hands "as late as possible" in the launch
  [D20, D21]. On a double dyno, keep hold of the start holds until momentum pulls
  the hands off, roughly at waist level [D17], and release "as late as possible"
  [D14].
- **Only as much momentum as you need.** Extra momentum makes the swing harder to
  control [D20].
- **Eyes on the target** through the whole move [D6, D21]. On multi-hold
  coordination moves, glance at each target, then rely on memory and peripheral
  vision [D7].
- **Aim high.** Aim about an inch (2–3 cm) above the target, not at its edge
  [D16, D17].
- **Three phases:** setup, launch, latch [D30].

### 1.5 Latching (summary; full entry below)

- Catch with a slightly bent, "straight-ish" arm and engaged shoulders, not a
  dead-hang straight arm [D20, D21, D26]. Josh Larson's illustrations show
  catches in a slight lock-off [D7].
- Latch the best part of the hold [D6]. As the weight comes on, push down hard
  through the feet so they stay on [D21].
- Match quickly with the second hand when the hold is poor or the swing is big
  [D20, D21, D22].
- Catching at speed loads the fingers, elbows and shoulders heavily [D20].

### 1.6 Why dynamic movement matters more from about V4–V5 in modern gyms

**Evidence for the claim**

- *Coaching clinics place dynos among the V5–V6 skills.* Touchstone's
  "Gateway to Advanced Technique" is for climbers "consistently climbing around
  V5–V6". It lists "dynamic movement, and dynos" alongside drop knees, heel/toe
  hooks and mantles as skills "commonly found in V5–V6 boulders" [D36].
- *Lower-grade climbers typically lack the skill.* Louis Parkinson's
  dynamic-movement plan targets the "V1 - V5 area", because "it is more common
  for climbers of this experience level to have less confidence with dynamic
  movement" [D38]. Depot Climbing (UK) runs a "Fundamentals of Dynamic
  Movement" course aimed at climbers on their V1–V3 circuits who want to "move
  into the higher grades" [D37]. Movement gyms teach deadpoints in an
  *intermediate* class, recommended after 2+ months of climbing [D39].
- *Commercial setting has shifted toward comp moves.* Climbing Business Journal's
  2023 trend review found more "comp moves" set for ordinary members, with World
  Cup boulders adapted to lower grades. Setters judge a move "compy" by how
  dynamic and coordinated it is: "usually runs, jumps or dynos" [D32]. A 2025
  CBJ page notes that "Holds seen on World Cup boulders are making appearances
  on commercial V2s" [D33]. Climbing.com (2018) traced "skate-style" setting
  (big volumes, run-and-jump starts, parkour-influenced dynamics) from the World
  Cups into local gyms [D8]. Gripped (2020): "some modern gym routes can't be
  climbed without" momentum [D28].
- *Mechanics.* As holds get smaller, a static lock-off to them costs more and
  more. A deadpoint arrives when the hold carries "the smallest possible load"
  [D1], and the steeper the wall, the more efficient deadpoints become [D13].

**Counter-evidence and nuance**

- No source gives a hard grade at which dynos become *required*. A Q&A thread
  argues you "can't generally equate specific techniques to specific grades".
  Dynos are rarer at low grades but not excluded [D51] (search-result excerpt
  only).
- Comp-style moves now appear on easy circuits too (World Cup holds on V2s
  [D33]). The V4–V5 threshold describes where dynamic moves become *frequent and
  unavoidable*, not where they first appear (judgement).
- Some climbers dislike comp setting as "circus tricks" that don't transfer
  outdoors [D50]. The reader climbs indoors only, so this matters less here.

**Synthesis (judgement):** deadpointing becomes a needed skill around V3. Full
dynos, cutting loose and comp coordination moves start to show up regularly
around V4–V5. This matches the reader's plateau: all V3 and most V4 sent, no V5
yet.

### 1.8 Reading the wall: master checklist for "this move is meant to be dynamic"

Each technique entry has its own cues. These apply generally:

1. **Gap with no intermediates.** The next hand is beyond your straight-arm reach
   from the best stance, and no foothold or intermediate would let you move
   your feet up first (judgement, consistent with D2, D21).
2. **No feet where you would need them.** A blank panel, a dual-tex or no-tex
   volume, or a single low foot [D24, D32, D35].
3. **Target shape.** A big positive jug, a deep-lipped "catch" hold, or a pair of
   holds side by side far above you: setters use these when they expect you to
   arrive with momentum. Small crimps, pockets and slots at distance usually mean
   a deadpoint or a static move, not a dyno [D20, D21] (partly judgement).
4. **Holds only good from one direction.** A sloper or pinch that is only
   holdable if you arrive moving into it, so that momentum loads it in its good
   direction, but which you cannot load statically from your stance (judgement;
   cf. "direction of pull" [D41, D43]).
5. **Spread starts.** Start holds wide apart or high, a 4-point start with each
   limb on a marked hold [D35], a start hold far above the mats with clear floor
   in front (run-and-jump, jump start) [D8].
6. **Rows of holds or volumes.** Several holds in a line at the same height
   (paddle dyno), or a row of volumes (run across, step-through) [D12, D31, D35].
7. **Chalk and rubber marks**, and watching others: Gripped advises reading
   "chalk stains, thumb prints, and shoe rubber marks" and reading top-down
   [D25].

**Failure symptoms that mean you needed a dynamic solution**

- You can reach the hold statically but cannot hold the lock-off long enough to
  grip it, or you reach it with no strength left to close the hand (judgement;
  matches D13, D38).
- You touch the hold but cannot latch it, because you are already falling: you
  arrived after the deadpoint. Throw more from the hips and leave the hands
  later [D10, D13, D21].
- You pause halfway and the move dies [D13, D15, D16].
- Your arms are destroyed after two attempts at one move, though the holds are
  decent. You were pulling instead of pushing [D6, D26].
- You stick the hold but the feet cut and the swing rips you off. You needed
  cut-loose control, a foot catch, or a two-hand catch [D6, D11, D21].

## Part 2 — Technique entries

### Deadpoint
- **Also called:** dead point; "slap" for small versions (*Bouldering Essentials*
  [D20]); "dynamic layback" (Gill 1969 [D4]); German *toter Punkt*,
  "Deadpoint" [D47]. US gym slang: "throw", "snatch" **(unverified as
  standard terms)**.
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V3** (judgement). Deadpoints are taught in
  "intermediate" gym classes to climbers with 2+ months' experience [D39]. Will
  Bosi calls deadpoint climbing "pretty much the most valuable technique I've
  learned in my career" [D42]. At V4–V5 many moves between small holds can't be
  done as static lock-offs without wasting the arms [D13].
- **What it is:** A controlled dynamic move with at least one foot (and usually
  one hand) staying on. The hips are thrown upward, and the free hand grabs the
  target at the top of that motion, when the body is momentarily weightless
  [D1, D2, D13].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Stance.** Put the push foot on a good hold below and slightly to the side
     of the target, standing on the inside edge [D13]. The other foot is flagged
     or on an opposing foothold [D13].
  2. **Sink.** Straighten the arms and let the hips sag *away* from the wall and
     slightly down, opposite to the direction of travel. This "loads" the move
     [D10, D13].
  3. **Drive.** Push quickly off the lower or opposing foot to swing the hips
     onto and over the push foot. Keep driving up through that leg "as if
     jumping", arching the back to bring the hips into the wall [D10, D13].
  4. **Arms stay passive.** Keep the arms as straight as possible during the
     drive. Bend the holding arm only once the hips reach the foot, and then
     mainly to get it out of the way, not to pull [D13].
  5. **Throw.** Near the top of the rise, as upward speed fades, rotate the upper
     shoulder toward the wall and snap the hand to the hold. Aim slightly above
     it [D10, D13, D16].
  6. **Catch at the dead point.** Grab as you stop rising. Don't grab while
     still accelerating (you overshoot), and don't grab after you start falling
     (you can't hold it) [D1, D20].
  7. **Lock it in.** Engage the shoulder and push down through the push foot so
     it stays on as your weight transfers to the new hold [D21].
  8. The whole move is one continuous motion with no pause [D13].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The next hold is just beyond a comfortable static reach (roughly 10–30 cm
    beyond, judgement) and you have a solid foot to push from.
  - The wall is steep enough that a static lock-off would leave you hanging on
    one bent arm. "As climbs get steeper, deadpoints become the most efficient
    option" [D13].
  - The target is reasonable but small enough that you want precise contact. A
    deadpoint is more controlled than a dyno [D14, D45].
  - Low, flagged or opposing feet with a clear diagonal between push foot and
    target [D18].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you reach it statically but can't hold the lock-off
    long enough to grip; you hit the hold but slide off it (late, you were
    falling); you overshoot or slap the wall above it (too much drive, or the
    hand left too early); your lower arm is pumped after each attempt (pulling
    instead of pushing).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Starting from a bent arm, which kills power [D13, D16].
  - Pausing halfway, which loses momentum [D13].
  - Pulling with the arms before the hips have moved [D10].
  - Hips drifting away from the wall, so the throw goes outward [D10, D17].
  - Defaulting to lock-offs out of habit; common in newer climbers [D13].
  - Aiming at the edge of the hold instead of slightly above it [D16].
- **Tall climber note:** You will often reach these holds with a slow lock-off.
  Practise deadpointing them anyway: the cheaper deadpoint saves the arms for
  the V5 moves where a lock-off can't work (judgement). Deadpoints also put less
  load on the hold and fingers than a full dyno, which suits a heavier climber
  [D1, D45].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile on a 20–30° overhang. Two figures
  (ghost and solid). *Ghost* (load): right foot on a foothold at knee height,
  left leg flagged out behind; both arms straight, left hand on a side-pull at
  head height, right hand hanging; hips sagged out and down, 30–40 cm from the
  wall. *Solid* (catch): right leg nearly straight, hips pressed into the wall
  over the right foot, back arched, left arm slightly bent, right hand just
  closing on a jug about 40 cm above the old reach. A curved arrow traces the
  hips' path: out-down, then in-up, flattening at the top. Mark the flat top of
  the arc "dead point – grab here". Put a centre-of-gravity dot on the hips in
  both figures.
- **Related:** Dyno, One-handed dyno, Hip pop from backstep or drop knee,
  Latching, Generating momentum
- **Watch:** Lacrux, "3 ultimate technique tips from Will Bosi" (article built
  around Bosi's video; deadpoint is one of the three tips),
  https://lacrux.com/en/training/3-ultimate-technique-tips-from-will-bosi-climbing-training/
- **Sources:** [D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, D10, D13, D14, D16, D17, D20, D21, D39, D42, D45, D47]

### Dyno (two-handed launch)
- **Also called:** dyno, "mo" (1970s California slang [D5]), jump; German
  "Dyno" [D47] ("Dynamo" **(unverified)**). The term "dyno" was not coined by
  Gill and likely emerged at Mt. Baldy, California, in the 1970s [D5].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V5** (judgement). Clinics list dynos among the
  skills "commonly found in V5–V6 boulders" [D36]. Climbers in the V1–V5 range
  commonly lack confidence with dynamic movement [D38]. Practice can start
  earlier: the two-handed dyno between big holds is the easiest dyno to learn
  [D14].
- **What it is:** A dynamic move in which both feet (and here both hands) leave
  the wall. Legs and hips generate the momentum, and you catch one or two target
  holds [D2, D21, D30].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Pick the stance.** Both hands on good start holds. Feet on the biggest
     footholds roughly 60–90 cm (2–3 ft) below the hands, with the foot on the
     same side as the leading hand slightly higher [D22]. The farther the
     target, the lower you sink and the higher the feet should be, but not so
     high that you feel "scrunched" [D6, D21].
  2. **Straighten the arms and sink.** Bend the knees, let the hips go low, arms
     fully extended. Lock your eyes on the target [D6, D21, D22].
  3. **Optional single pump.** One swing toward the target and back down, then
     go. More pumps waste energy (see Generating momentum) [D16, D22].
  4. **Drive with the legs.** Push through the feet and knees and ankles
     [D7, D54]. Keep the arms nearly straight so they act as a pivot: the body
     swings up *around* the hands rather than being pulled up [D6, D16].
     Griffith's order differs: start the pull mainly with the arms, then drive
     hard with the feet once the start holds reach about chest height [D22].
     Treat this as a disagreement. Most sources put the legs first.
  5. **Hips in.** Drive the hips toward the wall and along the direction of
     travel. Arch the back so the hips stay close [D14, D17].
  6. **Release late.** Keep pushing down on the start holds until the arms are
     fully straight above you, or until momentum pulls the hands off (around
     the waist) [D17, D22].
  7. **Reach and catch.** Snap the lead hand (or both) to the target at the top
     of the flight, aiming 2–3 cm high. Catch with slightly bent arms and
     engaged shoulders [D16, D21, D26].
  8. **Hold the swing.** See Cutting loose and Latching.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The gap to the next hand is bigger than a deadpoint from your best foot can
    cover, and no intermediate holds exist (judgement; cf. [D2]).
  - Target is a big, positive hold or pair of holds, sometimes with a
    flat-topped or deep lip. Setters choose these so a moving body can stop on
    them [D22] (partly judgement).
  - Good launch footholds sit low and centred under the target. The wall is
    vertical to gently overhanging [D22].
  - Practise on a "gently overhanging wall", because slabs make knee and elbow
    injuries likely [D22].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you get halfway and run out of upward speed (bent arms
    at launch, or hips away from the wall); you reach the hold height but can't
    close the hand (arms too straight at catch, shoulders passive); you fly
    *outward* (pushed away from the wall instead of along it).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Pulling with bent arms to start [D6, D16].
  - Facing the wall square instead of turning the hips in [D6].
  - Multiple pumps, which use up the energy [D22].
  - Letting go of the start holds too early, so the hips drift away and you
    fall short [D17].
  - Hesitating; commit fully [D21, D54].
  - Practising on slabs or with a cluttered landing [D22, D21].
- **Tall climber note:** A long body gives a big arc around the hands. Sink
  fully: long legs give more drive, but only if you use the range. On low,
  compressed starts, don't jam the feet up high. Choose lower feet and accept a
  slightly longer arc [D6, D21] (judgement). Landing from a missed two-handed
  dyno is a feet-first drop from high, which is the most common injury
  mechanism [D44], so check the mats before every attempt.
- **Illustration brief:** Climber seen from behind, facing a vertical or slightly
  overhanging wall. Three ghost stages left to right, or overlaid. (1) Sink:
  both hands on two jugs at shoulder-width, arms straight, knees bent about 90°,
  hips low and back, feet on footholds about 70 cm below the hands, right foot
  slightly higher. (2) Launch: legs extending, arms still straight, body
  pivoting up over the hands, chest close to the wall. (3) Catch: both feet off,
  body fully extended, right hand latching a large jug 1.2 m above the start
  holds, left hand reaching. A dotted parabola traces the hips, its apex labelled
  "grab here". Draw the arrow for leg force through the feet *along* the wall,
  not away from it.
- **Related:** Deadpoint, Double dyno, One-handed dyno, Generating momentum,
  Latching, Cutting loose, Bailing and falling safely
- **Watch:** Gripped, "Watch and Learn How to Dyno at the Climbing Gym" (video
  featuring Ayo Sopeju), https://gripped.com/?p=23960
- **Sources:** [D2, D5, D6, D7, D14, D16, D17, D21, D22, D26, D30, D36, D38, D44, D47, D54]

### One-handed dyno / lunge
- **Also called:** single dyno, lunge (Gill's term for an all-out dyno [D4, D5]),
  throw, slap (small versions [D20]); "jump" when one hand stays on and both
  feet leave [D20, D21].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4** (judgement). This is the dyno type most often
  forced on commercial problems: one big hand move to a single jug. Coaches call
  it a "deadpoint on overdrive" [D15], and it is the natural next step once
  deadpoints are solid.
- **What it is:** A dynamic throw for a single target hold. In the basic form one
  foot (or one hand) stays on. In the "all points off" form both feet leave but
  only one hand catches [D15, D21].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Set up as for a deadpoint, but sink lower and look at the target [D15, D21].
  2. **Twist the hips toward the throwing arm** as you drive. On a right-hand
     throw, the right hip turns toward the wall. This lifts the throwing
     shoulder higher [D7].
  3. **Counterweight with the arm.** Swing the leading arm slightly back as you
     sink, then drive it forward and up with the chest leading [D7].
  4. **Keep the other hand on as long as possible,** pushing down on its hold.
     Keeping one hand on also improves accuracy to small targets [D21].
  5. **Throw at full extension**, arm long at contact, catching at the dead
     point [D7, D21].
  6. **Arrive and stabilise.** The free side will swing. Press the remaining foot
     hard, or use the counter-swing kick or a foot catch [D7, D21].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - A single isolated jug or big pinch far above or diagonally away, with no
    matching second target (judgement).
  - The free side has no foot, so a static reach would barn-door you open
    (judgement).
  - The remaining hand hold is good enough to push down on (a jug or rail, not a
    crimp) [D21].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you reach the hold and spin away like a door (hips not
    turned; see Counter-swing kick); you fall short by a few centimetres (hips
    square, throwing shoulder low); you get it but the remaining foot pings off
    (not pushing down as the weight transfers [D21]).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Throwing with hips square to the wall [D6, D7].
  - Treating it as separate steps instead of one fluid motion [D15].
  - Letting go with the lower hand too early (judgement; cf. [D21]).
  - Dynoing to small pockets or slots, which is a common finger-injury mechanism
    [D21].
- **Tall climber note:** You can often reach a single hold by turning in and
  stretching statically (drop knee, flag). Whether to throw instead depends on
  how much lock-off it costs. On steep walls, throwing is usually cheaper
  (judgement, from [D13]).
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind on a vertical wall. Left hand on a
  jug at chest height, left foot on a foothold below it. Right leg in the
  air, about to flag. Ghost position: hips sunk, right arm swung slightly back
  and low. Solid position: left leg straight, right hip rotated toward the wall
  so the torso is side-on, right arm fully extended catching a single jug up and
  to the right (about 1 m diagonal). Arrows: a hip rotation arrow (curved, around
  the vertical axis), and a hand arrow along a diagonal arc. Centre-of-gravity
  dot on the hips, rising along the arc.
- **Related:** Deadpoint, Dyno, Counter-swing kick, Hip pop from backstep or
  drop knee, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D4, D5, D7, D13, D15, D20, D21]

### Double dyno
- **Also called:** two-handed dyno, double-hand dyno, "all points off" (when
  feet also leave) [D14, D17]. Note: Method Climb uses "double dyno" for "a
  sequence of back-to-back dynos" [D35]. That usage conflicts with the other
  sources, which mean both hands leaving at once [D14, D17].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5** as a forced move (judgement). PRG notes the
  basic double dyno is "rarely needed" on real problems, because a deadpoint or
  single dyno usually works when the feet can stay on [D17]. It shows up as a
  set piece in comp-style circuits [D8, D35]. Learn it earlier on jugs as a drill
  [D14, D17].
- **What it is:** A dyno in which both hands leave the start holds at once and
  both catch the target (one or two holds), with feet on (two-handed deadpoint)
  or off (all points off) [D14, D17].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Both hands on two holds, arms straight. Feet in a front-step or toe-in
     position [D14].
  2. Knees bent, arch the back so the hips move into the wall near the upper foot
     [D14, D17].
  3. Press up with the legs and "spin" around the arms. Bend the arms only when
     the hips reach the line of the start holds [D14].
  4. Shoulders back to keep tension. Let go "as late as possible", roughly at
     waist level [D14, D17].
  5. As the legs fully extend, flip the shoulders forward and catch both holds
     [D14]. Lead slightly with one hand toward the better hold while keeping the
     body symmetrical [D7].
  6. Catch in a slight lock-off to check the swing [D7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Two holds side by side (or one big rail or volume edge) directly above, too
    far for a one-hand throw, with nothing for the other hand to stay on (judgement).
  - The target is too poor to hold one-handed with a swing, so both hands need
    to share the catch [D20, D21].
  - Start holds you can't push down on one-handed, so both must release
    together (judgement).
  - *Failure symptoms:* one hand arrives before the other and you twist off
    (asymmetric drive); you reach the holds but the swing peels you
    (catching with dead straight arms).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Releasing early [D14, D17].
  - Losing body tension (shoulders sag) in flight [D14].
  - Missing: a miss "will almost always result in a fall" [D14]. Plan the
    landing (see Bailing).
- **Tall climber note:** Your span makes double catches on wide-spaced pairs
  easier. Don't over-sink on short-gap doubles: a long body generates more swing
  than needed. Use "only enough" momentum [D20].
- **Illustration brief:** From behind. Ghost: hands on two jugs 40 cm apart at
  head height, feet on two footholds at shin height in toe-in, knees bent, back
  arched, hips close to the wall. Solid: body fully extended, both feet just
  off, both hands closing on a horizontal rail about 1 m above the start, arms
  slightly bent, shoulders engaged. Vertical arrow along the wall from the feet
  through the hips. Small label at the hands: "release here (hips at hand
  height)".
- **Related:** Dyno, Double clutch, Paddle dyno, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D7, D8, D14, D17, D20, D21, D35]

### Generating momentum: hip pump / swing before the launch
- **Also called:** pumping, rocking, "the bounce", wind-up **(informal,
  unverified as standard terms)**; dynamic weight shifting [D18].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V4** (judgement). Rocking the hips across
  between feet is the basis of high steps, mantles, deadpoints and dynos [D18].
  Timing a single pump becomes important once you start doing real dynos (V4–V5).
- **What it is:** Moving the hips before or as part of a move, side to side or
  in and out, to build momentum in the direction of travel, so the legs and hips
  do the work rather than the arms [D18, D28].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Choose the motion point**, the foot you will push from. Swing the hips
     over it first, dipping them slightly onto that bent leg [D18].
  2. **Dynamic weight shift:** when the footholds are far apart, push the hips
     quickly from one foot to the other, using the opposite foot like a small
     hop [D18].
  3. **One pump, timed.** Straighten the arms, use the feet to swing the body
     toward the target, then sink as low as possible at the bottom of the swing.
     Launch on the next upswing [D22]. Avoid repeated bouncing [D16, D22, D54].
  4. **Exaggerate.** On steep walls "feel" is unreliable, so deliberately
     over-swing the hips over the heel and arch the back so the hips come close
     to the wall [D19].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Footholds wide apart, so you can't shift weight by bending a knee and
    leaning [D18].
  - Sideways targets: extra swings may help build sideways momentum, at an
    energy cost [D16].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you start the move from a dead stop and fall short;
    your hips don't get over the push foot, and the move "spins out" [D19].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Multiple pumps, which waste energy and add downward momentum you then
    have to overcome [D16, D22].
  - Shortening the hip swing, so the hips never get fully over the heel [D19].
  - Under-arching, so the hips stay away from the wall [D19].
  - Sources disagree on whether to pump at all. Present it as optional [D21].
- **Tall climber note:** With a long torso and legs, a small hip shift moves the
  centre of gravity a long way. Keep pumps small and deliberate (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** Front view (from behind) on a vertical wall with two
  footholds 1 m apart at knee height and two handholds at head height. Three
  ghost hip positions: left of centre over the left foot (dipped), centre (low),
  right over the right foot (rising). A sinusoidal arrow traces the hip path.
  Label the left foot "motion point (push foot)". Arms straight throughout.
- **Related:** Deadpoint, Dyno, Pogo, Hip pop from backstep or drop knee
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D16, D18, D19, D21, D22, D28, D54]

### Pogo (leg-swing momentum)
- **Also called:** pogo, leg swing; "moon-kick" (Gripped lists it as an exercise
  [D28]). A search-result summary credited Ben Moon with developing and
  popularising it "per climbing lore" **(unverified)**.
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5–V6** (judgement). Griffith says pogos are
  "rarely necessary" and forced pogos are "uncommon in gyms" [D23]. Lattice
  notes that static, crimp-strong climbers rarely pogo, which limits them on
  big moves [D40]. Comp-style setting uses it more [D7, D35].
- **What it is:** A move in which you swing one free, nearly straight leg like a
  pendulum toward the target to generate momentum. The other foot pushes off a
  (usually low) foothold to launch you to a hold out of reach [D23, D24, D35].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Three points on: both hands on start holds, one foot (say left) on a low
     foothold. The right leg is free [D23].
  2. **Back-swing.** Extend the arms and sink the body as low as possible while
     the free leg swings back and down [D23].
  3. **Forward swing.** Swing the free leg up toward the target, keeping it "as
     extended as possible". A long lever with the hip as fulcrum makes more
     momentum [D23]. One search-result excerpt quotes a coach saying the leg
     is "mostly straight with some knee flexion" to keep tension
     **(unverified, source unclear)**.
  4. **Lead with the hips** [D7]. Point your centre of gravity at the finish
     hold [D23].
  5. **Push.** Midway through the leg swing, push off the standing foot and pull
     with the arms [D23].
  6. **Catch.** Latch with the hand on the same side as the swinging leg (right
     leg, right hand in Griffith's set-up). The arc carries the legs up, so
     engage the shoulders and hold tension [D23].
  7. It is "explosive but less violent than a dyno". Grace and precision matter
     more than force [D23].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - You have only one foothold, it is very low, and it lies *opposite* the
    target. Lacrux's example: next hold up and left, only foot low on the right
    [D24].
  - Slightly overhanging wall, wide start holds, nothing for the free leg
    [D23].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you push off the single low foot and stall halfway
    (no momentum source); you can deadpoint it but only from a foot that isn't
    there.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Bent, short swing leg (short lever, little momentum) [D23].
  - Jumping violently, as for a dyno [D23].
  - Not engaging the shoulders after the catch [D23].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs are an advantage here, since the lever is
  longer [D23]. Make sure the swing leg clears the mats and the wall; a tall
  climber's foot can strike the floor or a volume on the back-swing on low
  starts (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind on a 15° overhang. Hands on two big holds
  about 1 m apart at head height (left hand higher). Left foot on a small
  foothold 1.2–1.8 m below the left hand. Right leg drawn three times as a
  pendulum: ghost back-swing (behind and below), ghost mid-swing, solid
  forward-swing nearly straight and pointing up-right toward a jug 1.5 m above.
  Curved arrow for the leg swing, with a fulcrum dot at the right hip. Second
  arrow from the left foot showing the push. Solid figure's right hand reaching
  for the jug.
- **Related:** Generating momentum, Dyno, Counter-swing kick, Cutting loose
- **Watch:** Lacrux, "Pro tip: Use the pogo technique correctly when
  bouldering" (page built around a Tomoa Narasaki video),
  https://lacrux.com/en/bouldern/pro-tip-pogo-technique-when-bouldering-correctly/ ;
  Vimeo, "The Pogo • Understanding a Forgotten Climbing Move" (uploader not
  verified), https://vimeo.com/13761179
- **Sources:** [D7, D23, D24, D28, D35, D40, D52]

### Cutting loose and controlling the swing; getting feet back on
- **Also called:** cutting feet, cut loose, foot cut, swinging out; "barn door"
  (sideways rotation, a related failure).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4** (judgement). On steep gym walls at V4–V5,
  many dynos and big moves end with feet off. Lattice names failure to cut loose
  as a limiter on big moves [D40].
- **What it is:** Letting the feet leave the wall during or after a move (by
  choice or because they slip) and then controlling the swing so you hold on
  and replace the feet [D6, D11].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Expect the swing.** Vertical moves make little swing. Steep walls and
     roofs swing the lower body out after the catch [D6]. The bigger the
     horizontal gap between launch and target, the bigger the swing [D21].
  2. **Shoulders on, arms slightly bent** while the feet are off. Don't hang on
     passive straight arms [D27].
  3. **Don't fight the swing.** Arch the back and let the legs travel back with
     the momentum. *As the swing peaks*, snap the core and lower body back
     toward the wall [D6].
  4. **Reduce the swing** by tucking the knees toward the stomach or arching the
     back [D21].
  5. **Use the swing to get the feet back.** The farther back the feet swing, the
     more momentum they have to come back in. Bend the knees to keep the hips
     close and give the feet time in the air. Pulse the lower abs as the toes
     arrive [D11]. Avoid the "limp noodle": slow, straight legs that can't
     return [D11].
  6. **Target a specific foothold** with the toes on the return. If you miss,
     add core and swing again until the foot sticks [D11].
  7. Lock off or pull up as soon as you can to check the swing [D7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Steep wall, a big move to a good hold, and no foothold under the target
    (judgement).
  - A foothold set so that it can only be reached after a swing (e.g. high and
    far to one side) [D11].
  - *Failure symptoms:* you stick the hold but are ripped off by the swing; your
    feet dangle and you can't get them back; you only stay on with your feet
    if you lock off very hard.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Fighting the swing at its fastest point instead of waiting for its peak
    [D6].
  - Straight, slow legs ("limp noodle") [D11].
  - Straight passive arms and disengaged shoulders [D27].
  - Clinging to a slipping foot by jumping off early. Gripped advises *not*
    letting go when a foot starts to slip, because smeary feet often keep you
    on [D25].
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs swing with more momentum (good for getting
  feet back on, harder to stop). Tuck the knees early to shorten the pendulum
  (judgement, from [D21]). On low roofs, long legs may touch the floor or mats
  mid-swing. Check the clearance first.
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile on a 45° overhang. Climber hangs from two
  jugs, arms slightly bent, shoulders engaged. Three leg positions: ghost 1,
  legs swung out and back under and behind (feet off, knees slightly bent);
  ghost 2, swing peak with knees tucked; solid, both toes landing on a foothold
  at hip height on the wall. A curved arrow traces the pendulum. Label the peak
  "snap core in here". Centre-of-gravity dot below the hands, moving under them.
- **Related:** Latching, Counter-swing kick, Jump to a foothold, Campus move
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D6, D7, D11, D21, D25, D27, D40]

### Campus move (as a movement technique)
- **Also called:** campusing, footless move; "campus" (from the training board
  Güllich invented [D3]).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5+** on steep walls (judgement). Low-grade gym
  problems rarely force footless moves. Steep V5+ problems sometimes have no
  usable feet for a move or two. Thin sourcing here, and the search budget ran
  out before I could find coach-level placement.
- **What it is:** Moving one or both hands with no feet on the wall, using arms
  and body tension alone. As a *movement* it is usually short and dynamic. A
  forum definition: "'footless' dynos trying to do as big of moves as possible"
  [D46]. NBC: "Climbing using only the arms, usually needed when an overhang is
  so steep that the feet can't help" [D53].
- **How to do it:** (sources are thin; steps are partly judgement, built from
  the latching and cut-loose sources)
  1. Engage the shoulders, arms slightly bent, legs together and slightly
     forward (a slight hollow body) so they don't swing [D27].
  2. Generate the move with a small pull and a hip "pop" toward the wall, not by
     cranking one arm (judgement).
  3. Release at the dead point of that small upward movement and catch the next
     hold with a bent arm [D20, D26].
  4. Get the feet back on as soon as a foothold appears, using the swing
     [D11].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Roof or very steep panel with no footholds between two good holds.
  - Footholds that exist but are so far below or behind that standing on them
    pushes you off (judgement).
  - *Failure symptoms:* your feet keep ripping off anyway and you keep trying to
    replace them (the move is meant to be footless); you swing wildly after
    every hand move (not engaged).
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Letting the legs dangle and swing [D27].
  - Hanging on straight, passive arms [D26, D27].
  - Using campus moves where feet *would* help. On anything less than very
    steep, feet save effort (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** Long legs make footless positions swingier and heavier
  to control. Keep the knees tucked [D21] (judgement). Long arms can also
  reach holds that others must campus to dynamically.
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile under a 45–60° roof. Climber on two jugs,
  arms slightly bent, knees tucked toward the chest, feet off. Ghost: right hand
  on the lower jug. Solid: right hand latching the next jug 50 cm farther out.
  Small upward arrow at the hips showing the "pop". Label "feet off, legs
  tucked".
- **Related:** Cutting loose, Latching, Double clutch, Paddle dyno
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D3, D11, D20, D21, D26, D27, D46, D53]

### Run-and-jump (running start)
- **Also called:** run and jump, running start, "runs" (setters' slang [D32]),
  parkour start, skate start (cf. "skate style" [D8]).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V5** in comp-style circuits (judgement). These
  starts are common enough that IFSC setter Percy Bishton avoided them in a
  finals round because they had been "used a lot" [D8]. Commercial gyms set
  "runs, jumps or dynos" for general members, often adapted to lower grades
  [D32]. No source ties them to a specific grade.
- **What it is:** A start (or move) where you run a few steps across the mats
  or along footholds or volumes and jump, often pushing off the wall or a
  volume with one foot, to catch a hold you could not reach from a standing
  start [D8, D12, D49].
- **How to do it:** (step detail partly judgement. I could not fetch a
  step-by-step coaching source; the UKC piece covering it was paywalled [D49])
  1. **Walk it first.** Mark your take-off spot and the take-off foothold or
     volume. Count the steps (typically 2–3) back from it, and decide which foot
     hits the wall (judgement).
  2. **Shoes.** Softer shoes make short sprints and jumps on parkour-style
     starts easier [D25].
  3. **Approach light and fast.** "Run lightly" [D12]. Accelerate into the last
     step rather than sprinting from the start (judgement).
  4. **Push, don't kick.** On the wall or volume foot, push up and along the
     direction you want to travel. Engage the calves for big jumps [D12]
     (cf. parkour "push, not kick" [D48]).
  5. **Eyes on the target hold**, then let the arms reach for it [D6, D48].
  6. **Latch and stabilise.** Abs and toes on immediately to stay on after a
     lateral move [D12]. Find a foothold quickly.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - The start hold is high above the mats, with a clear runway of floor in front
    of or beside it, and no start footholds marked [D8].
  - A volume or foothold placed at waist height, or just the angle of the wall,
    serves as a take-off step (judgement).
  - Comp-style problems can span 8–15 ft (2.5–4.5 m) and block traffic.
    Setters leave space around them [D8]. Clear space is itself a cue.
  - *Failure symptoms:* you can touch the hold from a standing jump but
    can't hold it; you arrive with no momentum left to stay on.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Running into other climbers. Check the runway every attempt; these problems
    "can get in the way of climbers... when the gym is busy" [D8].
  - Stiff edging shoes [D25].
  - Kicking the wall instead of pushing along it [D48] (judgement for
    climbing).
  - Skipping the warm-up of hips, knees and ankles [D25].
- **Tall climber note:** You may reach the start hold with a standing jump or
  even standing on tiptoe. Do so if allowed. The added momentum of a run makes
  the landing harder on your knees and ankles. When you do run, the landing
  zone is the whole runway: plan it (judgement, cf. [D44]).
- **Illustration brief:** Side view of floor plus wall. Three footprints on the
  mat marking a 3-step approach (L-R-L). A fourth footprint on a volume at hip
  height on the wall, shown as the push. Climber (solid) in the air, body angled
  up and along the wall, right hand reaching a jug about 2.3 m above the mats.
  Dotted line traces the centre of gravity from the run through take-off to the
  hold. Arrow at the wall foot showing push direction "up and along, not into".
- **Related:** Tic-tac, Jump start, Dyno, Latching, Bailing and falling safely
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D6, D8, D12, D25, D32, D44, D48, D49]

### Paddle dyno
- **Also called:** paddle, paddles, multi-touch dyno; triple/double paddle
  (number of touches; search excerpt only [D49]).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V6+** as a forced move (judgement). A signature
  comp move. In commercial gyms it is a set piece rather than a common
  requirement. Setters' trade press discusses "tips to set paddles" [D55].
  Josh Larson notes that comps use such high-risk moves to separate athletes
  [D7].
- **What it is:** "A climber leaps and rapidly bounces their hands along
  multiple holds without losing momentum" [D31]. It is a multi-part dyno that
  builds or redirects momentum through intermediate holds before the target
  [D9]. After the jump, you move through two or more holds using only the upper
  body [D35].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Read the line: holds arranged in a row or arc. Identify the direction
     momentum must travel between them [D7, D9].
  2. Launch as for a dyno, aimed at the *first* intermediate [D7].
  3. **Hit each intermediate locked off** (arm bent) so you can move straight on
     to the next [D7]. Don't try to hold it; tap and redirect [D31].
  4. Sync the hands with the swing of the hips. Glance at each target and use
     memory and peripheral vision for the next [D7].
  5. Latch the final hold with the hand that arrives last and absorb the swing
     (see Latching, Cutting loose).
- **When to use it (reading the wall):**
  - Several holds in a horizontal or diagonal row at roughly the same height,
    each too poor to stop on, ending at a better hold [D31, D35].
  - No feet along the line (judgement).
  - *Failure symptoms:* you stop on an intermediate and can't restart (you
    tried to hold it); you run out of momentum before the last hold.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Gripping each intermediate fully, which kills momentum [D7, D31].
  - Looking at every hold for too long instead of glancing and remembering [D7].
- **Tall climber note:** Your span covers more of the row per touch. You may
  skip an intermediate if the rules (and the hold spacing) allow (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind, wall with four holds in a horizontal row
  60 cm apart at head-plus height, ending at a big jug on the far right. Climber
  drawn as a time-lapse: launch from two start holds at left; ghost hand
  positions tapping holds 2 and 3 with arms bent; solid figure catching the
  final jug. Arrow traces the hip path as a shallow descending-then-rising
  curve. Label the intermediates "tap, don't hold".
- **Related:** Double clutch, Lache, Dyno, Double dyno
- **Watch:** Gripped, "Crazy 24-Move 40-Foot Paddle Dyno" (Emil Abrahamsson),
  https://gripped.com/indoor-climbing/crazy-24-move-40-foot-paddle-dyno/
- **Sources:** [D7, D9, D31, D35, D49, D55]

### Double clutch
- **Also called:** double-clutch. Not to be confused with *Double Clutch*, a
  named outdoor boulder in Colorado [D5].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5–V6** in comp-style circuits (judgement, weakly
  sourced). One search-result summary of a UKC coaching article called the
  double clutch "a good starting point" among comp dynos [D49] **(unverified)**.
- **What it is:** Sources disagree on the definition:
  - Method Climb: moves that take you "from having both hands on the same hold
    to two new holds", one hand on each [D35].
  - Rock Prodigy forum: "when you dyno to one hold, and use that momentum to
    pull you to another hold" [D46].
  - A UKC article summary in search results described it as catching two holds
    at once [D49] **(unverified)**.
  - The UK Bouldering wiki glossary gives the traditional "double-clutch" method
    on *The Joker* as an example of a "2nd generation" hand movement [D57]
    (search-result excerpt only). This matches Johnny Dawes's dyno tiers, where
    a second-generation dyno goes again off an intermediate [D5].
  - Working definition for the book: a dynamic move in which the hands arrive
    in quick succession on two different holds, the second catch using the
    momentum of the first. Flag the term as used loosely.
- **How to do it:** (judgement, consistent with D7 and D35)
  1. Launch as for a two-handed dyno.
  2. Let the lead hand hit the first hold slightly bent and locked [D7].
  3. Use that hand to redirect the body toward the second hold, and catch it
     with the other hand before the swing reverses.
  4. Engage both shoulders and absorb the swing.
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** two separate target holds a hand-width
  or more apart that the body must reach in one leap, often with the start being
  a single hold matched with both hands [D35]. Failure symptom: you catch one
  hold and spin off it before the second hand gets there.
- **Common mistakes:** catching the first hold with a straight arm, so the
  momentum dies before the second catch (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind. Ghost: both hands matched on one large
  start hold, feet on. Solid: both feet off, left hand on a hold up-left, right
  hand arriving on a hold up-right 80 cm apart, with a small "1" and "2" beside
  the hands showing catch order. Hip arc arrow.
- **Related:** Double dyno, Paddle dyno, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D5, D7, D35, D46, D49, D57]

### Tic-tac
- **Also called:** tic tac, tac (parkour terms) [D48].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5+** in comp-style setting (judgement,
  **unverified for climbing**). I found no climbing-media source defining the
  tic-tac in bouldering before the search budget ran out. It is a parkour move.
  Its climbing use (pushing off one wall or volume with a foot to redirect onto
  another) is common in comp commentary but **unverified** here.
- **What it is (parkour definition):** a two-step move off a surface: step onto a
  wall or object and push off it to redirect the body upward or sideways, onto
  or over something else [D48]. In bouldering this means using a foot on a wall,
  arête or volume as a brief springboard during a jump or run-up **(unverified)**.
- **How to do it:** (from parkour source; climbing application is judgement)
  1. Approach at an angle to the surface you will step on [D48].
  2. Place the foot and **push off it, not kick** [D48].
  3. Twist the body out and up toward the target, throw the arms toward it and
     let the eyes guide [D48].
  4. In climbing, catch the target hold and get a foot on immediately [D12].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** a corner, arête or volume beside a
  run-up that offers a step but no foothold you could stand on; a start hold
  reachable only by stepping off a side wall (judgement). Failure symptom: your
  foot slips off the wall step. In parkour this happens when you stand too
  upright against the wall (search-result excerpt).
- **Common mistakes:** kicking the wall instead of pushing [D48].
- **Illustration brief:** Bird's-eye or 3/4 view of a 90° corner. Footprints on
  the mat leading to the left wall, a foot on a blank patch of the left wall at
  knee height, arrow redirecting to the right wall where the right hand catches
  a jug. Body drawn mid-flight, rotated toward the right wall.
- **Related:** Run-and-jump, Jump start, Jump to a foothold
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D12, D48]

### Jump start
- **Also called:** jump-start, jumping start, "jump to the start" (informal).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V3–V5**, strongly height-dependent (judgement).
  Shorter climbers meet it earlier, since "where tall people can reach, short
  people often have to jump" [D45]. Comp-style "4-point starts" add start holds
  for each limb, which sometimes forces a jump into position [D35].
- **What it is:** A start in which you leave the ground with a standing jump
  (no run) to catch the start hold(s), then bring your feet onto the wall.
  I found no formal source definition before the search budget ran out
  **(unverified as a defined term)**.
- **How to do it:** (judgement, built from the dyno and latching sources)
  1. Stand under the hold(s) with feet about hip-width apart and the hips
     slightly behind your feet.
  2. Sink into a quarter squat and swing the arms down and back.
  3. Jump straight up, arms swinging up. Aim slightly above the hold [D16].
  4. Catch with bent arms and engaged shoulders [D26]. Bring the feet up to the
     marked footholds as the swing dies [D11].
  5. In comp-style 4-point starts, establish control with every limb on its
     start hold before moving [D35].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** start holds marked but out of reach
  from standing, with no start footholds you can stand on from the floor. The
  holds are positive (jugs, pinches) rather than tiny crimps (judgement).
  Failure symptom: you reach the start but immediately swing off (catching
  with straight, passive arms).
- **Common mistakes:** jumping toward the wall rather than up, so you hit it;
  landing badly from a failed start (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** Often unnecessary for a tall climber. You may be able to
  pull on from standing. Check the problem's rules; some gyms set "jump start"
  as part of the problem (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile. Ghost: climber crouched on the mat, arms
  back. Solid: in the air, arms extended, hands closing on two jugs about 2.4 m
  up, knees bent; dotted line from feet to the footholds where they will go.
- **Related:** Run-and-jump, Dyno, Latching, Bailing and falling safely
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D11, D16, D26, D35, D45]

### Jump to a foothold (dynamic toe or foot catch)
- **Also called:** toe-hook catch, dynamic toe catch, foot catch (comp
  usage [D25, D35]).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5** (judgement). Gripped says dynamic toe
  catches show up "often" in comp-style problems [D25].
- **What it is:** During or after a dynamic move, a foot (usually the top of
  the toe box in a toe hook, or the sole on a foothold) is thrown out to catch
  a hold or volume, which stops the swing or sets the next move [D25, D35].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Plan the foot target before you move. Note which foot and which part of
     the shoe (toe hook vs toe on) [D7].
  2. As you launch or latch the hands, throw the foot out toward the target,
     using the swing. For toe hooks, use shoes with rubber on the top of the
     toe box [D25].
  3. Pull the toe into the hold (toes up, flex the shin) to check the swing [D7].
  4. Pulse the lower abs as the toes make contact [D11].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** a foothold or volume edge level with
  or above your hips after the move, often far to the side. A big move whose
  swing would barn-door you away from that side [D7, D35]. Failure symptom:
  you latch the hold but the swing peels you every time.
- **Common mistakes:** throwing the foot late, after the swing has started
  (judgement); stiff shoes with no toe rubber [D25].
- **Illustration brief:** From behind. Right hand just latched a hold up-right.
  Body swinging left. Left foot thrown out left with the top of the toe hooked
  under a volume edge at hip height. Arrow on the foot pulling toward the body.
- **Related:** Cutting loose, Counter-swing kick, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D7, D11, D25, D35]

### Lache
- **Also called:** lache (pronounced "lah-shay" in parkour) **(pronunciation
  from parkour source, unverified for climbing)**.
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V5+** in comp-style circuits (judgement). CBJ
  reports laches became a noticeable commercial gym trend by mid-2023, and are
  usually a set-up for the crux rather than the crux itself [D32].
- **What it is:** A parkour-derived move in which you hang from your hands with
  feet off, swing the legs to build momentum, and let go to fly across to
  another hold or surface [D9, D32].
- **How to do it:** (judgement, built from D9, D32 and the swing sources)
  1. Hang with arms slightly bent and shoulders engaged [D27].
  2. Swing the legs back and forth to build momentum along the direction of
     travel (usually sideways) [D9].
  3. Release at the end of the forward swing, when the body is moving toward the
     target and rising slightly (dead point of the swing) [D4, D20].
  4. Catch with the leading hand, then match or catch with the second hand to
     stop the sideways swing [D20, D21].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** two good holds (or rails) at the same
  height, too far apart to reach across, with no footholds between them
  [D9, D32]. Failure symptom: you swing but can't release at the right moment
  (too early: you fall short; too late: you swing back).
- **Common mistakes:** releasing on the backswing; catching with straight arms
  (judgement).
- **Tall climber note:** A long body is a long pendulum. Your swings are slower,
  so time the release later than shorter climbers do (judgement; basic
  pendulum physics).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind. Climber hanging from a jug on the left,
  feet off. Ghost legs swung left (back-swing). Solid figure released and in
  flight with legs swung right, right hand reaching a jug 1.5 m to the right at
  the same height. Pendulum arc arrow, with a "release here" marker at the
  right end of the arc.
- **Related:** Paddle dyno, Cutting loose, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D4, D9, D20, D21, D27, D32]

### Latching (catching a hold dynamically)
- **Also called:** latch, sticking the move, catching, sticking.
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4** (judgement). Once dynos and big deadpoints
  appear, the catch, not the jump, is often the hard part. McColl says holding
  the swing is "often the hardest part" [D6].
- **What it is:** The moment of closing the hand on a target during a dynamic
  move and absorbing your body's momentum, so the hold, shoulder and core stop
  the swing.
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Arrive at the dead point.** Grab as you stop rising [D1, D20].
  2. **Open hand early, aim high.** Fingers reach slightly above the hold
     (about 2–3 cm) and drop onto it [D16]. Latch "the best part" of the
     hold [D6].
  3. **Hand shape** (thin sourcing; partly judgement): have the hand already
     shaped for the hold: open for slopers and jugs, half-crimp for edges.
     Avoid catching pockets or small edges at speed with a full crimp, since
     dynos to small pockets are a common finger-injury mechanism [D21]
     **(hand-shape advice unverified beyond D21)**.
  4. **Arm slightly bent, shoulders engaged.** Not a dead straight arm [D20, D21,
     D26]. Gripped's jump-and-catch drill: catch with "engaged shoulders and with
     a slight bend in your arms", hold for 2 seconds [D26]. A catch in a slight
     lock-off helps check the swing [D7].
  5. **Feet push down** on their holds as the weight comes over, so they stay on
     [D21].
  6. **Commit.** Once you touch, grip fully [D20, D21].
  7. **Second hand.** If the hold is poor or the swing big, snap the second hand
     onto a second hold or a match quickly [D21, D22].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** every dynamic move, but especially:
  targets that are good only in one spot (latch the sweet spot); steep walls
  where the swing after the catch is large [D6]. *Failure symptoms:* hand
  touches and slides off; you catch it but the shoulder "pops" open and you peel
  [D26]; your feet ping off at the moment of catch [D21].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Straight-arm catch with passive shoulders [D26].
  - Aiming at the hold's edge, so you catch the lip badly [D16, D17].
  - Hesitating on contact [D20, D21].
  - Not matching quickly on poor holds [D21].
- **Tall climber note:** Heavier climbers load the hold, fingers and shoulders
  more at the catch (physics; cf. [D20] on finger/elbow/shoulder loads). Prefer
  deadpoints to all-points-off dynos where both work, and train the
  jump-and-catch drill on a bar first [D26] (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** Close-up of hand and forearm, side view. Three
  frames: (1) open hand arriving with fingers 3 cm above a sloping jug, arm
  bent about 20°; (2) fingers dropping and wrapping over the lip; (3) closed
  grip, elbow bent, shoulder blade drawn down (show a small arrow on the
  shoulder labelled "engage"). Inset: whole-body silhouette with feet pressing
  down on footholds (downward arrows on the feet).
- **Related:** Deadpoint, Dyno, Cutting loose, Counter-swing kick
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D1, D6, D7, D16, D17, D20, D21, D22, D26]

### Counter-swing kick (kicking a leg to stop rotation)
- **Also called:** counter-kick, flag kick **(informal terms, unverified)**; the
  failure it prevents is the "barn door".
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4–V5** (judgement). Comes with one-handed throws
  and side-pull catches.
- **What it is:** After catching a hold one-handed, or when one foot pops,
  you throw the free leg in the opposite direction to the rotation to cancel
  the swing, then flag or place it.
- **How to do it:** (partly judgement; built from swing-control sources)
  1. Anticipate the side the body will rotate toward. With a right-hand catch on
     a hold to the right, the body usually swings open to the left, away from
     the wall (judgement).
  2. As you latch, kick the free leg *across and against* that rotation, keeping
     it straight and close to the wall. A leg drifting out adds dead weight that
     pulls the hips away [D19].
  3. Use the leg as a lever: relax the hip, not the knee, and let it settle
     into a flag [D19].
  4. Snap the core in at the peak of the swing [D6]. If there is a toe-hook
     target, catch it with the foot [D7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** one-handed throw to a side-pull or a hold
  out to one side; no foothold for the free foot on the side you will swing
  toward (judgement). Failure symptom: you stick the hold and then swing open
  "like a door".
- **Common mistakes:** kicking out away from the wall; holding the leg still
  instead of using it [D19].
- **Tall climber note:** A long leg is a powerful counterweight. Small, fast
  kicks are enough (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind. Right hand just latched a side-pull up
  right, left hand off. Ghost: body rotating open to the left (curved arrow
  around the right arm). Solid: left leg kicked across behind the right leg,
  straight, toes pointing right and down, body squared back to the wall.
  Opposing curved arrows: body rotation vs leg kick.
- **Related:** Cutting loose, One-handed dyno, Jump to a foothold, Latching
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D6, D7, D19]

### Hip pop / "throwing" from a backstep or drop knee
- **Also called:** twist-lock throw, hip throw **(informal, unverified)**;
  related: backstep deadpoint, Egyptian throw. Bosi lists the "Egyptian" (drop
  knee) next to deadpoints in his top three techniques [D42].
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V4** (judgement). Clinics list drop knees at
  V5–V6 [D36]. On steep gym walls at V4 many moves are thrown off a backstep or
  drop knee.
- **What it is:** A deadpoint started from a *closed-hip* position (one hip
  turned into the wall via a backstep or drop knee). The hip rotates into the
  wall and up to throw the hand far (cf. [D10]).
- **How to do it:**
  1. Set the backstep or drop knee. The outside edge of the foot (backstep) or a
     dropped knee turns that hip into the wall [D10, D19].
  2. Sit back slightly on that leg, arm straight, with the hips sagging away from
     the wall [D10].
  3. **Pop**: push off the foot while rotating the hip into the wall and upward,
     "Snappy hips" [D10]. Twist the hips toward the leading arm [D7].
  4. Throw the far hand at the dead point, arm long [D7, D13].
  5. On steep walls, choose the foot carefully. A foot "behind the line" can make
     the lower-body lock fail and you spin out. Convert it into a drop knee with
     the opposite foot [D19].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** steep wall, next hold up and to the
  side; a foothold out to the side under the target (good for a backstep);
  side-pull hand holds that pull best with a closed hip (judgement, cf. [D10,
  D41]). Failure symptom: you reach statically with square hips and can't
  hold the lock-off, or you spin away from the wall.
- **Common mistakes:** shortening the hip swing; under-arching so the hips stay
  away from the wall [D19]; pulling with the arm first [D10].
- **Tall climber note:** Drop knees on short walls or compressed sequences jam
  long legs. You may need a higher foot and a shorter throw (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** From behind on a 20° overhang. Right foot backstepped
  on a foothold at knee height (outside edge), right hip turned into the wall,
  left leg flagged. Left hand on a side-pull. Ghost: hips sagged out, right arm
  hanging. Solid: right hip pressed into the wall and higher, right arm reaching
  a hold up and right. Curved arrow around the vertical axis at the hips plus an
  upward arrow.
- **Related:** Deadpoint, One-handed dyno, Generating momentum
- **Watch:** Lattice Training YouTube playlist "Improve Climbing Technique"
  (covers dynamic movement and twisting; individual video titles not checked),
  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6IUF1HrYUJ8&list=PLiZDfsSZ2zD0XvyD2SQzrCzzOFdU7uc06
- **Sources:** [D7, D10, D13, D19, D36, D40, D41, D42]

### Bailing off and falling safely from dynamic moves
- **Also called:** falling, bailing, jumping off, downclimbing (alternative).
- **Category:** Dynamic movement and coordination
- **Becomes important at:** **V0** for basics. **Critical from V4**, once
  dynamic moves bring involuntary falls from awkward positions (judgement).
  "If you don't know how to fall safely, you should not attempt these types of
  problems" (Gripped on comp-style problems) [D25].
- **What it is:** Controlling your body and landing when a dynamic move fails,
  or deliberately letting go from a dynamic position.
- **Evidence:** A 2025 survey of 245 injured indoor boulderers (301 injuries)
  found [D44]:
  - 85% of injurious falls were involuntary.
  - 41% happened during dynamic movement, 41% during static movement and 14% were
    voluntary. Dynos caused 22% of falls.
  - 62% of falls involved a rotation, and 73–74% ended feet-first.
  - 67% of injuries were to the lower limb, and ankle sprain was the single most
    common injury.
  - Most falls came from the top (47%) or middle (31%) of the wall, and from
    vertical (45%) or steep (29%) walls.
  - 94% landed on a pad, so mats alone don't prevent injury.
  - Industry view: the Climbing Wall Association blog calls comp-style
    bouldering the riskiest form of bouldering, and worries about newcomers
    trying it without knowing the movement [D34].
  - The authors suggest coaches train voluntary falls and teach athletes to fall
    "with their arms crossed in front and rolling onto their backs".
  - A Munich study (search-result summary only, **unverified detail**) found
    elbow injuries mostly from low-height falls into crash pads, warning of a
    "false sense of security" [D56].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Before each attempt,** check the landing: no people, bags or brushes on the
     mats, and note gaps between mats or between mats and the wall [D44]
     (judgement for the checklist).
  2. **Commit or bail early.** Decide before the launch what you will do if it
     fails. A half-committed dyno gives the worst falls (judgement; cf. [D21]
     "commit fully").
  3. **In the air, stop the rotation**: bring the arms in (crossed in front) and
     keep the legs together under you [D44].
  4. **Land on both feet,** feet roughly hip-width apart, knees bent to absorb.
     Don't land stiff-legged (judgement; consistent with the box-jump cue "knees
     bent to absorb the landing" [D26]).
  5. **Roll back** onto the bum and back, chin tucked, arms still in front. Don't
     reach back with straight arms to break the fall [D44] (arms rule from D44;
     "don't reach back" is judgement, consistent with D56).
  6. **Practise falls deliberately** from low, then mid height, including from
     sideways and from feet-off positions [D25, D44].
  7. **Foot slips:** don't panic-jump the moment a foot slips. Smearing feet often
     keep you on, and you can practise falling from that spot if it's safe [D25].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** every dynamic move. Watch especially
  for: moves near the top of the wall [D44]; moves that will rotate you
  (one-handed throws, laches); landings near the wall, volumes or gaps;
  run-and-jump starts that cross the runway of other climbers [D8].
  If a fall would be dangerous, choose a static method [D20, D21].
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Falling with straight arms out behind (cf. [D44, D56]).
  - Landing on one foot, or with a twisted ankle after rotation [D44].
  - Not warming up the hips, knees and ankles before a comp-style session [D25].
  - Assuming mats make falls safe [D44, D56].
- **Tall climber note:** For a tall climber, the hips start higher above the mats, and body
  mass multiplies landing energy. Downclimb or drop from lower when you can, and
  practise rolling back (judgement; body weight noted as a risk factor in
  [D44]'s introduction).
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile, four poses in sequence. (1) Climber
  peeling off a wall, arms coming in. (2) Mid-air, arms crossed over the chest,
  legs together, knees slightly bent. (3) Landing on both feet on a mat, knees
  bent about 90°, hips dropping back. (4) Rolling back onto the upper back,
  chin tucked, arms still crossed. A red-cross ghost shows the wrong version: arm
  reaching back straight to the mat. Arrows show the downward then backward
  rolling motion.
- **Related:** all dynamic moves; Run-and-jump, Dyno
- **Watch:** none found
- **Sources:** [D8, D20, D21, D25, D26, D34, D44, D56]
