## Part 1 — Terrain

### 1.0 What the wall angle changes (overview)

| Wall | Where your weight goes | Hip orientation | Hands mostly... | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slab (less than vertical) | Almost all on feet | Square-ish to wall, hips over toes, *away* from the wall | Press, palm, guide | Feet skate off; hands pull and lever feet off [E1, E3, E4] |
| Vertical | Mostly on feet if hips are close to the wall | Hips in toward the wall *and* over the standing heel | Hang on straight arms | Hips drift out, arms over-grip [E10] |
| Overhang / steep | Shared; arms carry more as the angle increases | Turned sideways into the wall (outside edge, backstep, drop knee) | Straight arms, pull on feet | Square hips, bent arms, feet cut [E7, E8, E9, E11] |
| Roof | Arms plus hooking feet | Body horizontal; feet pull (toe/heel hooks) | Straight arms; hooks act as "third arm" | Feet swing off [E7, snippet E15] |
| Arête | Split between the arête and the side wall | About 45° to the arête | Layback/pinch the edge | Barn door [E2] |
| Corner / dihedral | Pressed outward into both walls | Facing out or into the corner, legs bridged | Press or rest | Not bridging, so the arms work [E20, E24, snippet E17] |

Mckellar et al. define "slab" for their study as a wall angle "greater than 90
degrees from horizontal" (i.e. leaning back from vertical, measured on the
climber's side) and add a "slab-like" category: walls slightly steeper than
vertical where big volumes make climbers mostly *push* on holds or allow
hands-free positions [E6]. That is a useful reading cue: **if the problem mostly
asks you to push, climb it with slab technique even if the wall is vertical.**

### Slab technique

- **Also called:** friction climbing, smearing (US/UK); German *Plattenklettern* (unverified).
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V3 (judgement). At V0–V1 slab problems usually
  have positive footholds. By V2–V4, gym circuits start adding "complexity and
  risk" [E52], and setters use flat or sloping holds paired with tricky footwork
  even on easy grades [E46]. Gyms that set many vertical and slab walls do so
  specifically to teach smearing and trusting the feet [E52].
- **What it is:** Climbing a less-than-vertical wall by keeping your weight over
  your feet and using friction and precise foot placements, with the hands
  mainly for balance and pressing rather than pulling [E1, E3, E24].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Before each step, move your hips over the foot you are about to stand
     on**, then stand up. Shifting the weight first presses the shoe onto the
     hold [E3].
  2. **Keep hips back over your toes, not pressed into the wall.** Leaning your
     chest into a low-angle slab pushes the feet outward and off. Pull the hips
     back so the force goes *down* through the shoe [E3, E24].
  3. **Smear with heels low.** On a smear, drop the heel so more of the
     forefoot's rubber touches the wall [E1, E3, snippet E16]. Stand on holds
     with the big toe rather than the side of the foot, so you can pivot and
     raise or drop the heel [E3]. On edges, stand on the toe part of
     the edge (Gresham suggests about 45° to the wall for most edging) [snippet E16].
  4. **Commit full weight to the foot in one go.** Half-weighting makes the shoe
     slip [E1]. Keep even, constant pressure through the move [snippet E16].
     Don't test a foothold over and over [snippet E16].
  5. **Take several small steps instead of one big high step** [snippet E16].
  6. **Use the hands as guide rails, not anchors.** Slab handholds are often
     bad because they are not meant to be pulled hard. Approach from the side
     and use the hands to guide your body over the foot [E4]. Where there are
     no holds, press a palm flat on the wall, sometimes fingers pointing down,
     for friction and push [E3, E1].
  7. **Press out on palms to gain height.** Straighten the arms as you rise
     (mantle-style), with the hips as close to the wall as you can while
     pressing [E1].
  8. **Rest a pumped calf** by placing a heel on a hold (toe pointing down and
     away from the wall), leaning in so the bone takes the weight, then shaking
     out the other leg [E1].
  9. **Move slowly** so you can feel each balance point [E3].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** wall leans back from vertical; few or
  no positive footholds, just smears, small edges or big flat volumes; hand
  holds are slopers, small or point the "wrong" way; volumes that make you push
  more than pull [E6]. **Failure symptoms:** foot skates as you stand up;
  you're standing on a foot but can't let go with a hand; your calves burn;
  you grip handholds hard but they don't help.
- **Common mistakes:**
  - Placing feet *near* a hold rather than on its best part [E1].
  - Lifting heels on smears [E1].
  - Tentative weighting [E1, snippet E16].
  - Leaning in (chest to wall) on low-angle slab [E3].
  - Pulling hard on small handholds [E4].
  - Overly tight shoes that won't flex for smearing [E1]. Sources differ on
    stiffness: [E1, E5] favour softer, flexible shoes for smearing, while one
    search-result source claimed stiff shoes grip better when smearing
    **(unverified; source not identified)**.
- **Tall climber note:** long legs make high steps easier to reach but a
  deeper crouch puts the hips further behind the feet. Keep high steps modest
  and use more, smaller steps (judgement, from [snippet E16]).
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile of a climber on a 75° slab. Left foot
  smearing with heel dropped (show a small angle arc between shoe sole and
  wall, heel below toe). Right foot on a small edge with the toe tip. Hips
  directly above the toes of the left foot (dashed vertical plumb line from
  the hips down to the left forefoot, centre-of-gravity dot on that line).
  Right palm flat on the wall at chest height, fingers pointing down. Left hand
  lightly on a sloper above. Arrow showing force going *down* through the left
  shoe into the wall. A ghost figure leaning chest-to-wall with an arrow showing
  the foot being pushed out, labelled "leaning in pushes feet off".
- **Related:** smearing, edging, heel drop, palming, rock-over, mantle,
  dynamic slab (below), volume technique.
- **Watch:** see Dynamic slab.
- **Sources:** [E1, E3, E4, E5, E6, E16, E24, E46, E52]

### Dynamic slab (comp-style slab)

- **Also called:** comp slab, coordination slab, run-and-jump (one move type),
  volume walk (one move type).
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V4–V6 indoors (judgement). Gym circuits shift
  around V4–V6 from teaching skills to testing them with dynamic moves [E52].
  At elite level, 18% of the 2019 World Cup finals boulders contained a
  coordination move [E6].
- **What it is:** Slab or slab-like problems (often on big volumes) that need
  fast, multi-limb movement: jumping or running between volumes, catching
  holds or footholds in motion, rather than slow balance [E5, E6, E19]. Mckellar
  et al. defined a coordination move as at least three limb movements, each
  contacting a hold, within less than 2 seconds [E6].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Read the end position first.** Work out where your body must be when
     the sequence finishes, then plan backwards: "plan footsteps in reverse"
     (Narasaki) [E27]. Pardy also advises reading comp boulders from the top
     down [E5].
  2. **Plan the move in three dimensions.** Decide how far from the wall you
     launch, and set the angle and arc so you arrive balanced: not chest
     against the wall and not overstretched [E5, E27].
  3. **On volumes, stand on the far side** (the side away from the wall) so
     your hips sit over your feet. Smear with the front half of the shoe, heels
     lower than toes, and make small precise adjustments [E5].
  4. **Stay relaxed between efforts**, tensing only at the moment of catching
     a hold. Tension spoils balance [E5, E27].
  5. **Think "pull the body into the final position"** rather than "grab the
     hold" [E27].
  6. **Don't bail when a foot slips.** Practise riding out the slip if the fall
     is safe [E5].
  7. **Break long coordination sequences into pieces.** Nail the first contact,
     then add the second, then the third [E5].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** big volumes with no obvious holds;
  holds spaced further apart than you can step statically; a single hand hold
  or a foothold on a far volume that you can only reach in motion; elite comp
  problems that elite climbers solved with a running start. **Failure
  symptoms:** you arrive at the target with the right foot but your body is too
  close to the wall and you topple back; you can static to the next position
  but not stop there.
- **Common mistakes:** jumping without having pictured the landing position;
  launching straight at the wall instead of along the wall; tensing the whole
  body throughout; giving up after a foot slip [E5, E27].
- **Safety:** these problems involve unusual positions and frequent falls; warm
  up hips, knees and ankles as well as fingers, and get fall training first [E5].
- **Tall climber note:** none found in sources.
- **Illustration brief:** Climber seen from behind, two large triangular
  volumes on a vertical wall. Ghost 1: standing on the left volume's far face,
  hips over the left foot. Arrow (curved, labelled "arc stays parallel to
  wall") to Ghost 2: right foot landing on the far face of the right volume,
  right hand slapping a sloper above it, left hand releasing. Final pose solid:
  hips over the right foot, body about a forearm's length off the wall. Small
  numbers 1–2–3 marking the order of limb contacts.
- **Related:** slab technique, volume technique, deadpoint, dyno, run-and-jump.
- **Watch:** Hannah Morris Bouldering, slab masterclass with World Cup climber
  Jenya Kazbekova ("Climbing with World Cup pro climber Jenya Kazbekova"),
  found only via a mirror listing: https://yt.d0.cx/v/yt/7j7DDLxI2YA (YouTube ID
  7j7DDLxI2YA). Gripped, "Top Olympian Tomoa Narasaki Teaches Coordination"
  (embeds the video): https://gripped.com/?p=40443
- **Sources:** [E5, E6, E19, E27, E28, E52]

### Vertical walls (free-form)

- The key change from slab: on vertical ground gravity pulls the hips away from
  the wall, so you can't balance fully over one foot. Before each move bring the
  hips **in toward the wall *and* over the heel of the standing foot**. New
  climbers only think "left/right" and leave the hips slightly out, which loads
  the arms and causes over-gripping [E10].
- Default foot technique is inside edge with hips more or less square; switch to
  the outside edge with crossed legs when holds line up, and flag instead of
  swapping feet when they don't [E12].
- On a volume on a vertical wall, the most balanced spot is often *further*
  from the wall, so you lean in instead of being pushed backward [E18].
- Movement as a rule of thumb: "hand placement drives body position" — decide
  which hand takes each hold, then let the hands set your direction [E32].

### Steep-wall technique (overhangs)

- **Also called:** hips in, twist lock + backstep (Hörst), body positioning on
  steep ground.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V3–V4 (judgement). Hörst recommends learning the
  twist lock and backstep on boulder problems about 20° past vertical before
  steeper ground [E9]. Gresham lists "bent arms with square hips on steep
  ground" among beginner-to-intermediate faults [E11].
- **What it is:** Turning the hips sideways into an overhanging wall, keeping the
  arms straight and loading the feet so the legs and body rotation do the work
  [E9, E11].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Feet up.** As the wall steepens, bring your feet higher so your legs keep
     weight [E8].
  2. **Turn the hip of the reaching side into the wall.** Place the outside
     edge of that foot on a hold below or behind your body (the backstep) [E9].
     Brace the other foot to form a stable tripod [E11].
  3. **Straight arms.** Hang from the holding arm; pull the opposite arm down
     and in to the torso to "lock" the twist [E9]. Between moves, keep the arms
     straight so back and shoulders carry the load [E7, E8].
  4. **Move the hips first, then pull** — don't lead with the head [E8].
  5. **Pull with the feet:** press toes down and in "as if gripping with them",
     not slapping the ball of the foot onto the hold [E8, E11]. Keep the feet
     loaded through the move [E11].
  6. **Use same-side pushing:** reaching with the right hand, push off the right
     foot [E8].
  7. Use a **drop knee** (turn one knee in and down, twist toward the other
     foot) to extend reach statically [E7, E20].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** wall past vertical; footholds below and
  to the side of the hand holds; sidepulls; a hold you can only reach "just" with
  square hips. **Failure symptoms:** arms pumped quickly; feet cut when you
  reach; reach is a few cm short; you swing out after a move.
- **Common mistakes:** square "frog" hips on steep ground [E11, E12]; bent arms
  and pulling in [E8]; low feet and high hands (stretched) [E8]; slapping feet
  [E8]; swinging and dynamic moves as a crutch [E8]; zig-zagging hips instead of
  snapping into position [E11].
- **Tall climber note:** none in sources.
- **Illustration brief:** Climber seen from behind on a 30° overhang. Left hand
  on a jug at head height, arm straight. Right foot on a hold below the left
  hand using the outside edge (knee pointing left), right hip touching the wall;
  left foot on the inside edge out to the left. Right hand reaching up and right
  with a dashed ghost showing the square-hips position reaching less high.
  Arrow on the left arm pulling down and in. Centre-of-gravity dot just above
  the right foot, close to the wall.
- **Related:** backstep, outside edge, drop knee, flagging, toe hook, heel hook,
  twist lock, body tension.
- **Sources:** [E7, E8, E9, E11, E12, E20]

### Roof technique

- **Also called:** roof climbing, cave climbing, turning the lip.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V4–V5 indoors (judgement): roofs at low grades
  usually have jugs and positive feet, and hooking feet become necessary once
  the footholds face down or sideways [E7, snippet E15].
- **What it is:** Climbing a near-horizontal ceiling, where feet stay on only if
  they pull (toe hooks, heel hooks, bicycles) and the exit needs a heel-hook
  over the lip [E7, E13, E15].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Keep feet on by pulling, not pushing.** Toe-hook the underside of a
     hold (flex the foot, pull the toes toward you, engage the shin) [E7, E20].
  2. **Bicycle:** toe down on top of a foothold with one foot while
     toe-hooking the same hold (or one nearby) with the other, and squeeze
     both feet together [E7, snippet E15].
  3. **Heel-hook as a "third arm"** along breaks and on the lip; a succession of
     heel hooks along a horizontal break is common [snippet E15, E13].
  4. **Plan how the toe comes out** before you set it, and move the feet in
     small steps so you don't cut loose when you take the toe out [snippet E15].
  5. **Look sideways for hooks:** toe hooks around the sides are easy to miss
     because they're out of your normal field of view [snippet E15].
  6. On a big roof, going **feet first** (inverting, high toe hook, then
     tracking the body round) often works [snippet E15].
  7. **Turning the lip:** heel-hook the lip first, then rock over [E13, E7].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** holds on the ceiling; footholds that
  face down or sideways; undercut flakes you can snag with a toe [snippet E15].
  **Failure symptoms:** feet swing off as soon as you move a hand; you hang
  feet-free at the lip.
- **Common mistakes:** pushing on footholds that face down; missing side toe
  hooks; setting a toe hook with no plan to release it [snippet E15].
- **Equipment note:** down-turned shoes clamp better than flat ones [snippet
  E15]; rubber on top of the toe box helps toe hooks [E5].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile, roof at 10° from horizontal. Climber's
  body horizontal under the roof, both arms straight on two holds. Right foot
  toe-down on top of a foothold, left foot toe-hooking the same hold's far side
  (arrows showing the two feet squeezing toward each other). A second inset:
  heel hooked over the lip, knee bent, arrow showing the heel pulling the hips
  up toward the lip.
- **Related:** toe hook, heel hook, bicycle, knee bar, cutting loose, campusing.
- **Sources:** [E5, E7, E13, E15, E20]

### Arête technique

- **Also called:** arête laybacking, laying away on an arête.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V2–V4 (judgement). Arêtes appear in many low-grade
  problems; the barn-door control they demand is an intermediate skill.
- **What it is:** Climbing a protruding outside edge of the wall by pulling on
  the edge and pushing with the feet (layback), or by combining the edge with
  holds on the side wall [E2, snippet E17].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Decide per section: arête only, or arête plus side-wall holds.** On
     harder climbs combining laybacking with side holds is usually most
     efficient; plan which sections use which [E2].
  2. **Layback where the side wall is blank:** hands and feet move up the
     arête alternately, working in opposition [E2]. Keep arms straight so the
     feet do the work [E24].
  3. **Hold your body at about 45° to the arête**, halfway between the arête
     and the wall. Too close to the wall and smeared feet slip; too far round and
     the holds feel worse and you barn-door [E2].
  4. **Heel-hook round the arête** for stability and to rest the arms [E2].
  5. **If you start to barn-door, tense the core immediately** — but prevent it
     with body angle rather than rescue it [E2].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** a problem that uses the wall's corner
  edge as a hold ("arête in" or marked tape on the edge); few holds on one
  face. **Failure symptoms:** you swing off like a door when you move a hand;
  feet slip and you bash your shins [E2].
- **Common mistakes:** wrong body angle; not committing feet; no backup sequence
  [E2].
- **Illustration brief:** Climber seen from slightly behind and to the left of
  an arête. Both hands pinching/laybacking the arête at shoulder and head
  height, feet smearing on the right-hand face. Torso at 45° to the arête (draw
  the 45° angle as an arc on the floor plan inset, seen from above). Ghost
  outline swung around the arête labelled "barn door". Right heel hooked around
  the arête in a second inset.
- **Related:** layback, barn door, heel hook, pinch, flagging.
- **Sources:** [E2, E17, E24]

### Bridging / stemming (corners and dihedrals)

- **Also called:** stemming (US), bridging (UK); pressing.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V1–V3 (judgement); mainly a resting and efficiency
  tool in indoor corners.
- **What it is:** Pushing outward against two opposing surfaces with any mix of
  hands and feet, holding position by keeping even pressure on both [E20, E24].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Place one foot on each wall of the corner and press outward and down [E20,
     E24].
  2. Keep even pressure on both surfaces — balance comes from the pressure, not
     from gripping [E24].
  3. Push with palms (pressing) rather than pulling where there are no holds
     [E20].
  4. Use a wide bridge to take weight off the arms; often a no-hands rest is
     possible [snippet E17].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** inside corners; two volumes or wall
  panels at an angle to each other; features where your feet can push in
  opposite directions [E20]. Gresham adds bridging and palming between volumes
  [E11]. **Failure symptoms:** your arms tire in a corner that has good feet on
  both walls.
- **Common mistakes:** facing straight into the corner with both feet on one
  wall; gripping hard instead of pressing.
- **Illustration brief:** Climber seen from behind in a 90° inside corner: left
  foot on the left wall, right foot on the right wall, both at knee height,
  knees bent; hands flat on both walls at chest height, arms straight. Arrows on
  hands and feet pointing outward into each wall. Centre-of-gravity dot midway
  between feet.
- **Related:** palming, chimneying, compression, knee bar.
- **Sources:** [E11, E17, E20, E24]

### Volume technique

- **Also called:** volume climbing; standing on volumes; volume compression.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V1 for standing on volumes; V4+ for volume-only or
  "features only" problems (judgement). Volume-only problems are harder to read
  than problems with angled holds [E19].
- **What it is:** Using large wall-mounted shapes as hand and foot holds: standing
  on their faces, pressing and palming, gripping their edges, and squeezing them
  between hands or feet [E18, E19, E11].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Standing on a volume:** stand on the far side, away from the wall, so
     your hips are over your feet; smear the front half of the shoe, heel lower
     than the toe; make small precise movements [E5, E18].
  2. **Use the whole surface:** smear with as much shoe as possible; for hands,
     use palms and chalk the whole hand [E18].
  3. **Find the edge:** grip a sharp volume edge with a "meat-hook" (palm on one
     face, fingers on the other), a pinch, an open hand or a crimp on a straight
     edge [E18]. On a rounded volume, look for a bulge or scoop; chalk marks show
     where others found the grip [E18].
  4. **Get underneath it:** on a slopey volume, hang low with straight arms so
     gravity pulls you onto it [E18].
  5. **Push and pull in opposition:** press hands and feet against the direction
     of travel; with feet, push down and pull toward you with the heel of a
     flexed foot [E18].
  6. **Compression:** on steeper walls, squeeze a volume between both feet on
     opposite sides [E18], or between palms/pinching sides [E11].
  7. **Look around the sides and underneath for hidden holds**, using reference
     points to remember them [E11]; heel and toe hooks on volumes stop swings;
     knee-bars fit between close volumes; rest on an angled top [E11].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** volume faces with no holds; edges of
  volumes forming arêtes; two volumes close together (compression, knee bar).
  Note: volumes are usually "in" for every problem on that wall [E19]
  (rules vary by gym; check).
- **Common mistakes:** rushing (speed is "not your friend" on holds without an
  obvious grip) [E18]; standing too close to the wall on vertical/slab [E18];
  sloppy placements that scrape skin [E18]; not committing [E18].
- **Tall climber note:** compression spans and far-side standing are easier with
  long limbs; scrunched positions between close volumes may be harder
  (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** Close-up side profile of a big triangular volume on a
  vertical wall. Climber's right shoe on the far face of the volume, front half
  smearing, heel below toe; hips above that foot (plumb line). Second panel from
  behind: two volumes either side of the climber, feet pressing inward on their
  outer faces (arrows inward, "compression"), arms straight on the volume edges
  with a meat-hook grip on one.
- **Related:** slab technique, compression, palming, pinch, dynamic slab, knee bar.
- **Sources:** [E5, E11, E18, E19]

### Top-out (mantle and heel-hook rock-over)

- **Also called:** topping out; mantle / mantel / mantelshelf; heel-hook
  rock-over; "beached whale" (the failure mode).
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** wherever your gym has top-out boulders; mantle-type
  finishes at V2–V4 (judgement). Some gyms have designated top-out problems for
  practice **(unverified: search-result claim, source not pinned down)**.
- **What it is:** Getting from hanging on the lip to standing on top, either by
  pressing down on the palms (mantle) or by hooking a heel on the lip and rolling
  over it (heel-hook rock-over) [E14, E22, E23].
- **How to do it (rounded or steep lip: heel-hook rock-over):**
  1. **Feet high first** — ideally level with your waist [E14, E23].
  2. **Swing a heel up onto the lip** and place it carefully on an edge, bump or
     small divot; a heel placement can't be adjusted later [E14, E23]. On
     rounded or undercut tops this is "always" the first priority, as it takes
     load off the arms [E13].
  3. Pull with arms and heel while straightening the lower leg [E14]. Toe-cam
     variation: after placing the heel, rotate the toe sideways to cam under a
     feature [E13].
  4. **Turn the pivot knee in and pull the torso over the lip**; flag or smear
     with the other foot as a counterbalance [E14].
  5. **Roll onto the toe** once high enough; check the foot is close to the lip,
     not far to the side [E23, E14].
  6. **Turn a palm down and press**, then the other; release the heel, bring the
     free leg up and stand [E14, E22].
- **How to do it (flat ledge: mantle):** push with the feet to lift the hips,
  lead with the chest over the hands, straighten the arms, bring a toe up to the
  lip, stand slowly [E23, E21].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** the finish is "top out" rather than a
  marked final hold; a flat or rounded top with no holds. **Failure symptoms:**
  you end up belly-flat on top ("beach whaling") [E22]; you hang at the lip with
  low feet and can't get the hips up [E23].
- **Common mistakes:** hesitating mid-move [E23]; low feet [E23]; badly placed
  heel [E23]; reaching blindly with a hand [E22]; trying a belly flop on steep
  ground instead of jumping off safely [E22].
- **Indoor notes:** look for the top-out descent before you start (stairs,
  ladder or downclimb) [E65, E33]; practise low mantles without topping out
  first [E22].
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile, three ghosted stages at a rounded lip of
  a vertical wall. Stage 1: both hands on the lip, right heel hooked on the lip
  at hand height, left foot smearing low. Stage 2: hips at lip height, right knee
  turned in, chest over the lip, left palm pressing down beside the right foot.
  Stage 3: standing on the right foot on top. Arrows: heel pulling, palm pushing
  down, hips moving up and over.
- **Related:** mantle, heel hook, rock-over, palming.
- **Sources:** [E13, E14, E21, E22, E23, E33, E65]

### Traverse technique

- **Also called:** traversing; cross-through, foot swap, foot match.
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V1–V3 (judgement).
- **What it is:** Moving sideways along the wall, which emphasises sidepulls,
  laybacks, crimps, stemming and cross-throughs [E25].
- **How to do it:**
  1. **Cross through** (one hand crosses over or under the other) to make moves
     longer and smoother instead of shuffling hands [E25].
  2. **Flag instead of swapping feet** when the holds don't line up; double foot
     swaps waste energy [E11, E12].
  3. Use the outside edge with crossed legs when holds line up [E12].
  4. Use directional holds: lean away on sidepulls [E21]; traversing is a good
     place to practise them [E21].
- **When to use it (reading the wall):** the start and finish are at the same
  height; holds are sidepulls facing the direction you came from. **Failure
  symptoms:** lots of small hand shuffles; repeated foot swaps; getting pumped on
  easy holds.
- **Common mistakes:** "double foot swaps instead of flagging" [E11].
- **Illustration brief:** Climber from behind moving right: left hand on a
  sidepull, right hand crossing over the left to the next hold on the right;
  left foot outside-edge on a hold, right leg flagged behind. Arrow for the
  crossing hand.
- **Related:** cross-through, flagging, foot swap, sidepull.
- **Sources:** [E11, E12, E21, E25]

### Sit start

- **Also called:** sit-down start (SDS), sit start; "low start".
- **Category:** Terrain
- **Becomes important at:** V3–V5 (judgement): sit-start problems often add a
  hard first move; forum reports agree the first pull off the ground is
  typically where power is needed (forum, snippet-level) [E26].
- **What it is:** Starting a problem seated on the mat with hands on the start
  holds; the first move is pulling the bottom off the ground [E11].
- **How to do it:**
  1. Put the active foot on its outside edge, hips at about 90° to the wall
     (sideways), not frogged [E11].
  2. Keep the bottom close to the wall [E11].
  3. Lay back on the start holds if they allow it [E11].
  4. Rock back for momentum, then pull and stand in one movement; Gresham also
     describes rolling one knee onto the floor and pushing off [E11].
- **Rules note:** some climbers hold that you must be static on the start holds
  before moving and that using the swing from the sit is cheating; others
  disagree, and conventions vary (forum) [E26]. In gyms, ask staff [E55].
- **Tall climber note:** taller climbers may still have their bottom on the
  ground when shorter climbers have already pulled on, and vice versa (forum,
  attribution unverified) [E26]. For a tall climber: sit to the side of the start
  holds with legs off to one side, rather than folding straight under (judgement).
- **Illustration brief:** Side profile: climber seated on mat, both hands on
  start holds at chest height, body turned 90° to the wall. Right foot outside
  edge on a low hold, left leg extended flat on the mat. Arrow showing the rock
  back then up. Ghost: frog-position climber with knees splayed, labelled
  "avoid".
- **Related:** layback, outside edge, body tension.
- **Sources:** [E11, E26, E55]

## Part 2 — Route reading

### 2.1 What the research says (short)

- **Previewing improves how you climb more than whether you top.** In Sanchez et
  al. (2012), 29 climbers did indoor routes with or without a 3-minute preview.
  Previewing did not change completion rates, but it gave fewer and shorter
  stops; the shorter stops appeared only in experts [E34].
- **Experts read in sections.** Interviews with expert climbers describe moving
  from broad to specific: view the whole route, study details, then read it
  sequence by sequence, marking cruxes and rests; compare moves with their stored
  repertoire; look for "traps", especially at the start, where a wrong hand or
  foot would force a fall or a reversal; and read the feet as well as the hands
  [E35].
- **Reading is motor simulation, and it depends on what you can already do.**
  Expert climbers remembered a hard route's hold sequence better than novices,
  with no difference for easy or impossible routes [E36]. Experts make fewer
  severe planning errors; novices make "short-sighted" choices of nearby holds
  that aren't in the correct sequence [E37]. Expert climbers perceive the
  functional use of holds (affordances) better than novices [E38 citing Boschker
  et al. 2002 and Whitaker et al. 2020]. **Practical consequence:** you can't
  read a move you've never felt. Build the repertoire on the wall, then reading
  improves.
- **Elite competitors spend real time reading.** In 2018 World Cup qualification
  and semi-finals, athletes looked for 47–51 s before their first attempt (finals
  have a 2-minute group preview) [E40].

### 2.2 Step-by-step method for an indoor boulder

**Step 1 — Rules and boundaries (15 s).**
- Use only holds of the problem's colour or tape [E65].
- Start: marked by extra tape, a coloured box or the grade card; begin with your
  hands on the start hold(s) [E65]. Whether both hands go on one start hold,
  whether feet must also be on marked holds, and whether volumes and wall
  features are "in" varies by gym. Volumes are commonly treated as part of the
  wall for every problem [E19]. Ask staff if unsure [E55].
- Sit start: marked on the tag at many gyms (judgement; no source found for a
  standard marking). Be clear whether you must be static on the start [E26].
- Finish: a marked top hold near the top of the wall, or a top-out onto a
  feature [E65]. In competition a "top" means securing the final marked hold
  [E6]; gyms usually expect two hands matched and controlled (judgement).
- Find the way down before you start [E33, E65].

**Step 2 — Stand back, then move around (30–60 s).**
- View from several angles and from far back, to see bulges and the top;
  pay particular attention to widely spaced footholds [E30, E11].
- Get close to the base to see undercuts and stand to the side to see
  sidepulls and incuts [E30].
- Read the top from well back: tunnel vision is most likely there [E30]. "You
  might think the crux is the hardest move, only to struggle at the top" [E33].

**Step 3 — Read each hold's orientation → required body position.**
A hold "faces" the way its opening (the incut your fingers go into) points. The
rule: **you pull a hold toward yourself, so your body must end up on the side
opposite its opening.** (Mechanics, my synthesis; consistent with [E21, E29,
E30].)

| Hold (which way the opening faces) | Pull direction | Where your body goes | Default hand / cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jug or crimp (opens up) | Down | Below the hold | Either hand; hips under it |
| Sidepull (opens sideways, e.g. right-facing) | Sideways, toward you | On the side away from the opening: lean away from the hold and turn a hip in [E21, E29] | The hand on the hold's side of your body: a right-facing sidepull takes the right hand, body to its left (BMC: "a right-facing hold generally takes the right hand" [E29]) |
| Undercling (opens down) | Up | Above it: hold at waist or chest height, feet high and secure [E21] | Get close to the base of the wall to spot underclings [E30] |
| Gaston (a sidepull taken with the "wrong" hand) | Outward, away from your midline, elbow out, thumb down | The same side as for the sidepull; the hand crosses in front of the body | Engage core and legs [E21]. Any vertical edge: same-side hand = sidepull, other hand = gaston (judgement) |
| Sloper (no opening, rounded) | Down and in | Low, centre of gravity below the hold, arm straight [E11, E18] | Steer hips first when moving sideways [E11] |
| Volume face | Press | Feet on the far side, hips over feet [E5, E18] | Palm; meat-hook on edges [E18] |
- Chalk on a hold shows where people grip; check the chalk print for
  orientation, but watch for false prints [E30, E31, E32].

**Step 4 — Map the hand sequence (count hands).**
1. Number the hand holds from start to finish.
2. Assign L or R to each hold from its orientation (step 3).
3. **Check parity**: if two consecutive holds need the same hand, you need a
   **match** (both hands on one hold), a **cross-through**, or a "go again"
   (same hand moves twice) (judgement; options from [E30]).
4. For each ambiguous point, list the options in order (cross-over, match, go
   again) and decide when you feel the holds [E30].
5. Pick 2–3 **marker holds** (e.g. "the big blue pinch") that anchor the
   sequence so you can recall the rest from them [E30].
6. Decide where you'll need to bump, cross or reach, and let the hands set the
   direction [E32].
7. Setters deliberately use left/right sequences to force a type of movement
   (forum, snippet-level) [E48], and use matching, crossovers, underclings and
   sidepulls even on easy problems [E46]. So expect at least one non-ladder
   moment.

**Step 5 — Find the feet.**
- Rubbered holds with no chalk are probably footholds; black rubber marks show
  where people step [E30, E31].
- On steep ground, look for outside-edge options; on roofs and steep panels,
  heel hooks, toe hooks and bicycles [E30].
- Look for moves where a **flag** saves two foot swaps in a row [E30, E12].
- Feet decide the next hand move; read them with the hands, not afterwards [E35].
- Gresham says the full foot sequence is the hardest thing to predict and
  suggests beginners skip it at first [E30]. For a V3–V4 climber: read feet
  for the crux and the start, and work the rest out on the wall (judgement).

**Step 6 — Find the crux, the traps and the rests.**
- Crux types: a dynamic move, a balance or tension problem, a cut-loose move, or
  small/tricky holds [E29]. Decide in advance how you'll get through it [E29].
- Traps: places, especially near the start, where a wrong hand or foot forces
  a fall or a costly reversal [E35].
- Rests: shake-outs, stand-up positions, knee bars, heel hooks [E29, E30, E32].
- Section the problem by crux, roof and style change (e.g. overhang to slab)
  instead of memorising every move [E32].

**Step 7 — Recognise setter intent.**
What setters say they do (so what to expect):
- They start from a brief: who the problem is for, what grade, what movement and
  effort [E49 snippet]. Questions they ask include which skills are tested
  and what share of members should top it [E52].
- Non-ladder movement is deliberate: matching, crossovers, underclings,
  sidepulls on easy grades; intricate sequences, big slopers and big holds with
  small feet on harder ones [E46]. Easier gym problems use undercuts, sidepulls,
  easy heel hooks and dynos to vary movement [E47].
- To force body position, setters remove footholds or put the needed foot far to
  one side (forum, snippet-level) [E48].
- **Clichés that setters warn each other about** (so you'll meet them):
  ladder-style climbs where each foot follows a hand [E47]; extra feet added as a
  shortcut [E46]; difficulty made by reach alone, which tall setters are prone
  to [E47, E48]; jugs-only easy climbs; small holds plus big spans for hard ones
  [E46].
- Climbers often find beta the setter didn't expect, and grades sometimes get
  adjusted from community feedback [E54, E18].
- Comp-style problems: read from the top down; use chalk stains, thumbprints
  and shoe marks as clues [E5].

**Step 8 — Visualise and rehearse.**
- Go through the problem slowly once, then again [E30]. Gresham's levels: twice
  for the hand sequence, three times with rests, four times with feet [E30].
- Mime the moves standing in front of the wall, as competitors do [E29, E32].
- Picture the crux positions: one expert memorised the length and features of
  each crux so he knew how to position himself when he arrived [E35].
- Use short cue words for complex sequences ("cross, flag, match") [E32].
- Visualise realistic success before the attempt [E44].

**Step 9 — Climb, then review.**
- On the wall, check whether it's going as planned and note changes [E29].
- After coming down, compare predicted vs actual: what you got right and what
  you missed. Elite climbers do this, and it's the main way to improve reading
  [E29, E30, E11].
- Find out why you fell: misjudged hold, lost in the sequence, startled [E31].

### 2.3 Using other climbers' beta (and its limits for a tall climber)

- Watching others, asking advice and talking beta are standard tools [E29, E32];
  apps like Kaya carry community beta [E32].
- **Body size changes the beta.** Height and arm span strongly affect how a
  problem feels; adapt moves to your body [E29]. Size dependency is more than
  reach: tall climbers may get bunched up, small hands may not hold wide pinches
  or slopers as securely, and shorter climbers may not reach [E46]. New setters,
  especially tall ones, set to their own spans without realising [E46, E47].
- Gym grades also depend on body proportions: long moves feel harder to a
  short-armed climber; high footholds may feel easier to a tall one [E51].
- Look for "micro beta": small changes to how you grip holds, tailored to your
  size, strength and style [E41].
- **Tall-climber checklist (judgement, not sourced):**
  - When copying a shorter climber's sequence, expect to be able to skip an
    intermediate hand hold, and expect their foot positions to leave you
    folded up. Try the next foothold *lower or further out* so your arms stay
    straight.
  - If you feel scrunched, the problem was probably set for a shorter span:
    stand up on a lower foot, or use a higher hand hold earlier.
  - On sit starts, the floor is the problem: sit to the side, legs out.
  - Distrust "you just have to jump" beta from light climbers. Look for a static
    alternative that uses your reach first.

### 2.4 Flash strategy vs projecting strategy

**Flash (first try counts).**
- In World Cup finals data, the first attempt on a non-slab boulder was the
  best chance of a top, with a steep drop in success after it. On slab and
  slab-like boulders the success rate stayed more stable over the first three
  attempts [E6]. **So: on steep problems, invest in the first try. On slabs,
  a "look-see" first attempt costs less.**
- Successful elite attempts lasted longer (about 31–39 s) than failed ones (about
  6–21 s) [E40]. That suggests most failed attempts end early, so reading the
  start and its traps pays off (judgement).
- Flash routine (synthesis of [E29, E30, E35, E40]): full read (steps 1–8),
  at least 30–60 s of looking; mime the crux; rest fully; climb at a steady pace;
  don't stop to re-read on the wall (previewing reduces stops [E34]).

**Projecting (working a problem over attempts or sessions).**
1. **Choose sensibly.** A project should be no more than 2–3 grades above what
   you typically climb in a session [E41], "slightly beyond your current ability"
   [E45].
2. **Learn the moves, then link the crux, then link longer sections** [E44, E45].
   Work individual moves in isolation: get the first contact dialled before
   adding the second [E5].
3. **Split into sections** by cruxes, rest positions and style changes [E32, E43].
   Work one aspect per session: linking sections or refining individual moves
   [E43].
4. **Link through the crux and keep going**; big overlapping links measure
   progress better than high points from the start [E44].
5. **Practise the easy parts too.** Slapping or campusing through easy moves wastes
   energy you'll need when linking [E41, E44].
6. **Count quality attempts.** Treat each attempt as information and consider how
   many good attempts you can make in a day [E43]. In elite finals, success was
   zero after the sixth attempt, and on non-slab boulders the fifth attempt after
   four failures succeeded only 6% of the time [E6]. That's elite comp data with
   short rests, but it supports a rule of thumb: **after about 4–6 full-effort
   attempts with no progress, change something (rest longer, change beta, or
   leave it for another session)** (judgement from [E6, E43]).
7. **Keep side projects** to stay fresh [E43].

**Rest between attempts.**

| Situation | Rest | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Single hard move or short crux (a few seconds of work) | ~2.5 min for a 3 s move (20–50× work time) | [E42] |
| Full attempt on a hard boulder (~30 s burn) | 10–15 min (20–50× work time) | [E42] |
| Physically demanding problem, any length | at least 5 min | [E41] |
| Longer boulder problem or route | 10–25 min | [E41] |
| What elite competitors actually do in comps (time-limited) | ~114 s (UK national comp, 2010); ~32 s average between attempts in 2018 World Cups | [E39, E40] |

The comp figures are not recommendations: they describe athletes working under
a 4–5-minute limit. For working a limit boulder, the coaching sources agree on
minutes, not seconds; resting too little changes the energy system you are
training [E42].

### 2.5 When to change the beta

- When sessions keep going down "blind alleys", question the method regularly
  [E41].
- When stuck, retest and switch your beta; find your own beta for each move
  before asking others [E44].
- After each fall, name the cause. If it's the sequence, find an easier one; if
  it's strength, keep the beta and train [E31, E41].
- Try something new every time, even on familiar ground: a different foot or
  grip [E44].
- If the move feels impossible, rethink it: indoor problems are set for an
  intended sequence, but you can find your own [E29].
- Signal to change (judgement): you fail at the same point, in the same way, on
  three consecutive fresh attempts; or the move only "works" for climbers of a
  very different size.

## Part 4 — Indoor grading

### 4.1 How indoor grades are set

- **The setter suggests a grade, then the team tests it.** Typically the setter
  grades after a climb or two, sometimes adjusting after asking other setters or
  climbers [E58]. Some gyms grade by consensus after setters forerun each
  other's problems [E50]. Forerunning is where setters tweak problems using
  teammates' feedback until they hit the intended grade [E46].
- **Disagreement:** a gym writer argues gym grades "rarely benefit from feedback"
  and that modern gyms don't truly use consensus grading [E51]. Both may be true
  at different gyms.
- **Consistency over time matters more than matching outdoors.** Setter Louie
  Anderson says grading stiff or soft relative to outdoor is fine; the goal is a
  gym's grades staying consistent as problems change and different setters take
  them down. Regulars adapt to a gym's grading [E46].
- **Anchors differ.** Gyms far from rock anchor grades to the Kilter Board or
  Tension Board, or to setters' outdoor trips [E52].

### 4.2 Why grades vary between gyms

- **Magnitude:** an analysis of 40 US gyms reports bouldering grades differing by
  "two, three, or even four V-grades" between gyms; a solid V4 climber could
  expect V2–V6 depending on the gym [E53]. (A blog analysis of a commercial
  gym dataset, published October 2026; not peer-reviewed.)
- **Gym type and wall style:** large commercial chains grade about one V-grade
  or more softer than training-focused gyms; steep, overhang-heavy gyms grade
  harder; slab/vertical-heavy gyms and comp-style setting tend to be softer
  [E53].
- **Beginner economics:** gyms have many beginners, so they offer many easy
  problems and soften the lower V-grades rather than subdivide them, which can
  spill into intermediate grades [E57]. One blog claims some gyms bump problems
  up a grade to keep newcomers motivated (no data given) [E64].
- **Style, holds and sequence:** slab vs overhang vs dynamic; hold type and
  size; one awkward transition can make a whole problem feel harder; setter's
  energy on the day; beta the setter didn't expect [E54].
- **Climber body and strengths:** height, reach, finger size, style [E57, E51, E46].
- **Grades are subjective to "both the climber and route setter"** [E55].
- **Research status:** I found no peer-reviewed study of indoor grade
  reliability; the evidence is setter commentary and one blog dataset [E53]. The
  IRCRA position statement defines a research conversion scale for climbing
  grades [E63], but I could not access its bouldering table.

### 4.3 Colour and circuit systems

- Some walls group problems into circuits that share one grade or span two or
  three; some mark a circuit by hold colour (BMC example: purple V0–V2, yellow
  V3–V5) [E55].
- Circuits trace back to painted circuits at Fontainebleau [E56]. One stated aim
  is to de-emphasise grades and encourage discovery, so colour ranges overlap,
  and a harder colour isn't always harder than an easier one [E56].
- Example legends: Terra Firma, pink V0–V2, blue V2–V4, green V4–V6, orange
  V6–V8, black V8–V10, overlapping so harder climbs appear in each; Apex, red
  V0–V2, yellow V2–V4, green V4–V6, blue V6–V8, black V8+ [E52]. Gripped lists
  gyms with coloured circuits and no V-grades, and own scales (C1–C8; hexagons
  1–6) [E57].
- Customers often press circuit gyms for a V-grade legend [E57].
- UK walls typically use Font or V for bouldering [E55].

- Conversion tables are "only ever indicative" and vary by several levels
  between versions [E59]. Rockfax calls its grades "indicative rather than
  definitive" (snippet-level) [E62].
- For the reader: "first V5" = a 6C (or 6C+) in a Font gym.

### 4.5 Indoor vs outdoor

- Sources agree indoor grades are softer at lower and middle grades. One
  "consensus" claim is that up to about V5/V6 an outdoor grade is about two V
  grades harder [E64] (no data). Another says indoor grades feel softer up to
  about V7 / 7C+ [E59]. Gripped says upper and elite indoor grades are closer
  to outdoor ones [E57]. A climber comfortable at V3 indoors may find outdoor
  V0 hard [E56].

### 4.6 How to calibrate yourself in a new gym

1. **Ask staff** how the gym's system works and how its levels compare with
   standard grades [E55, E56].
2. **Warm up 3–4 grades below your max** and start below your limit while you
   learn the gym's style [E53, E56].
3. **Time-boxed circuit test:** in the first week, do as many problems as you can
   in 15 minutes, two grades below your perceived max. Repeat every two weeks
   and compare between gyms [E53].
4. **Benchmark by style.** Pick a reference problem at home for each style
   (crimps, slopers, compression, dynamic, technical/slab). After 3–4 sessions,
   compare the new gym's problems against them [E53].
5. **Expect style bias:** steep gyms feel stiffer, slab/comp gyms softer [E53].
6. **Track which problems you send reliably** and which feel harder or easier for
   your body; build your own grade sense [E51].
7. **Measure progress by more than grades:** smoother movement, better rests,
   faster reading [E56].

## Gaps and doubts

- No peer-reviewed study of indoor grade consistency found; the 40-gym analysis
  [E53] is a blog dataset.
- IRCRA bouldering conversion table not accessed [E63].
- UKC (Gresham) and Climbing.com claims are snippet-level only; attribution of
  some roof/toe-hook snippets between Gresham's UKC articles is uncertain [E15,
  E16].
- Tall-climber advice is mostly judgement; only [E46, E47, E29, E51, E26] speak
  to body size.
- Traverse, corner and sit-start material is thin and mostly from Gresham [E11],

- Gym rules (feet-follow-hands, start conventions, matching the top) are not
