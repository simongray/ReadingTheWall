# Dossier H: climbing small

Paste-ready text for editors, written for the revision in `research/REVISE-3.md`. Sources are in `research/H-sources.yml`.

**The evidence in brief.** Studies say height predicts little about grade, and they disagree on its direction. We found no study that tests a technique for small climbers. We found one climbing-specific test of a hand measure (H27): among 48 people hanging from a 4 cm fingerboard crimp, longer middle fingers went with shorter hangs. It is weak evidence, because only 10 of them had climbing experience and a 4 cm crimp isn't a small hold. The how-to advice comes from climbers and coaches working around their own reach. Their heights run from under 5 ft to 5 ft 7 in and much of what they say comes from rock, so the section below says that plainly.

## 1. Candidate `small:` notes

Ranked by usefulness, most useful first. All keys are in the REVISE-3 table. When you paste a note, add each key it cites to the entry's `sources:` list, because the printed Sources line comes from that list (`book/template.typ`). ### 1. `sidepull`

```typst
small: [Burying your hand in a deep sidepull or #tref("undercling") feels secure but shortens your reach. When you need distance, slide your hand out towards the edge, as far as it still holds, before you move off it #src("H3").],
```

Evidence: Samet's method, shown in photos (H3). It is a small trick that readers are unlikely to work out alone. The tall note is about leaning off the hold, so the two don't overlap.

### 2. `hand-foot-match`

```typst
small: [For you this is also a reach move. Bring a foot up to the hold your hand is on before you go for the far hold, because higher hands start from higher feet #src("H2", "D45").],
```

Evidence: a training site (H2) and a hobby site (D45). The entry now presents this move only as a way onto ledges, so the note adds a use. The tall note is about folding the leg, so the two don't overlap.

### 3. `high-step`

```typst
small: [Even tiny footholds can count as the in-between step. One climber of 5 ft 6 in walks his feet up on them while his hands stay put, so his hips are already high when he commits. That means more time on the handholds, and his taller partners argue that this style takes more lock-off strength, though he doubts it #src("H3").],
```

Evidence: one experienced climber's method, written mainly about rock (Samet, H3). It extends the entry's first step and agrees with Gresham's warning about big high steps in the grade note (A7). Samet also recommends stiff shoes here, which the note leaves out (see "Stiff shoes" in section 4). The tall note is about knees jamming, so the two don't overlap.

### 4. `backstep`

```typst
small: [Turning your hip in brings you closer to the wall. Climbers' own estimates of the gain run from an inch or so #src("H1") to a few inches #src("H2"), and we found no measurement. So when the holds suit a backstep, try it before you decide a move is too far (judgement). A #tref("drop-knee") does a similar job if your knees are mobile enough #src("H4"), but read the knee box in @cons-tall first.],
```

Evidence: H1 and H2 name the backstep or outside edge. H4 and H8 name the drop knee, and H8 offers it as an alternative to a flag. H3, H5, H6, H9, H10 and H11 name none of these moves. Osius tells readers to make the outside edge a habit, with exceptions (H1). This note doesn't, because the entry limits the move: overhangs first, good holds, and a foothold on the line. The tall note is about what counts as knee height.

### 5. `deadpoint`

```typst
small: [Short climbers often name this as their way to far holds #src("B34", "H6", "H8"). To build it, go for holds just out of reach on easy problems #src("D45"), stay low on the wall at first #src("H4"), and repeat a long move after you stick it so you learn your aim #src("H6"). Keep big jumps for the moves that need them, and don't snatch at small holds (@cons-heavy).],
```

Evidence: Traversi (B34), Quinn (H6) and Pillai (H8) report this from experience, and a head setter agrees (H10). Quinn hedges that short climbers only possibly move dynamically more often, so the note makes no claim about how often you will need it. D45 is a hobby site. The last sentence follows REVISE-3 and the wording in `research/evidence-3.md`, item 14. The tall note says tall climbers can often lock off instead, and this note doesn't invert that.

### 6. `lock-off`

```typst
small: [On a big reach you may need your hand below your shoulder, at or even below your chest, which is harder than a lock beside your shoulder. One coach under 5 ft suggests trying these deeper locks on climbs at your warm-up grade #src("H9"). Add a few at a time, because they load your elbow and shoulder (judgement).],
```

Evidence: a youth coach's drill (H9). The tall note covers the half lock against the full lock, so this note goes straight to the hand position and the drill. Randall suggests lock-offs as part of the reason for Lattice's height result, without measuring it (H7). Quinn's three-second hovers are left out: they belong to a 5.13 climber's programme, and she warns readers to train at their own level (H6).

### 7. `edging`

```typst
small: [When a hold is just out of reach, stand on the highest point of the foothold #src("H9") and drive up through your big toe with your calf #src("H3"). One climber puts the gain from rising on your toes at a few centimetres #src("H8"), but a heel that goes too high can pop the foot #src("A2").],
```

Evidence: a coach (H9) and two climbers' experience (H3, H8). This note moved here from `front-pointing`, because these sources describe edging and foot placement, while that entry is about a foot pointing straight at the wall. Stiff shoes are left out (see "Stiff shoes" in section 4). The tall note is about sit starts.

### 8. `pinch`

```typst
small: [If your thumb can't press back on a wide pinch, you may be able to hold it like an edge #src("C37"), or pull it as a #tref("sidepull") with your body in line (judgement).],
```

Evidence: a retailer's blog, which says only that holding a pinch like a ledge may be possible (C37). The sidepull option is judgement. The setter's point about small hands (E46) already sits in chapter 2 and in section 2 below, so the note doesn't repeat it, and it doesn't mirror the tall note on big hands.

### 9. `volume`

```typst
small: [One head setter angles some volumes so that climbers can smear along the whole face, which evens out height differences #src("H10"). So before you decide a step is too high, look along the face for a spot you can reach, on the far side if you can (judgement).],
```

Evidence: one setter's practice, and only where smearing is involved (King, H10). It doesn't cover all volumes. "On the far side" keeps the note in line with the entry, which teaches that standing near the wall pushes you off. The tall note is about squeezing and folding up.

**Considered and left out.** These either add little to the entry or rest on too little:

- `bumping`: the entry defines a bump as the same hand moving twice with the feet still and no settling on the small hold. H1 and H2 describe a high foot, or a stop to reset the feet, which is a different move. What remains repeats the entry, and "Add moves" in section 2 keeps the general point.
- `heel-hook`: A9 and A15 support the point, but the entry already says it twice and its tall note says it again.
- `regrip`: Samet's three-finger catch is one 5.14 climber's habit. He says it works only on certain holds or for very strong climbers, and his method ends in a full crimp (H3). Without the crimp it isn't his method, and for a V3 reader it normalises pulling up on three fingers after a fast catch at full stretch. We found no source for it at V3.
- `compression`: no source deals with compression for a small span. Hanes's drill pulls on holds in any direction with no squeeze (H2), so it trains what the entry calls a fault. Quinn says only that ring work also helps squeeze strength (H6).
- `dyno`: Quinn writes about fear and says nothing about checking a landing, which the entry already covers (H6). Samet's line is about reachy moves in general (H3). Summerlee's line defends setting jumps, and he concedes that a tall climber needn't jump as far (H10).
- `stemming`: no source, and it mirrors the tall note.
- `front-pointing`: the note moved to `edging`.
- `sloper`: E46 supports the point, but it leads to no action beyond the entry. Chapter 9 covers it.
- `knee-bar`: a real advantage (H2, H6), but rare at this level. Chapter 9 mentions it.
- `overhang`: the shorter lever is only "in theory" (H6).
- `jump-start`: the grade note already makes the point (D45).
- `undercling`: the entry already says it gives a long reach. H1 could support an extra citation there.

## 2. "Climbing small" for chapter 9

**Not in the brief.** REVISE-3 asks for notes on a few entries and for a shorter book. It doesn't ask for this section or for the doubled box in section 3, so Simon should confirm them before an editor pastes either.

Place it straight after `@cons-tall`. The chapter's opening paragraph describes the reader as tall and heavier, so its owner needs to adjust that sentence.

In a scratch preview the section fills about a full page. To bring it down to half a page, run the four heads into the paragraphs as bold labels, then cut "Where being small helps and hurts". Keep the last sentence of the "Learn momentum" step through any cut, because it carries the pointer to the shoulder evidence.

```typst
== Climbing small <cons-small>

==== What height doesn't predict
#ix("Small climbers", main: true)

Being small changes which problems feel hard, but it doesn't set your grade. Studies of height point both ways #src("H14"). In a V0–V8 competition, height and body mass didn't help predict 67 boulderers' scores, while ring-finger strength, experience and how often they bouldered did #src("H13").

What height may change is the price of a grade. In a 2017 podcast, Lattice#ix("Lattice Training") reported that among over 500 clients, shorter climbers at the same grade had stronger fingers, by about 2.5% per 10 cm. Its coaches say these climbers need that strength, whatever they weigh #src("H7"). One blogger argues instead that strength relative to bodyweight comes more easily to a shorter body, though he has no test of this #src("H15"). We found no published version of the figure. Lattice's written post on height, about a different test, calls its own correlation not strong #src("H28").

==== Where being small helps and hurts

One columnist, writing about an outdoor slab, thinks reach hurts most on vertical walls with big moves between good holds #src("H11"). Indoors, one head setter admits that a lot of setting is built for the average man's body #src("H10"). By climbers' own accounts, being small helps on sit starts #src("H11"), high feet #src("H8") and tight knee bars #src("H2", "H6"), and perhaps on steep walls #src("H6"). If you are also light, each finger carries less load (judgement), but don't chase a lower weight (@cons-heavy).

==== How to climb small
#ix("Small climbers!how to climb")

We found no study that tests these points. They come from climbers and coaches of about 150 to 170 cm who work around their own reach, and much of the advice comes from rock. No source draws a common line for small, so take what fits you.

+ *Add moves where the problem lets you.* Split a long move with a small hold or a high foot #src("H1"). Indoors there aren't always more options #src("H8"), though some setters add a small hold for this reason #src("H10").
+ *Raise your feet.* Walk them up on tiny footholds #src("H3") or use a high #tref("heel-hook") #src("H2").
+ *Turn in.* Where the holds suit it, bring your hip to the wall with a #tref("backstep") #src("H1", "H2").
+ *Learn momentum in small steps* #src("B34"). On easy problems, go for #tref("deadpoint", body: [deadpoints]) to holds just out of reach #src("D45"), low on the wall at first #src("H4"). Keep big jumps for the moves that need them, and don't snatch at small holds (@cons-heavy has the shoulder evidence).

==== Small hands
#ix("Hand size")

One setter says small hands can't use wide #tref("pinch", body: [pinches], show-page: false) and #tref("sloper", body: [slopers], show-page: false) as securely as large ones #src("E46"). You may be able to hold a wide pinch like an edge #src("C37"), or pull it as a #tref("sidepull", body: [sidepull], show-page: false) (this book's suggestion). We found no study of hand size on small holds. The nearest is a test of 48 people hanging from a 4 cm crimp, where longer middle fingers went with shorter hangs, but only 10 of them had climbing experience and a 4 cm crimp isn't small #src("H27").
```

```typst
#ix("Tall climbers!reading a problem")#ix("Small climbers!reading a problem")These points apply the sources above to your own body, and we found no study that tests them. For more, see @cons-tall and @cons-small. When you copy a shorter climber:
- Expect to skip an intermediate handhold.
- Expect their foot positions to fold you up. Try the next foothold lower or further out, so your arms stay straight.
- If you feel scrunched, the problem was probably set for a shorter span. Stand up on a lower foot, or take a higher handhold earlier.
- Distrust "you just have to jump" from light climbers. Look first for a slow move that uses your reach.

When you copy a taller climber:
- Expect more moves where the problem has holds for them. Their one reach may be your two or three, using small holds they skip, so look at holds with little chalk #src("H1", "H8"). Setters decide which holds a gym problem has, and indoors there aren't always more options #src("H8").
- Read the feet first. A higher foothold may bring the hold within reach #src("H2", "H3"), and so may a place to smear #src("H8"). Someone your own size can show you where #src("H8").
- Check the distance before you believe it's too far. Count the bolt holes between the holds and compare them with your reach #src("H9").
- Distrust "you just have to reach", and read little into the grade. One reachy move can stop you on a V1 even if you send most V3s #src("E51").
]
```

## 4. Evidence in reserve

None of this appears in the text above. Editors can use it to swap or extend a point.

- **Measuring a move.** Besides counting bolt holes, you can hold a long brush up to the move (H8). If a move still stops you, ask whether the limit is reach, strength or commitment (H8).
- **Height and ability.** The 2025 review found four studies of body height, with correlations from r = −0.38 to r = 0.69 in male climbers (H14).
- **Height and bouldering.** Among 19 men, finger strength on a 22 mm edge tracked bouldering closely (r = 0.89). Body weight tracked it moderately (r = −0.49). Height had no significant link (r = −0.27, p = 0.28), so use "no significant link" and not "no link" (H17).
- **The elite picture is mixed.** Nine World Cup boulderers averaged 3.38% shorter than 25 national-level men, and they also had longer arms for their height. The authors call the benefit unclear (H18). Ten elite Polish boulderers also showed small stature with long arms (H19).
- **Heights in the rankings.** Women in the 2023 world ranking ran from 150 to 175 cm and averaged 163.3 cm. Men averaged 174.8 cm and the shortest stood 162 cm, but that average includes a height the blogger later says is wrong: listed as 198 cm, while the climber says 190 cm (H20). These figures come from the joint lead and boulder ranking, not from boulderers alone. This is one blogger's script run over incomplete federation profiles, and H11 wrongly calls it a study.
- **Women climbers.** In 55 experienced women, height and ape index didn't differ between ability groups, according to the Breaking Beta hosts' reading of Giles et al. (H21).
- **A second coaching dataset.** Height predicted maximum grade poorly in Power Company's assessments of about 600 climbers, and so did weight. This is H15's account of two third-party analyses, which we didn't read.
- **Strength and body size.** Muscle force grows more slowly than body mass, about as mass to the power 0.67 (H23). That supports H15's argument about bodyweight tests, but the paper doesn't deal with climbing. Junior competitors show small stature and a high grip-to-mass ratio, and the same authors worry about young climbers cutting weight (H24).
- **Hand size and grip.** On round tool handles, force and contact area depended largely on handle width relative to hand length (H16). In 46 men using tool handles, the span they rated best shifted with hand size (H22). One blogger thinks thin pinches suit small hands and that small feet help on tiny footholds (H15), and Lattice's coaches suspect hold size could matter slightly (H7).
- **Edge depth.** Finger force rose with hold depth from 1 to 4 cm, and the authors linked that to finger contact (H25). This hints that fingertip size could matter, but the study didn't test it.
- **Setters.** Danielson shortens a reach during forerunning, or adds another sequence, so the shortest expected climbers can make the move. He also balances problems across a set (H26). King adds extra footholds of the same quality (H10).
- **Stiff shoes.** No note recommends them, because the sources' context doesn't fit a gym reader. Samet recommends stiff shoes for standing tall on small edges, but he writes about edging on rock and calls soft shoes fun on boulders and at the gym (H3). Hanes normally prefers soft shoes and keeps a stiff pair for a hold just out of reach, for an extra few millimetres (H2). Pillai says stiffer shoes help you rise on your toes (H8), and Silver repeats Samet (H4). Chapter 3 already says that a softer shoe may be enough for bouldering (A6).
- **Shoulders.** A physiotherapist remarks that one climber's fully extended arm, on a dynamic board move, could irritate the shoulder (H12). It is a remark about one position, so don't generalise it. Quinn has had surgery on both shoulders and calls shoulder strength key if you often cut your feet, though she doesn't blame the moves for the surgery (H6).

## 5. Notes for editors

- **Index.** The book has "Short climbers" index terms in chapters 2, 3 and 6. Rename them to "Small climbers", or the index will split the topic.
- **Lattice's figure.** The evidence for it is thin. The 2.5% comes from a 2017 podcast about Lattice's client assessments (H7), and we found no published version of it. H4, H5 and H6 only re-report that podcast, so don't count them as extra support. Lattice's written post on height (H28) returns 404 at its live address, but the Wayback Machine has a copy. It graphs a max-moves circuit for climbers with a reported 8a redpoint, calls the correlation not strong and says more data are needed. In its comments the author says ape index wasn't controlled. The post gives neither the 2.5% nor the 500. These caveats attach to that graph and not directly to the finger figure, so keep the two apart. Don't state the figure as a requirement. "Stronger at the same grade" is what the data show, and "need" is Lattice's reading.
- **`H27`.** The regression allowed for weight, pull-ups and grip strength. The standardised coefficient for middle-finger length was −0.31 (p = .013) on the non-dominant hand and −0.30 (p = .034) on the dominant hand, and little-finger length dropped out. The authors offer finger length only as a hint for talent scouting, so don't use the study to say that short fingers help.
- **A possible clash.** "Climbing tall" reports forum claims that height's advantage fades by about V6. Lattice says its finger-strength result holds at every grade. Both are weak, but they shouldn't sit side by side unexplained.
- **`C31`.** The conference paper cited in the pinch entry now has a journal version: Huang et al. (2026), J Sports Sci 44:431–442, doi 10.1080/02640414.2025.2585743. Neither abstract gives the hold widths, so its figures say nothing about hand size.
- **Claims not used.** D45 says being under about 5 ft 5 in costs two or three grades, that small climbers balance better because of a lower centre of gravity, and that they tend to do better as grades advance. It gives no evidence for these. It also advises staying lean, which conflicts with the "Don't diet for grades" box.
- **Chapter 9, the Polish boulderers (`G5`, for that chapter's owner).** The existing sentence says they differed from non-climbers most in arm span and lean body mass. G5 is a blog summary of H19, whose abstract gives those two the weaker significance level (p ≤ .05, against p < .01 for height, density and fat). P levels don't show the size of a difference anyway. H19's abstract also reads the group as small in stature, with long arms and low body fat, so correct the sentence before anyone places H19 beside it. We read only that abstract.
- **Duplicate keys.** H12 is the same source as B71, and H14 the same as G68. `tools/merge_sources.py` maps them, so the bibliography lists each once.

## Disputed findings

The sceptic was right on the substance of nearly every item, and the text above now reflects that. These points in the report were wrong or went too far:

- **`H26` author.** The sceptic says no author can be named beyond the metadata's "Guest Post". The page's own text says The Climbing Doctor connected with Danielson for the interview, and `research/STYLE.md` allows an organisation as author, so the author stays and the note records the metadata.
- **`H27` hands.** The sceptic gives −0.31 (p = .013) to the dominant hand and −0.30 (p = .034) to the non-dominant hand. The paper's table lists the non-dominant hand first, so these are the other way round.
- **`H20` "almost 1 cm".** That is the blogger's own figure, and the sceptic repeats it. Removing one 198 cm height from 72 athletes lowers a mean of 174.8 cm by about 0.3 cm, so the bullet names the error without a size.
- **Chapter 9 step 2.** The sceptic says a hooking heel can't also be the outside-edge foot. Those can be different feet, because the entry lets the other leg stand wide. The sequence still has no source, so the step is split anyway.
- **`regrip`.** The note doesn't contradict the entry's rule on moving your fingers, because Samet gets his feet on and his hips up before he resets, so his weight is on his feet. The note is cut for the other reasons the sceptic gives.
- **Light climbers and stiff soles.** The sceptic's own suggestion, that light climbers deform a stiff sole less, has no source, so it isn't used.
