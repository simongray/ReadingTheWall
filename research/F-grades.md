# F: Grade-by-grade progression guides for indoor bouldering

Dossier for the book's grade-band chapter: a section per V-grade band, with extra depth on V4 to V5. Source IDs use the prefix **F** and are listed at the end.

## 0. Read this first: what the evidence can and cannot support

- **No coaching syllabus ties named techniques to V-grades.** The UK NICAS Bouldering scheme ties skills to five levels but publishes no grades and no level-by-level technique list [F28, F29, F30, F31]. The DAV *Boulder-Check 1/2* (Boulderschein) covers safety, falling ("Fallschule") and fall-zone hygiene, not graded technique [F32]. I found no USA Climbing, Mountain Training or "Movement for Climbers" document that maps skills to grades (see Gaps).
- **The best grade-tied technique lists are gym clinic syllabi.** Climbing gyms publish clinics for named grade bands, such as "Intermediate Technique, V3 and up" and "Gateway to Advanced Technique, stuck around V5", and list what each one teaches [F33, F34, F35, F36, F37]. These are the backbone of the "techniques that become necessary" sections below. They are marketing pages, not research, but they are the coaches' own statement of what a V3 or V5 climber is missing.
- **Strength-by-grade data comes mostly from Lattice Training's private database** [F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6]. The peer-reviewed studies group climbers into broad ability bands, mostly sport climbers [F16, F17, F18]. Treat the numbers as averages with very wide spread. Forum users report V6 climbers who can hang one-armed with added weight next to V6 climbers who cannot hang their bodyweight on two arms [F15].
- **Indoor grades are relative to the gym.** Bouldering Project notes that each gym sets differently, so the same grade can feel easier or harder from gym to gym [F27]. The BMC notes that many walls grade by colour circuit, and a circuit can span two or three grades, for example "V3–V5 yellow" [F26].
- Everything under "What problems look like" and "Reading problems" draws on the sources where it can. Much of it is coaching judgement, **marked (judgement)**. The book should present those parts as typical patterns, not rules.

## 1. V-scale and Font conversion

UK and European gyms mostly grade in Font, US gyms in V. The table below follows Mountain Project's conversion table [F22]. The Font column agrees with Wikipedia's table, which cites a UIAA 2016 report, the Rockfax bouldering grade table and theCrag [F23].

| V-grade | Font (Mountain Project [F22]) | Font (Wikipedia/UIAA [F23]) | 99Boulders band [F24] |
|---|---|---|---|
| VB | 3 | 3 | Beginner |
| V0− | 4− | 4− | Beginner |
| V0 | 4 | 4 | Beginner |
| V0+ | 4+ | 4+ | Beginner |
| V1 | 5 | 5 | Beginner |
| V2 | 5+ | 5+ | Beginner (V0–V2 = 4–5+) |
| V3 | 6A (V3+ = 6A+) | 6A / 6A+ | Intermediate |
| V4 | 6B (V4+ = 6B+) | 6B / 6B+ | Intermediate |
| V5 | 6C (V5+ = 6C+) | 6C / 6C+ | Intermediate (V3–V5 = 6A–6C+) |
| V6 | 7A | 7A | Advanced |
| V7 | 7A+ | 7A+ | Advanced |
| V8 | 7B (V8+ = 7B+) | 7B / 7B+ | Advanced (V6–V8 = 7A–7B) |
| V9 | 7C | 7C | Expert |
| V10 | 7C+ | 7C+ | Expert |
| V11 | 8A | 8A | Expert |
| V12 | 8A+ | 8A+ | Expert (V9–V12 = 7B+–8A+) |
| V13 | 8B | 8B | Elite |

| V15 | 8C | 8C | Elite |
| V16 | 8C+ | 8C+ | Elite |

Notes and disagreements:
- **Below V5, one V-grade spans more than one Font grade** (V3 covers 6A–6A+, V4 6B–6B+, V5 6C–6C+). The two scales become nearly one-to-one from about 7A or 7B up [F24, F23]. Wikipedia says the systems align exactly only from V9/7C up [F23].
- **Not every chart agrees at the low end.** Boulder Trainer maps V2 to 6A/6A+, V3 to 6B, V4 to 6B+ and V5 to 6C/6C+ [F25], which is half a Font grade to a full grade harder than Mountain Project and Wikipedia for V2–V4. Charts differ mainly in how they line up the wide V steps with the narrower Font steps below V5.
- **IRCRA numbers.** Researchers use the IRCRA scale, which turns every grade into an integer [F19]. Boulder Trainer reproduces its anchors as V0 = 12, V6 = 20 and V15 = 31, citing Draper et al. 2016 [F25]. I could not open the position statement to check these values or its ability groupings **(unverified)**.
- **For the reader:** in a Font gym, the V4→V5 step means moving from 6B/6B+ to 6C/6C+. Many UK gyms set those grades as one colour band, so "most of the V4s" might mean "most of the 6B+ problems on the green circuit" **(judgement)**.

## 2. Cross-grade data: strength, body and time

### 2.1 Finger strength against grade

| Measure | V1–V4 | V5–V7 | V8–V11 | V12+ | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two-arm max hang, average **added** load (kg), all climbers | +19.0 | +24.2 | +38.4 | +52.9 | Lattice, 6,000+ climbers [F1] |
| Same, female climbers only | +6.6 | n/r | n/r | +29.5 | [F1] |
| Two-arm added load by grip used in test | open hand +22.5, half crimp +14.5 | n/r | n/r | open +55.5, half crimp +55.3 | Lattice, 3,484 climbers [F6] |

[F1] gives no edge size or hang time. Lattice's standard finger test uses a 20 mm edge [F50], and a 2025 Lattice analysis describes its test as a 7-second two-arm hang on 20 mm, scored as % of bodyweight [F8, search snippet only]. n/r = not reported.

**Reconstruction by grade, men only, one-arm, 20 mm edge, 7–10 s, % bodyweight** [F15]. A climber reverse-engineered these values from Lattice material (UKBouldering, 2024). They are **not Lattice-published figures** and are partly extrapolated:

| V4 | V5 | V6 | V7 | V8 | V9 | V10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 49% | 55% | 61% | 67% | 73% | 79% | 85% |

Peer-reviewed findings:
- **Giles et al. 2021** studied 55 advanced to elite women. Finger strength and upper-body power were associated with ability, and the model fit was stronger for bouldering, where finger strength explained about 53% of the variance [F16; figure taken from a search summary of the abstract; full text blocked, **(unverified exact wording)**]. Practical reading: even in that group, roughly half the spread in bouldering grade is *not* explained by finger strength.
- **Baláš et al. 2012** studied 205 sport climbers. A hand-arm strength and endurance factor (grip strength, bent-arm hang, finger hang), together with % body fat, climbing volume and experience, predicted climbing performance. A related paper by the same group found that body fat and experience acted *through* strength and endurance rather than directly [F17; abstract via search summary]. These are route grades, not boulder grades.
- **Vereide et al. 2022** studied male sport climbers. Isometric pull-up force on a 23 mm rung rose about 40% from intermediate to advanced, and peak force correlated r ≈ 0.73 with grade. Body mass did not differ between groups (74.6, 72.5 and 70.9 kg). Body fat was 14.3% for intermediate and 11.4% for advanced climbers, a difference the authors judged unlikely to matter for performance [F18].
- A Lattice post on sport climbers reports a multi-factor model with R = 0.704 that explained 49.6% of grade variance [F51; search snippet only, page 404 on fetch **(unverified)**].
- Lattice's 2017 test of 13 climbers found that half-crimp strength correlated better with boulder grade than open-hand strength. The authors called n = 13 too small for firm conclusions [F7].
- Lattice advises 6–12 months of consistent climbing before starting hangboard training [F6].

### 2.2 Pulling strength
- Lattice's weighted pull-up two-rep max, as % of bodyweight: men at V7–V8 average about 145%. Lattice's "gold standards" are 165% for men and 135% for women. Above roughly 160–165% (men), more pulling strength stops paying off, and finger strength, flexibility or movement skill matter more [F5]. The data come from 733 elite-assessment tests, so V4–V5 values are not given.
- [F1] says pull-up performance roughly doubles across the grade bands, while press-ups barely change.

### 2.3 Body mass and height
- Among 8a.nu top-100 male boulderers, BMI correlated slightly **positively** with boulder grade. Lattice adds that the model fits poorly and should be taken "with a large grain of salt" [F10]. That sample is elite, but it argues against "lighter is always better" for bouldering.
- Systematic reviews associate low body fat with better climbing [F21, abstract via search]. Baláš found that body fat acts through strength-to-weight [F17], and Vereide found no difference in body mass between levels [F18].
- [F1] says Lattice modelled height and flexibility but published no numbers. I found no source quantifying height against boulder grade.
- **Arithmetic for the reader (illustration, not a source claim):** the [F15] percentages are load ÷ bodyweight. A climber with a 42 kg one-arm max scores 49% at 85 kg and 52.5% at 80 kg. On that table, losing 5 kg closes about half of the V4→V5 gap without any gain in strength.

### 2.4 Time to grade
- **Lattice, 65,000 climbers, average years climbing and years of structured training** [F2]:

| Band | Years climbing | Years training |
|---|---|---|
| V1–V4 | 5.8 | 2.4 |
| V5–V7 | 8.6 | 3.2 |
| V8–V11 | 9.7 | 4.6 |
| V12+ | 11.8 | 6.5 |

  The author expects the V1–V4 figure to be inflated by long-time casual climbers and says he has no data to support this. **Age matters:** at V8, climbers under 25 average 3.8 years and climbers over 40 average 13.0 years. Older climbers report more years at every grade from V4 to V13 [F2].
- **Weekly structured training hours** [F3]: V1–V4 2.6 h, V5–V7 3.2 h, V8–V11 4.4 h, V12+ 7.5 h. At V5–V7, under-30s train 4.2 h a week and climbers in their 30s 2.5 h, so part of the age effect is simply less time.
- **Training days:** a V4 Lattice user logs on average 3 training days a week, a V12+ user 4 [F4].
- **Sport-climber study:** years climbing were 5.0 ± 4.8 (intermediate), 8.0 ± 5.8 (advanced) and 13.7 ± 6.4 (elite), with 2.4, 3.2 and 4.4 sessions a week [F18].
- **Plateaus:** Lattice says plateaus often arrive in a climber's first couple of years, "somewhere between V3-V6" [F9].
- **Anecdotes** (forum, self-selected): Mountain Project users' grades after one year range from V4 outdoors to V7 or V8 indoors [F47]. Bouldering Boss gives V4 at about 1 year, V5 about 2, V6 about 3 and V7 about 5, but these are opinions rather than data, and the article contradicts itself [F48]. 99Boulders describes intermediates (V3–V5) as "been bouldering for a while" and advanced climbers (V6–V8) as "most likely a few years" in [F24].
- **Bottom line for the book:** not sending V5 after about a year is normal. The large dataset puts the average V5–V7 climber years past that point, and older climbers take longer [F2]. Everything here is cross-sectional and self-reported. Self-reported grades have been shown to be fairly accurate for lead climbing [F20], but no study tracks the same people over time.

## 3. Grade bands

### 3.1 VB–V0 (Font 3–4+)

**What problems look like (judgement unless cited):** vertical or slightly overhanging wall, or easy slab. Big positive holds (jugs, deep edges) and large footholds, usually a separate hold for each foot. Four to eight moves, about one move per hold, laid out like a ladder. Almost entirely static: you can stop and hang on straight arms anywhere. NICAS asks Level 1 walls for problems that rise in difficulty with "a change of angles" [F30].

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Precise foot placement: look at the foothold until the shoe is on it, and place the big-toe edge on the best part.
- Straight arms: hang off your skeleton and bend the arms only to pull for the next hold.
- Pushing with the legs: most of the upward movement comes from standing up on a foot, not from pulling.
- Downclimbing and falling: landing feet first with knees bent, then rolling back. NICAS Level 1 and the DAV Boulder-Check both teach safe bouldering and falling [F28, F32].

**Focus:** move volume and many problems per session. Lattice advises 6–12 months of climbing before hangboarding [F6]. Learn the gym's system: colours, start holds, top hold [F26, F27].

**Reading problems:**
- Find the start holds (often two hands marked) and the finish hold (matched, or held for 2–3 s by gym rule).
- Count the hand moves.
- Point out where each foot goes for each reach.

**Physical requirements:** no special strength needed. Bodyweight hardly matters at this grade (judgement).

**Sticking points and fixes:**
- Climbing on bent arms ("T-rex arms") pumps you quickly. Fix: pause on straight arms before each move.
- Scuffing the feet. Fix: silent-feet drill, placing each foot without noise.
- Fear of falling. Fix: practise jumping off from progressively higher (NICAS/DAV fall training [F28, F32]).

**Timeline:** VB–V0 usually goes in the first few sessions [F48 (anecdotal)].

**Tall climber note:** reach makes many ladder problems easier. Low-start problems feel cramped, so sit the hips back and keep the arms straight (judgement).

### 3.2 V1 (Font 5)

**What problems look like (judgement):** still mostly vertical to slightly overhanging, around 10°. Jugs mixed with incut edges, the first sidepulls, pinches and slopers on volumes. Footholds are smaller and sometimes off to the side, so some moves need you to shift your weight across before reaching. Five to eight moves, static.

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Weight shift: move the hips over the foot you will stand on before you reach [F49].
- Edging on the inside edge, and the outside edge for the first hip turns [F49].
- Smearing on the wall or a volume when no foothold sits where you need one.
- Holding pinches (thumb opposing the fingers) and sidepulls (pull sideways, lean the other way) [F49].

**Focus:** two-handed matching and foot swaps. Repeat each problem until it feels smooth (judgement).

**Reading problems:**
- Note which way each handhold faces: a hold that faces left wants your body to its right.
- Look for the footholds that put your hips under the next hold.

**Physical requirements:** none special.

**Sticking points:**
- Square-on climbing (chest to the wall, both feet on front points) on every move.
- Over-gripping.
- Fix: one session a week on easy problems climbed with "quiet hands", holding no harder than needed (judgement).

**Timeline:** first few weeks [F48 (anecdotal)].

---

### 3.3 V2 (Font 5+)

**What problems look like (judgement):** gentle overhangs of 10–25° appear, often the first problems on the steep section. Holds include sidepulls, underclings, slopers and first gastons. Moves where the body must turn sideways to the wall. Footholds no longer sit under every handhold, so you reach from one foot with the other foot on nothing useful. Five to ten moves. A small deadpoint may appear, but most V2s can still be done statically.

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Backstep and hip turn: outside edge of one foot, that hip turned into the wall, reaching with the same-side hand [F49].
- Flagging, to stop the body swinging out like a door when you release one hand [F49].
- Layback on aligned sidepulls [F49].
- Undercling with feet high.
- First heel hooks on big holds.

**Focus:** learn to tell "front-on" moves from "side-on" moves and switch between them. On an overhang, get the hips close to the wall on the moving side (judgement).

**Reading problems:**
- When two handholds face the same way (both sidepulls facing left), expect a layback or backstep.
- A lone foothold far out to the side usually means a flag or a backstep.
- An undercling at belly height means feet high and reach up from it.

**Physical requirements:** none special, though a Mountain Project user notes that some V2s can still be muscled up [F44].

**Sticking points:**
- Barn-dooring (swinging off when one hand lets go). Fix: flag the foot opposite the moving hand.
- Arms tire on the steep. Fix: straight arms and turned hips.
- River Rock runs its intermediate clinic for climbers "stuck at about V2", which shows this is a recognised plateau [F36].

**Timeline:** often within the first month [F48 (anecdotal)].

---

### 3.4 V3 (Font 6A/6A+)

**What problems look like (judgement):** two families appear.
- *Steep:* 20–40° overhangs, mid-size edges and slopers, footholds that need active pulling to stay on.
- *Slab/vertical:* smears, volumes, tiny edges for the feet, balance moves.

Expect the first problems with one clear "move": a deadpoint to a sloper, a big span, a heel hook. Four to ten moves.

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Deadpoint (catching the hold at the top of the body's movement) [F33, F35].
- Heel hook on a good hold, to pull the hips in and up.
- Drop knee at an introductory level.
- Gaston.
- Smearing on slab.
- Basic mantle onto a volume or ledge.
- The Mountain Project user puts "basic footwork and body positioning" as necessary from V3/6B [F44], and Touchstone's Intermediate Technique clinic starts at V3 with "footwork, using momentum to generate, hand positions" [F33].

**Focus:**
- Flash more V3s by reading the problem first.
- On steep ground, drive through the toes into the wall to keep the feet on. Hooper's Beta notes that the off foot should push *into* the wall, perpendicular to it, rather than down [F41].
- Start learning to generate: a small sink at the hips, then a drive through the legs, rather than pulling only with the arms [F33, F39].

**Reading problems:**
- Is the crux hold good enough to catch moving (a deadpoint), or must you arrive static (a lock-off)?
- Does the problem give a foothold at hip height on the side you need to move toward (heel hook)?
- On slab: where can you stand, and is there a smear patch?

**Physical requirements:** nothing measured specifically. Lattice's V1–V4 band averages +19.0 kg on a two-arm hang [F1], and many V3 climbers will be below that (judgement).

**Sticking points:**
- Feet cutting on steep ground.
- Only climbing your best style (all steep, or all slab). Lattice lists avoiding weak styles as a main cause of plateaus [F9].
- Fix: spend one session in four only on the style you avoid.

**Timeline:** 3–6 months to be confident at V3 [F48 (anecdotal)].

### 3.5 V4 (Font 6B/6B+)

**What problems look like (judgement unless cited):**
- Overhangs of 30–45°, or technical vertical walls with poor feet.
- More crimps and slopers, fewer jugs. Footholds are smaller or are volumes that need smearing.
- Each problem combines two or three techniques: a heel hook to set up a drop-knee reach, for example.
- One or two moves that need momentum, such as a deadpoint, a dyno to a big hold or a simple coordination move.
- Usually four to eight hard moves.
- Bouldering Project groups V4–V6 as "intermediate, with more powerful movements" [F27].

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Heel hook as an active pull: bend the knee and pull with the hamstring to bring the hips in.
- Toe hook to stop a swing.
- Drop knee on the steep.
- Deadpoint to slopers.
- Beginning of compression on volumes.
- Simple dynos.
- Castle Climbing's Louis Parkinson ran his "Beginners Dynamic Movement" workshop for V3–V5 climbers, which places dynamic moves squarely in this band [F37].

**Focus:**
- Body tension: keep the feet pressing in when the hands are far above or out to the side.
- Choosing static or dynamic: Hooper's Beta advises a straight-arm start with the hips sitting back and down for big moves on overhangs, instead of locking off. Newer climbers tend to lock off too much [F39].
- Repeat V4s until they feel smooth, and start trying V5s in every session (judgement, consistent with [F9]).

**Reading problems:**
- Find the crux first, then work backwards: which body position must you be in to start the crux move?
- Look for heel-hook-shaped holds (a horizontal edge or volume at hip height to the side) and toe-hook features (a hold or volume edge facing down or sideways at foot level).
- Spot the move where your feet will cut, then plan the foot catch.

**Physical requirements:**
- Lattice V1–V4 band: +19.0 kg on a two-arm hang [F1].
- Reconstructed one-arm 20 mm for men at V4: about 49% of bodyweight [F15].
- Open-hand testers in V1–V4 held +22.5 kg against +14.5 kg for half-crimp testers [F6], so strength at this level varies a lot.

**Sticking points and fixes:** see 3.6, which covers the V4→V5 transition in depth.

**Timeline:** first V4 around 6–12 months, reliable V4 later [F48 (anecdotal, internally inconsistent)]. Mountain Project users report V4 to V5 after about one year [F47].

### 3.6 V5 (Font 6C/6C+): the V4→V5 transition in depth

#### 3.6.1 What changes between V4 and V5 indoors

| Dimension | Typical V4 | Typical V5 | Grounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holds | edges and slopers with some positive part; jug rests | smaller crimps, poorer slopers, pinches; few rests | [F48] "smaller, crimpier holds"; [F46] holds shrink again at V6 |
| Wall angle | 30–45°, or vertical with poor feet | more of the grade sits on 40–50° walls | [F46] (forum: harder gym climbs are more overhung); judgement |
| Footholds | small edges, volumes | smaller edges or none; feet must be held on by tension; feet-cutting moves | [F35, F48]; judgement |
| What the crux demands | one of several possible solutions works | usually **one technique, done precisely**, such as a toe hook to stop the swing or a drop knee to gain 20 cm | [F34, F35] syllabus lists; judgement |
| Dynamic content | deadpoints, small dynos | committed deadpoints, dynos, coordination ("run and jump", double-clutch) moves | [F34, F37] |
| Body tension | helpful | required to keep feet on through hand moves | [F35, F48] |
| Combinations | 2–3 techniques per problem | 3–5, chained without rests | judgement |
| Tactics | usually flashed or done in a session | often takes several attempts or sessions; beta must be worked out move by move | [F37] (projecting workshop for V4+); [F45] |

#### 3.6.2 What coaches say holds people at V4

1. **Relying on strength instead of position.** Lattice's first plateau cause is over-emphasising strength early: climbers who can muscle through moves learn technique more slowly [F9]. A Mountain Project user told a V5 boulderer that "muscling through" would hold him back [F45]. Hooper's Beta suggests the self-coaching question "If I'm strong enough to do this, what am I missing" [F42].
2. **Locking off instead of moving from straight arms on the steep.** Newer climbers stay in tight, bent-arm positions. Start big overhang moves from straight arms with the hips back and down, driving into the foot rather than pulling the knee in [F39].
3. **Being "tight to the wall" when the move needs rotation.** Staying pinned in blocks rotation and makes you swing when a hand comes off. Instead, relax away from the lower hand to a balance point before moving [F40].
4. **Ignoring the off foot.** The lower foot controls rotation and swing. Push it into the wall, place it near 90° to your hand-to-foot line, and dab it to stop a swing [F41].
5. **Avoiding weak styles and climbs at the limit.** Lattice lists avoiding hard climbs and weak styles, using drills that don't vary enough, a negative mindset, ignoring how you feel, and not learning from mistakes [F9]. In a Mountain Project thread about V5→V6, most replies named overhangs and small holds as the climber's gap [F46].
6. **Missing specific techniques.** Two coached clinics for the "V5 plateau" teach the same set: heel and toe hooks, flagging, body tension, compression, deadpoints, sequencing [F35]. A V5–V6 clinic adds drop knees, mantles and rock-overs, and dynos [F34]. Touchstone summarises the "stuck around V5" gap as "dynamic movement, precision footwork, and efficient body positioning" [F33].
7. **Small repeated errors.** Louis Parkinson, on moving from the 6th to the 7th Font grade: "it's the details that make the difference", and climbers "unconsciously make certain mistakes over and over again" [F38].
8. **Training without structure, or too little time on steep, short limit problems.** Climbing.com advises keeping most bouldering on steeper walls with 4–8 move problems at your limit [F43]. This is training advice, so the book should use it only as "where to spend your climbing time".

#### 3.6.3 Technique or strength? What the data says

- **The strength gap between V4 and V5 is modest.** Lattice's averages rise from +19.0 kg (V1–V4) to +24.2 kg (V5–V7) on a two-arm hang [F1]. The reconstruction puts men at V4 around 49% and at V5 around 55% of bodyweight on one arm [F15]. Within-grade spread is larger than that difference [F6, F15].
- **Lattice's own diagnosis for climbers with under five years' experience** is training "with a focus on technique or application of strength, rather than just getting stronger" [F14].
- **Finger strength explains about half the variance in bouldering grade**, even in an advanced group [F16 **(unverified exact figure)**]. The other half is technique, tactics, power, body shape and mindset.
- **Climbing builds the strength.** Lattice suggests 6–12 months of climbing volume before hangboarding [F6]. For someone at about one year, structured finger training becomes a reasonable *addition*, but it is outside the scope of this book (judgement).
- **Body mass:** relative strength is load ÷ bodyweight (see 2.3), so a few kilograms change the ratio as much as months of finger training. The research, however, finds no simple link between body mass and level [F10, F18], and body fat appears to act through strength-to-weight [F17].

#### 3.6.4 What to focus on

1. **Pick three to five V5s on different styles and work them over several sessions.** Break each into single moves, then link them in pairs (judgement; consistent with [F9, F37, F45]).
2. **Use a structured self-check after every fall:** where were my hips, where was the off foot, were my arms straight before the move? [F39, F41, F42]
3. **Learn the "V5 plateau syllabus" on easier problems first.** Climb V3 and V4 problems specifically to practise toe hooks, drop knees, compression and deadpoints [F34, F35].
4. **Spend more time on the steepest wall** [F43, F46].
5. **Copy the beta of climbers your height.** For a tall climber, beta from much shorter climbers often won't transfer (judgement).

#### 3.6.5 Reading V5 problems

- **Find the crux by its holds:** the smallest hold, the longest gap, or the hold facing the "wrong" way.
- **Ask what the crux hold wants.** Pulled from below means static is possible. A sidepull or gaston means you need body position. A sloper means low hips and straight arms.
- **Look for hidden footholds** (volume edges, the side of a hold) and heel/toe-hook features before you start.
- **Identify the swing move**, where one hand releases and the feet are bad, and decide in advance: flag, toe hook, or cut and catch.
- **Read in chunks of two to four holds.** Lattice summarises research linking route preview in 2–4 hold sequences to better performance on hard routes [F11]. That research was on routes.

#### 3.6.6 Tall, heavier adult starters

Notes for tall, heavier adult starters are kept separately.

#### 3.6.7 Timeline

The large dataset suggests the average V5–V7 climber has climbed for years [F2]. One coached reference puts the common plateau between V3 and V6 in the first couple of years [F9]. Forum reports range from V5 within one year to much longer [F47, F48]. Expect V5 to take months more, not weeks (judgement).

### 3.7 V6 (Font 7A)

**What problems look like (judgement unless cited):**
- Steep walls of 40–50° and roofs, or very technical vertical faces.
- Small crimps, poor slopers, wide pinches, compression between holds or volumes.
- One or two big dynamic moves, or long sequences of medium-hard moves.
- A Mountain Project user found V6s "too overhung and crimpy or too shouldery", and the replies agreed that holds get smaller and walls steeper [F46].
- This is the Font 6→7 step that Louis Parkinson addresses [F38].

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Everything in the V5 syllabus, done under load.
- Toe hooks and heel hooks used as main holds.
- Heel-toe cams.
- Compression with both arms and feet.
- Controlled cut-loose and re-placing the feet.
- Dynos that need precise direction.
- Touchstone's V5–V6 clinic covers drop knees, heel/toe hooks, mantles, dynamic movement and dynos [F34].
- Louis Parkinson's intermediate dynamic workshop is pitched at V6+ [F37].

**Focus:**
- Weak styles: overhangs and small holds, per the forum [F46].
- Limit projecting across sessions.
- Precision in the details [F38].

**Reading problems:**
- Identify where you'll need tension (feet that are low and far from the hands), where you need a hook, and which hold is the "catch" target of a dynamic move.
- Look at hold texture and the friction direction on slopers.

**Physical requirements:**
- Reconstructed one-arm 20 mm for men: about 61% of bodyweight [F15].
- Lattice V5–V7 band: +24.2 kg two-arm [F1].
- Finger strength becomes a more common limiter (judgement, consistent with [F46], where climbers add hangboard work for V6).

**Sticking points:**
- Climbing only V5s that suit you (judgement).
- A V5 climber who was told he was "muscling through" later reported reaching V6 [F45] (anecdote).
- Fix: rotate gyms and setters, since setters have blind spots [F46].

**Timeline:** sometimes 1–3 years [F48 (anecdotal)]. 99Boulders: advanced (V6–V8), "most likely a few years" [F24].

### 3.8 V7 (Font 7A+)

**What problems look like (judgement):**
- Same terrain as V6, with less margin: holds a size smaller, feet worse, moves longer or more powerful.
- Board-style problems (Moon, Kilter, Tension) become common reference points.

**Techniques that become necessary:**
- Sustained compression.
- Precise toe and heel tension on poor features.
- Highly specific body positions on poor holds.
- Fast, accurate dynamic catches ("contact strength" [F14]).

**Focus:**
- Specific weaknesses identified through testing [F14].
- Projecting over weeks.

**Reading problems:**
- Micro-beta: which way to rotate a hand on a hold, exactly where a toe goes.
- Testing several foot options for one hand move (judgement).

**Physical requirements:**
- Reconstructed one-arm 20 mm for men: about 67% of bodyweight [F15].
- Men at V7–V8 average about 145% bodyweight on a weighted pull-up two-rep max [F5].

**Sticking points:** finger strength, power, and moves that need a hand placed fast. Lattice says a climber who is strong on the hangboard but weak on boulders may lack "contact strength" to catch dynos and deadpoints [F14].

**Timeline:** about 5 years at three sessions a week [F48 (opinion)]. The Lattice V5–V7 band averages 8.6 years climbing [F2].

---

### 3.9 V8 and above (Font 7B+), briefly

- **Data:**
  - Lattice V8–V11 band: +38.4 kg two-arm added load [F1], 9.7 years climbing and 4.6 years training [F2], 4.4 h a week of structured training [F3].
  - Reconstructed one-arm 20 mm for men: V8 73%, V9 79%, V10 85% of bodyweight [F15].
  - Pull-up strength stops paying off above about 160–165% bodyweight (men) [F5].
- **Age:** at V8, under-25s average 3.8 years climbing, over-40s 13.0 [F2]. A 51-year-old sent V8 after a structured four-month plan [F13] (anecdote).
- **What changes (judgement):** specialist strength (fingers on specific grips, pinch, compression), refined tactics, and individual beta, with body shape driving solutions. Bouldering Project calls V7–V8 "advanced movements" [F27]. Bouldering Boss says most recreational climbers never reach V8 [F48] (opinion).
- For this book, V8+ is context only: the reader's next two to three years lie at V5–V7.

- **Search budget ran out** partway through the research, so these were not searched: Mountain Training syllabi, USA Climbing coaching curricula, Climbing Escalade Canada LTAD, a NICAS logbook, the "Movement for Climbers" brand, coaches' "skills for V5" articles on Climbing.com and UKC, and the technique books named in the brief. I could not verify the books for grade claims and did not cite them.
- **NICAS** publishes level descriptions but no grades and no technique checklists. The logbook seems to be available only through centres [F28, F30].
- **Pages I could not open:** Lattice's "predictors by ability level" post (901 climbers) [F8] and its sport-climber regression [F51] returned 404. The full text of Giles 2021 [F16] and of the IRCRA position statement [F19] was blocked. The figures from those come from search summaries.
- **The Lattice data** is self-reported, cross-sectional and drawn from Lattice users. Forum critics suspect it over-represents good trainers [F15].
- **The strength-by-grade reconstruction** [F15] is male-only and extrapolated.
- **Low-quality sources:** Bouldering Boss [F48] and forum threads [F44–F47, F49] are anecdotal. I used them only where nothing better was found, and labelled them.
- **Height:** I found no quantitative source on height against boulder grade.
